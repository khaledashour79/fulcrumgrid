How Do You Get Paid on Time by Government and Public-Sector Customers?

Public-sector customers rarely default but often pay slowly. Learn what to set up before invoicing, how prompt payment rules work and how to follow up politely.

November 24, 2026 · 6 min read

Government agencies, municipalities, schools and hospitals are some of the most reliable customers a business can have. They rarely go bust and they almost always pay in the end. The problem is the word “end”. Public-sector invoices often move slowly through processes that were built for accountability rather than speed. This guide explains why that happens and what you can do to get paid on time without straining the relationship.

Why do public-sector customers pay slowly?

Late payment from a public body is rarely about cash. It is usually about process. The most common causes are:

  • Approval chains. An invoice may need sign-off from the person who received the goods, a budget holder and a finance officer before it can be paid.
  • Purchase orders. Most public bodies can only pay against a valid purchase order. An invoice without the right PO number is often rejected or simply parked.
  • Budget and fiscal year cycles. Spending is tied to approved budgets. Near the end of a fiscal year, or when a budget has not yet been approved, payments can stall.
  • Vendor registration. If you are not fully set up as a supplier in the agency’s system, with verified bank details and tax information, your invoice cannot be released.

What should you set up before you send the first invoice?

Most delays are decided before the invoice is even issued. Get these basics right at contract stage:

  • Complete vendor registration. Register in every system the customer uses, whether that is a national supplier register or the agency’s own portal, and confirm that your bank details have been verified.
  • Get a purchase order number. Ask for the PO before you start work, and check that its value and description match what you will bill.
  • Know your contacts. Record the contracting officer or buyer who owns the contract, the person who will confirm delivery and the accounts payable team that releases payment. These are often three different people.
  • Learn the required invoice format. Many public bodies only accept invoices through a specific portal or as structured e-invoices. In the EU, public authorities must be able to receive e-invoices that follow the European standard, and some countries make them mandatory for suppliers.
  • Confirm the payment terms in writing. Make sure the contract states the terms and when the clock starts, for example on receipt of a correct invoice.

How do you invoice a government customer correctly?

Treat every public-sector invoice as a document that will be checked line by line. Quote the PO number and contract reference, use the exact legal name and address of the buying entity, match descriptions and quantities to the order, and attach any required proof of delivery or timesheets. Submit it through the official channel only. An invoice emailed to a project manager may never reach accounts payable, and in many systems the payment clock does not start until a correct invoice arrives through the right route.

Do prompt payment rules apply to public bodies?

In many jurisdictions, yes. Public authorities are often held to stricter payment deadlines than private companies. Two well-known examples:

  • United States. The federal Prompt Payment Act generally requires federal agencies to pay within 30 days of receiving a proper invoice, and late payments can trigger interest penalties. Many states have similar rules for state and local bodies.
  • European Union. The Late Payment Directive sets a standard 30-day payment period for public authorities in business transactions, extendable to 60 days only in limited cases such as public healthcare, with statutory interest and a fixed recovery fee if they pay late.

These rules vary by country, contract and type of body, and this article is not legal advice. Check what applies to your contracts. In practice, knowing the rules matters most as a reference point: a polite note that an invoice has passed the statutory payment period usually moves it up the queue.

How should you follow up and escalate?

Follow up early and keep it factual. A good escalation path looks like this:

  • Before the due date. Confirm that the invoice has been received, matched to the PO and approved. This step alone catches most problems.
  • Shortly after the due date. Contact accounts payable with the invoice number, PO number and amount, and ask for the expected payment date.
  • If payment is still stuck. Ask the contracting officer or budget holder to help, since they can often unblock an approval that finance cannot.
  • As a last step. Use the formal channels in the contract, such as a supplier helpdesk, a payment complaints process or a claim for statutory interest.

Keep a record of every submission, reference number and conversation. Public bodies respond well to clear documentation, and it makes any formal claim much easier.

How do you track public-sector receivables?

Tag public-sector customers separately in your receivables ledger so you can see their aging on its own. Track the status of each invoice, not just its age: submitted, accepted, approved or paid. Watch for patterns around fiscal year end and plan your cash flow around them. If a particular agency always takes 45 days, it is better to forecast that than to be surprised every quarter.

When should you not push?

Pressure works badly when the delay is outside the customer’s control, for example during a budget freeze, a government shutdown or a system migration. In those cases, stay in touch, confirm the invoice is valid and queued, and agree on a realistic date. Public-sector relationships often last for years, so a patient and well-documented approach usually pays off far more than an aggressive one.

Frequently asked questions

Why was my invoice to a government agency rejected?

The most common reasons are a missing or wrong purchase order number, an incorrect legal entity name, amounts that do not match the order or an invoice sent outside the required portal.

When does the payment clock start for a public-sector invoice?

Usually when the customer receives a correct invoice or confirms delivery, whichever is later. Check your contract, because the exact rule depends on the jurisdiction and the agreement.

Can I charge interest if a public body pays late?

In many jurisdictions, yes. Rules such as the US Prompt Payment Act and the EU Late Payment Directive provide for interest on late payments, but the details vary, so check what applies to you.

Should I stop work if a government customer pays late?

Only if the contract allows it and only after following the agreed escalation steps. Stopping work without that basis can put you in breach, so get advice first.
