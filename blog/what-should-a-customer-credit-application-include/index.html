What Should a Customer Credit Application Include?

A good credit application captures who the customer really is, what credit they want and the terms they accept. Here are the sections every small supplier needs.

November 15, 2026 · 6 min read

A new business customer wants to buy on 30-day terms. You could take their word for it and ship, or you could ask them to complete a short credit application first. For a small supplier, that form is often the only structured information you will ever collect about a customer before you hand over goods or services on trust. This guide covers what to include, how to review it and the mistakes to avoid.

Why use a credit application at all?

A credit application does three jobs at once. It tells you exactly who you are dealing with, it gives you enough information to decide whether to extend credit and how much, and it records the terms the customer has agreed to before any invoice is issued. That last point matters most when things go wrong. If a customer pays late or disputes a charge, a signed application with clear terms is far stronger than a handshake or a string of emails.

What sections should a credit application have?

Keep the form to two or three pages. Most small B2B suppliers need these sections.

1. Legal entity details

  • Full registered legal name and any trading names, so you invoice and, if necessary, pursue the right entity.
  • Company registration number and tax or VAT number.
  • Registered address and trading address, if different.
  • Type of entity, such as sole trader, partnership or limited company, plus the date it was established.

2. Ownership and contacts

  • Names of owners, directors or partners.
  • A named accounts payable contact with email and phone, and where invoices should be sent.
  • Any purchase order or invoice requirements the customer has.

3. Trade and bank references

Ask for two or three trade references from suppliers who already give the customer credit, plus bank details. A quick call or email to a reference asking how long they have traded, the limit they allow and whether payments arrive on time often tells you more than any report.

4. Requested credit limit and terms

Ask what limit and payment terms the customer is requesting and their expected monthly spend. Make clear that you decide the final limit and terms, and that you may review them at any time. How you actually set that limit is a separate decision, but the request gives you a useful starting point.

5. Signed terms and conditions

This is the heart of the document. At minimum, the terms should state:

  • Payment terms, such as net 30 from invoice date.
  • Late payment interest or fees, and how they are calculated, within whatever limits your local law allows.
  • A collection-cost clause making the customer responsible for reasonable costs of recovering overdue amounts.
  • Your right to suspend supply or change terms if accounts fall overdue.
  • Governing law and jurisdiction, so you know which courts and rules apply to a dispute.
  • How disputes must be raised, for example in writing within a set number of days of the invoice.

6. Personal guarantee option

For newer companies or larger limits, include an optional personal guarantee section signed by an owner or director. Keep it separate and clearly labelled so the signer understands what they are agreeing to.

7. Consent to credit checks

Include a statement authorising you to obtain credit reports and contact the listed references. In some countries this consent is required before you can run checks, particularly on sole traders.

8. Signature and date

The application should be signed by someone with authority to bind the business, with their name, job title and the date. Electronic signatures are widely accepted for commercial agreements and make the process faster for everyone.

How should you review a credit application?

  • Check it is complete. Missing registration numbers or a blank references section are warning signs in themselves.
  • Verify the entity against the official company register to confirm the name, number and status match.
  • Contact at least two references and note what they say.
  • Run a credit check if the requested limit justifies the cost.
  • Record your decision, the limit and terms granted, and who approved them.

When should you update a credit application?

An application is a snapshot. Ask for a fresh one or updated details when a customer requests a significantly higher limit, changes ownership or legal structure, starts paying late, or simply every two to three years for active accounts.

What are the most common mistakes?

  • Accepting a trading name only, which makes it hard to know who is legally responsible for the debt.
  • Unsigned or undated terms, which weaken your position if you need to rely on them.
  • Terms that conflict with those on your invoices or website, leaving room for argument.
  • Collecting the form and never using it, so the agreed late fees and terms are never applied.
  • Copying a template from another country without checking that its interest and fee clauses are enforceable where you trade.

This article is general information, not legal advice. Rules on late-payment interest, collection costs and guarantees differ between countries, so have your application and terms reviewed by a local lawyer before you rely on them.

Frequently asked questions

Is a credit application legally binding?

The signed terms and conditions within it usually form part of your contract with the customer. How enforceable each clause is depends on local law, so have a lawyer review it.

Do I need a credit application for every customer?

It is best practice for any customer buying on credit. Customers who pay upfront or by card at the time of order generally do not need one.

Can customers sign a credit application electronically?

In most countries electronic signatures are accepted for business agreements. Keep a record of who signed, when, and the version of the terms they accepted.

How often should I update a customer's credit application?

Update it when the customer asks for a higher limit, changes ownership or structure, or starts paying late, and otherwise every two to three years for active accounts.
