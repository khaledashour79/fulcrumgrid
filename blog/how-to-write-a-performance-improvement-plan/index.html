How Do You Write a Performance Improvement Plan (PIP)?

A practical guide to writing a performance improvement plan: purpose, when to use one, key components, SMART goals, check-ins, documentation, outcomes, and common mistakes.

October 1, 2026 · 5 min read

A performance improvement plan (PIP) is a structured, time-bound document that sets out where an employee’s performance falls short of expectations, what good looks like, and what support the employer will provide to close the gap. Done well, it is a genuine chance to recover — not a paper trail for a decision that has already been made.

What is the purpose of a PIP?

The aim is clarity and a fair opportunity to improve. A PIP turns vague dissatisfaction (“your work isn’t good enough”) into specific, observable expectations, and it commits the manager to helping. It also protects both sides: the employee knows exactly what is required, and the organisation can show it acted fairly and consistently.

When should you use one?

A PIP suits sustained, specific performance gaps that informal coaching has not resolved. It is usually the wrong tool when:

  • The issue is a one-off dip or a short-term personal difficulty — a supportive conversation is a better first step.
  • The problem is misconduct — that belongs in a disciplinary process, not a PIP.
  • The cause is on the employer’s side — unclear expectations, a poor role fit, or missing tools and training should be fixed first.
  • The outcome is already decided — a PIP used as a formality is not credible and creates legal and cultural risk.

Requirements on process, notice, and documentation differ by country and contract, so check local employment law and your own policy before you start.

The core components

  • Basic details: employee, role, manager, start date, and plan length.
  • The performance gap: specific examples with dates and evidence, not impressions.
  • Expectations: the standard required, tied to the job description and to what peers are held to.
  • Goals and measures: what will be assessed and how.
  • Support: the training, coaching, tools, or time the employer commits to provide.
  • Check-in schedule: when you will meet and review progress.
  • Possible outcomes: what happens if the goals are met, and if they are not.
  • Acknowledgement: signatures confirming the employee received the plan (not necessarily that they agree with it).

Writing SMART goals

Good PIP goals are Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, and Time-bound. Compare these two:

  • Vague: “Improve customer service.”
  • SMART: “Respond to at least 90% of customer tickets within four working hours, and reach a satisfaction score of 4.2 or higher, by the end of week six.”

Keep the list short — three to five goals is plenty — and make sure each one is within the employee’s control and consistent with what you expect of others in the same role.

How long should a PIP run, and how often should you check in?

Most plans run 30 to 90 days, depending on the complexity of the role. The period must be long enough for a real pattern to show; a window that is too short is unfair. Hold check-ins weekly or every two weeks, and add a formal mid-point review. In each one, go through progress against every goal, give specific feedback, adjust the support if needed, and write down what was said.

Documentation

Keep dated records of everything: the evidence of the original gap, the signed plan, meeting notes, feedback given, support actually delivered, and the employee’s own comments. Give the employee copies, and store the file confidentially with access limited to those who need it. Good records protect the employee as much as the employer.

Possible outcomes

  • Success: performance meets the standard. Close the plan in writing and keep supporting the employee afterwards.
  • Partial progress: consider a single extension with revised goals and a stated reason.
  • Redeployment: a different role or team may suit the employee’s strengths better.
  • Insufficient improvement: the next steps follow your policy and local law and may include ending employment. Take HR or legal advice before you act.

Common mistakes

  • Setting vague goals or targets nobody could realistically meet.
  • Promising support and then not providing it.
  • Skipping check-ins or failing to record them.
  • Treating the PIP as a box-ticking step before dismissal.
  • Applying a different standard to different people.
  • Overlooking health, disability, or other protected circumstances that may explain the performance issue — ask for advice if in doubt.

Frequently asked questions

What is a performance improvement plan?

A PIP is a structured, time-bound plan that sets out an employee’s performance gap, the standard expected, measurable goals, the support the employer will provide, and what happens at the end. Its purpose is to give a fair chance to improve.

How long should a PIP last?

Most run 30 to 90 days depending on the role. It needs to be long enough to show a real pattern of improvement, with check-ins every week or two and a formal mid-point review.

What makes a good PIP goal?

It is specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound, within the employee’s control, and consistent with what is expected of peers. Replace “improve quality” with a clear number, standard, and deadline.

Does a PIP always end in dismissal?

No. A well-run PIP often ends with the employee back on track. Other outcomes include an extension, redeployment, or, if there is not enough improvement, further steps under your policy and local law, which can include termination.

Is a PIP the same as a disciplinary process?

No. A PIP addresses performance and offers support to improve. Misconduct is handled through a separate disciplinary process, and the two should not be confused.
