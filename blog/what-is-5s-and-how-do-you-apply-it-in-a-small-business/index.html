What Is 5S and How Do You Apply It in a Small Business?

Learn what 5S means, how Sort, Set in order, Shine, Standardize and Sustain work in a small business, and how to run a first 5S day that actually sticks.

November 22, 2026 · 6 min read

A cluttered workbench, a stockroom where nobody can find the spare fuses, a shared drive with seven versions of the same price list. None of these looks like a big problem on its own, but together they cost minutes every day. 5S is a simple, low-cost method for fixing that. This Q&A explains where it comes from, what the five steps mean in different kinds of workplaces, and how to run your first 5S day without it turning into a one-off spring clean.

Where does 5S come from?

5S comes from Japanese manufacturing and is closely associated with the Toyota Production System and the wider lean movement. The name refers to five Japanese words that all begin with an S sound: seiri, seiton, seiso, seiketsu and shitsuke. The usual English versions keep the S: Sort, Set in order, Shine, Standardize and Sustain. The idea behind them is simple. A workplace where everything has a place and problems are visible is safer, faster and easier to improve.

What are the five steps?

  • Sort. Remove what is not needed. Keep only the tools, stock, files and information used for the work done in that area.
  • Set in order. Give everything that remains a fixed, labelled place close to where it is used, with the most-used items easiest to reach.
  • Shine. Clean the area and the equipment, and treat cleaning as an inspection: leaks, damage and missing parts show up on clean surfaces.
  • Standardize. Write down what good looks like, with photos, labels and short checklists, so everyone keeps the area the same way.
  • Sustain. Turn it into a habit through regular checks, clear owners and visible results.

What does 5S look like in different workplaces?

Workshop

Sort out broken tools and offcuts nobody will use. Paint shadow outlines on a tool board so a missing spanner is obvious at a glance. Wipe down machines at the end of each shift and note any oil leaks or worn guards.

Warehouse

Clear obsolete stock and empty pallets from the aisles. Mark floor zones for goods in, picking and despatch, label every bin location, and keep fast-moving items at waist height near the packing bench.

Office

Clear desks and shared cupboards, return borrowed equipment, and create one labelled place for stationery, printer supplies and incoming post.

Digital workspace

Shared drives and task boards fill up just like shelves. Sort by archiving or deleting outdated files and duplicate versions. Set in order with an agreed folder structure and a naming convention, such as date, client and document type. Shine by closing finished tasks, removing stale cards and fixing broken links. Standardize with a one-page guide to where things go, and sustain it with a short monthly tidy-up.

How do you run a first 5S event in a day?

Pick one small area, such as a single workbench, one aisle or one team's shared drive. A focused day works far better than trying to tackle the whole business at once.

  • First hour. Explain the five steps, agree the boundaries of the area and take before photos.
  • Morning. Sort. Go through every item and red-tag anything that is not clearly needed.
  • Midday. Set in order. Decide a home for what stays, label shelves, mark the floor and arrange items by how often they are used.
  • Afternoon. Shine. Clean thoroughly and log any defects you find.
  • Last hour. Standardize. Take after photos, write a one-page standard, name an owner and book the first weekly check.

What is the red-tag approach?

A red tag is a simple label attached to any item whose purpose is unclear. Note what the item is, who tagged it, the date and why. Move tagged items to a holding area for a set period, often two to four weeks. If nobody claims an item by then, decide whether to sell, donate, recycle, store or discard it. Red-tagging avoids arguments on the day: nobody has to decide instantly, but nothing stays just because it has always been there. For digital files, the equivalent is an archive folder with a clear deletion date.

How do you score a 5S audit?

Keep the scorecard short enough to complete in ten minutes. Score each step from 0 to 4, giving a maximum of 20.

  • Sort. No unneeded items in the area.
  • Set in order. Every item has a labelled home and is in it.
  • Shine. The area and equipment are clean and defects are logged.
  • Standardize. The standard is posted and up to date.
  • Sustain. Recent checks were done on time and their actions closed.

Post the score in the area and track it over time. The trend matters more than any single number.

What are the most common mistakes?

  • Treating it as a one-off clean-up. Without Standardize and Sustain, the area drifts back within weeks.
  • No owner. If everyone is responsible, nobody is. Every area needs one named person.
  • Starting too big. Tackling the whole site at once exhausts people before results show.
  • Using audits to blame. Scores should reveal what makes the standard hard to keep, not criticise individuals.

How do you sustain 5S with weekly checks?

Schedule a fifteen-minute check for each area every week, run by its owner with the same scorecard. Record the score, take a photo if something has slipped and agree one or two small actions. Once a month, swap auditors so fresh eyes spot what regulars stop noticing, and review the scores together as a team.

The short version

5S means sorting, organising, cleaning, standardising and sustaining a workplace, physical or digital. Start with one area in one day, use red tags to clear clutter fairly, score it simply, give it an owner and check it every week.

Frequently asked questions

Does 5S only work in manufacturing?

No. It started in Japanese factories, but the same five steps work in warehouses, workshops, offices, vans and digital spaces such as shared drives and task boards.

How long does a first 5S event take?

A focused day is enough for one small area, such as a workbench, an aisle or a team's shared drive. Start small and expand once the first area holds its standard.

How often should we run a 5S audit?

A short weekly check by the area owner works well, with a monthly review of the scores and an occasional audit by someone from outside the area.

What is the difference between 5S and a big clean-up?

A clean-up is a one-off event. 5S adds labelled homes, a written standard, a named owner and regular audits, so the area stays organised afterwards.
