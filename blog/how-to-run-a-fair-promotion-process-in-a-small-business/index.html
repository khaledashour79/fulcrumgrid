How Do You Run a Fair Promotion Process in a Small Business?

A fair promotion process rewards readiness, not tenure or favoritism. Learn how to set criteria, calibrate managers, handle pay and document decisions.

October 21, 2026 · 6 min read

Promotions are among the most visible decisions a small business makes. Everyone watches who moves up, and why. Without a clear process, the decision defaults to whoever is loudest, longest-serving or closest to the owner. This Q&A shows how to build a simple, fair process without a big HR department.

Why does a promotion process matter?

A promotion tells the whole team what the company really rewards. If the reasons are vague, good people assume the system is rigged and leave, while the wrong people get stretched into roles they are not ready for. A written process also protects you: it shows that decisions were based on job-related facts, which matters if someone challenges the outcome.

How do you define criteria for each level?

Describe what the work looks like at each level in plain language, before anyone is being considered. For every role, write three to five expectations covering:

  • Scope. The size and complexity of the work the person handles alone.
  • Impact. The results they deliver for customers, the team or the business.
  • Judgment. How well they decide without being told what to do.
  • Influence. Whether they help others, train newcomers or lead work.

Keep each expectation observable. 'Handles supplier disputes without escalation' can be checked; 'shows leadership' cannot.

Is it about readiness or tenure?

Readiness. Time in the job matters only because it usually gives people a chance to build evidence. Someone with five years who still needs close supervision is not ready for a senior role, and someone with eighteen months who already performs at the next level may be. Ask one question: is this person already doing most of the next-level job, consistently? If yes, promote. If you are only hoping they will grow into it, offer a stretch project first.

How do you calibrate across managers?

Different managers rate differently: one is generous, another strict. Before decisions are final, bring the managers together and review every proposed promotion against the same written criteria. Ask each manager to bring two or three concrete examples. Compare candidates at the same level side by side, and challenge ratings that rest on impressions. In a very small team, ask a second person, such as the owner or a peer manager, to review each case.

How do you tell people who were not promoted?

  • Tell them first. Do not let them learn it from an announcement.
  • Be specific. Name the criteria and the gaps, with examples.
  • Offer a path. Agree two or three actions and a date to review progress.
  • Be honest about timing. Never promise a promotion you cannot guarantee.

A clear, respectful explanation often does more for retention than the promotion itself would have.

How should the pay adjustment work?

Decide pay as part of the process, not after it. Set a salary range for each level so the new figure is not negotiated from scratch. A promotion with no pay change feels like extra work for free, so aim to move the person into the new range, even if in two steps. Check the result against peers at the same level to avoid creating unexplained gaps, which are a common source of pay-equity problems.

What should you document for fairness and legal safety?

  • The written criteria for the level.
  • The evidence for each candidate, such as results, feedback and examples.
  • Who was considered and how, including those not selected.
  • The reasons for the final decision and who made it.
  • The pay decision and its rationale.

Review the pattern once a year: are promotions going disproportionately to one gender, age group or team without a job-related reason? Rules on discrimination vary by country, so check your local requirements, but consistent criteria and records are good practice everywhere. Keep these records confidential and only for as long as the law allows.

What is a simple step list?

  • Step 1. Write level criteria and pay ranges.
  • Step 2. Set review dates, for example twice a year, and tell everyone.
  • Step 3. Managers nominate candidates with evidence.
  • Step 4. Hold a calibration meeting.
  • Step 5. Decide the outcome and the pay adjustment.
  • Step 6. Tell unsuccessful candidates first, then announce.
  • Step 7. File the records and review patterns yearly.

Frequently asked questions

How often should a small business review promotions?

Twice a year works well for most small teams. Fixed review dates make the process predictable and stop promotions from being decided ad hoc whenever someone asks.

Should we promote someone just to keep them from leaving?

Rarely. A promotion for retention alone sets an unfair precedent and can put someone in a role they are not ready for. Address the real reason, such as pay or growth, through a fair process.

What if there is no higher role available?

Be honest about it. You can still recognize growth through a bigger scope, a lead responsibility or a pay adjustment within the current range, without using a title you cannot support.

Do we need to publish salary ranges?

Not necessarily, but you should define them internally. Some countries require pay transparency, so check local rules. Sharing the range for each level builds trust.
