How Do You Keep in Touch With an Employee on Parental Leave?

How small-business managers can stay in touch with an employee on parental leave: agree a contact plan, share the right news and plan a smooth return.

November 27, 2026 · 6 min read

When someone on your team goes on parental leave, a quiet question follows: how much should you contact them? Too little and they come back feeling like a stranger in their own job. Too much and you intrude on one of the most demanding periods of their life. In a small business, where you may be manager, HR and payroll in one, getting this balance right is a real retention lever. Here is a practical way to do it.

Please note: parental leave rules vary widely by country and region, including what contact or work is allowed during leave. This article is general guidance, not legal advice, so check the rules where you operate.

Why does contact during parental leave matter?

People on long leave often worry about being forgotten: that a reorganization will happen without them, that a colleague will quietly take over their role, or that they will miss a promotion. Light, well-judged contact answers those worries. It also makes the return far smoother, because the employee already knows about new clients, tools or team members before their first morning back. Employees who feel valued during leave are more likely to come back, and to stay.

Why can too much contact backfire?

Parental leave is not remote work. Frequent messages, questions about projects or a stream of copied emails can feel like pressure to work unpaid, and can erode trust quickly. In some countries, excessive contact or asking someone to work during protected leave can also create legal risk. The goal is to keep the door open, not to keep the employee on call.

Agree a contact plan before leave starts

The best time to decide is before the leave begins, during a relaxed handover meeting. Ask rather than assume, and write down what you agree. Cover:

  • Frequency. Every few weeks, once a month, or only for major news.
  • Channel. Personal email, phone, a messaging app, or the work account if they prefer to keep it active.
  • Topics. What they want to hear about, and what they would rather not.
  • Contact person. One named person, usually their manager, so messages do not come from all directions.
  • Review. A promise that they can change the plan at any time, including asking for no contact at all.

What should you share, and what should you not?

Share news that affects their job, their career or their sense of belonging:

  • Organizational changes, new managers or restructuring.
  • Any proposed change to their own role, which they should hear about first, not last.
  • Promotion rounds, pay-review cycles and bonus timelines.
  • Job openings they might want to apply for.
  • Team social events, celebrations and training days, as an invitation with no obligation.

Do not send day-to-day work requests, client questions or "quick favors". If something truly cannot be solved without them, ask once, make it clearly optional and thank them, then improve the handover so it does not happen again.

What about keeping in touch days?

Some countries have formal rules for occasional work during leave. In the UK, for example, an employee on maternity or adoption leave can agree to work up to ten "keeping in touch" days without ending their leave, with separate days available for shared parental leave. Other countries have different arrangements or none at all, and pay, limits and eligibility vary. Where such days exist, they are voluntary for both sides and should be agreed, paid and recorded properly. Check your local rules before offering them.

Do not disadvantage people on leave

Being away should not cost someone their career progress. Include employees on leave in pay reviews and apply any general raise to them. Handle bonuses fairly according to your policy and local law. Tell them about promotion opportunities and let them apply. Keep their annual review on file, based on the time they actually worked. In many places, treating someone worse because of pregnancy or parental leave is unlawful discrimination, and it is a fast way to lose good people.

Plan the return

A few weeks before the return date, arrange a short call to confirm the date and discuss what they need. Consider:

  • A phased return. Shorter days or fewer days at first, sometimes by using remaining annual leave.
  • Flexible working requests. Many returning parents ask for changed hours or remote days. Respond promptly and fairly, following any legal process that applies.
  • A re-onboarding checklist. Updated access and passwords, an introduction to new colleagues, a summary of changed processes, priorities for the first month and a check-in after two weeks.
  • Practical needs. For example, a private space and time for breastfeeding or expressing milk.

A sample check-in message

Keep it short, warm and free of obligation:

"Hi Sam, I hope you and the baby are doing well. A quick update as agreed: we have hired a new account manager, Lena, who started this month, and the pay-review round opens in March. You are included as usual. The team dinner is on the 14th if you fancy joining, no pressure at all. No need to reply. Take care, Alex."

Frequently asked questions

How often should I contact an employee on parental leave?

There is no single right answer. Agree a frequency with the employee before leave starts, often every few weeks or monthly, and let them change it at any time, including asking for no contact.

Can I ask an employee on parental leave to do some work?

Avoid routine work requests. Some countries allow a limited number of voluntary, paid keeping in touch days by agreement, but rules vary, so check your local law before offering any work.

Should employees on parental leave be included in pay reviews?

Yes. Include them in pay reviews and general raises, and tell them about promotion opportunities. Treating someone less favorably because of parental leave is unlawful in many countries.

What should a return-to-work plan include?

Confirm the return date, discuss a phased return and any flexible working request, update their access, introduce new colleagues, summarize what changed and schedule a check-in after the first few weeks.
