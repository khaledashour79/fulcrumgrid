How Do You Run a Skip-Level Meeting That Works?

A skip-level meeting lets you hear from employees two levels below you. Learn its purpose, how often to hold it, what to ask and how to avoid undermining managers.

November 6, 2026 · 6 min read

A skip-level meeting is a one-on-one or small-group conversation between a leader and employees who report to one of their own managers. It is one of the simplest ways to learn what is really happening in your organization, because information is often softened or lost as it travels upward. Done well, it builds trust in both directions. Done badly, it makes managers feel watched and employees feel exposed. This guide covers what people ask most, from purpose to follow-up.

What is the purpose of a skip-level meeting?

The purpose is to listen, not to inspect. You want an unfiltered view of how work, priorities and culture look from the front line, plus a chance to show employees that senior leaders care about them as people. It also helps you spot problems early, find talent your managers may not have noticed and test whether your strategy is understood two levels down.

It is not a performance review of the manager, a way to collect complaints behind their back or a replacement for the regular one-on-one between employee and manager.

How often should you hold them?

Cadence depends on the size of your organization, but a steady rhythm matters more than a perfect one.

  • Small teams: every employee once or twice a year, 30 minutes each.
  • Larger organizations: a rotation so that every team is covered each quarter, either one-on-one or in groups of four to six.
  • New leaders: a round of skip-levels in the first 90 days to learn the ground truth.

Put them on the calendar in advance and make them routine. A meeting that appears only when something goes wrong sends the wrong signal.

What questions should you ask?

Open questions work best. Choose a few and let the conversation breathe instead of reading a script.

Work and priorities

  • What are you working on, and how does it connect to our goals?
  • What slows you down most each week?
  • What would you stop doing if you could?

Support and growth

  • What help would make the biggest difference to you right now?
  • What do you want to learn or do in the next year?
  • When did you last get feedback that was useful?

Team and culture

  • What is working well that we should protect?
  • What is one thing you would change if you were in my seat?
  • Is there anything you hesitate to raise with your manager that I should know about?

Company direction

  • How would you explain our priorities to a friend?
  • What decisions have felt confusing or poorly explained?

How do you avoid undermining managers?

Managers are right to worry that a skip-level will bypass them. Remove that fear with a few clear habits.

  • Tell managers first. Explain the purpose, the schedule and how you will handle what you hear before the first meeting.
  • Do not make decisions on the spot. Never reverse a manager’s decision, promise a raise or change priorities in the room. Say you will look into it.
  • Send issues back to the manager. Unless it involves harassment, safety or ethics, encourage the employee to raise it with their manager and offer to help them do so.
  • Look for patterns, not single remarks. One comment is a data point. The same theme from several people is a finding.
  • Share themes with the manager. Debrief without names and focus on what the team needs, not on blame.
  • Never criticize the manager to the employee. Even a raised eyebrow can do damage.

How do you make people comfortable enough to be honest?

Start by explaining why you are there and what will happen to what you hear. Keep the setting informal, listen more than you talk and avoid defending decisions. Thank people for candor, especially when it is uncomfortable. Be clear about confidentiality: individual comments stay private, themes may be shared, and anything involving safety or misconduct must be acted on. Promise only what you can keep.

What should you do afterwards?

The meeting is only half the job. Employees quickly learn whether skip-levels lead anywhere.

  • Write notes the same day, separating facts from opinions and listing any commitments you made.
  • Close the loop in a week or two. Tell the employee what you did or why you could not act.
  • Brief the manager on themes and agree on one or two improvements together.
  • Review quarterly. Look for repeats by team, role and tenure, then name an owner and a date for each fix.
  • Report back to the wider group on what changed because of the conversations.

Nothing makes people more cynical than feedback that disappears, and nothing builds trust faster than visible follow-through.

Frequently asked questions

What is a skip-level meeting?

It is a conversation between a leader and an employee who reports to one of the leader's managers. It skips one level of the hierarchy so the leader can hear directly about work, culture and support.

Does a skip-level meeting undermine the manager?

Not if it is handled well. Tell managers about the program in advance, make no decisions on the spot, send issues back to them and share themes without names.

How long should a skip-level meeting last?

Plan for 30 minutes one-on-one, or 45 to 60 minutes for a small group. That is enough time to move past small talk without feeling like an interrogation.

Should the manager attend the meeting?

No. The point is a candid conversation, and employees speak more freely without their direct manager present. Brief the manager afterwards on themes instead.
