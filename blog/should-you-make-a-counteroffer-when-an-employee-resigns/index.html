Should You Make a Counteroffer When an Employee Resigns?

Learn why counteroffers tempt managers, why they often fail, when one makes sense, how to decide quickly and how to make or decline one without damaging trust.

November 26, 2026 · 6 min read

A strong employee hands in their notice, and your first instinct is to ask what it would take to keep them. A counteroffer can feel like the quickest fix: match the new salary, avoid a painful search and carry on. Sometimes it works. More often it buys a few months and creates new problems. Here is how to tell the difference and decide calmly.

Why are counteroffers so tempting?

The pressure is real, especially in a small business where one person may hold an entire function together.

  • Replacement cost. Recruiting, onboarding and the months before a new hire is fully productive can add up to a large share of an annual salary.
  • Knowledge loss. The person leaving knows your customers, systems, suppliers and the unwritten shortcuts that make work flow. Much of that is not written down anywhere.
  • Timing. Resignations rarely arrive at a convenient moment. Losing someone in the middle of a busy season or a key project feels like a crisis.
  • Team morale. Managers worry that one departure will unsettle everyone else.

Why do counteroffers often fail?

Many managers report that employees who accept a counteroffer leave anyway within a year or so. The reasons are predictable.

  • Pay is rarely the only reason. People usually start looking because of their manager, workload, lack of growth, flexibility or recognition. A higher salary does not fix any of those, so the original frustration returns.
  • Trust shifts on both sides. You now know they were job hunting; they know it took a resignation to be valued. Both sides may start to wonder about loyalty, and the relationship often feels different afterwards.
  • Pay equity and compression. A rushed rise can push one person above peers doing the same job, or close to their manager. When colleagues find out, and they often do, it breeds resentment and fresh pay demands.
  • It sets a precedent. If resigning is the reliable way to get a raise, others will learn that lesson. You end up rewarding threats instead of performance.

When can a counteroffer make sense?

A counteroffer is not always a mistake. It is most likely to work when:

  • The person is genuinely underpaid against the market. If an honest look shows their pay has drifted below the going rate, correcting it is fair, not a bribe. Ask why it took a resignation to notice.
  • The real issue is fixable and not about pay. A change of manager, a different schedule, remote days or a clearer path to promotion can solve the underlying problem in a way money cannot.
  • The role is critical and hard to replace. For a specialist with rare skills, keeping them for a planned period may be worth it while you build cover and document their knowledge.

How do you decide whether to counteroffer?

Before you respond, ask yourself five short questions:

  • Why are they really leaving? Ask them directly and listen. If the answer is not mainly pay, money alone will not keep them.
  • Would I have offered this anyway? If they deserve more, they deserved it last month too. If not, you are paying to avoid inconvenience.
  • Does it fit our salary bands? Check where the offer would place them compared with peers in the same role.
  • What would others think if they knew? Assume the details will become known and ask whether you could defend them.
  • Do I still want this person in a year? If you have doubts about performance or fit, a resignation may be a natural exit.

How do you make a counteroffer well?

  • Move fast. Respond within a day or two. A slow, reluctant offer signals that they are not a priority.
  • Put it in writing. State the new pay, title, responsibilities and any agreed changes, with dates, so nothing is left to memory.
  • Address the real reason. If the problem was growth, include a development plan. If it was workload or flexibility, change those. Pay alone treats the symptom.
  • Stay within your salary bands. If the offer would break the band, either review the band for everyone in that role or reconsider the offer.
  • Do not start a bidding war. Make one considered offer. If the other employer raises theirs and you raise again, the decision becomes about money only, and nobody wins.

How should you respond if you decide not to counteroffer?

Letting someone go well matters as much as trying to keep them. Thank them sincerely, confirm the notice period and last working day, and agree on a handover plan: documented processes, open tasks, key contacts and passwords transferred properly. Tell the team promptly and honestly. Hold a short exit conversation to learn what drove the decision. Finally, leave the door open. Many good employees return later with new skills, and those who leave on good terms often refer customers and candidates.

How can you avoid the counteroffer dilemma?

The best counteroffer is the one you never need to make. Hold stay interviews: short, regular conversations asking what keeps people at the company and what might tempt them away. Run regular pay reviews against the market and your own bands, so nobody has to resign to be paid fairly. A counteroffer is a tool, not a reflex. Use it when it fixes a real problem fairly, and when it does not, let people go with goodwill.

Frequently asked questions

Do counteroffers usually work?

Sometimes, but many managers report that employees who accept a counteroffer leave within a year anyway. They work best when the person is genuinely underpaid or when a real non-pay issue is fixed as part of the offer.

How quickly should I make a counteroffer?

Within a day or two of the resignation. A fast, written offer shows the person is valued; a slow, hesitant one suggests the opposite and gives them more time to commit to the new employer.

Should a counteroffer go above our salary band?

Generally no. Breaking the band for one person creates pay equity problems with peers. If the band itself is below market, review it for everyone in that role instead.

What if the other employer raises their offer again?

Avoid a bidding war. Make one considered offer that addresses the real reason for leaving. If it is not enough, accept the decision gracefully and focus on a good handover.
