Can You Pass Credit Card Processing Fees On to Customers?

Yes, often, but the rules depend on your card network, country and state. Here is how surcharges, convenience fees and cash discounts differ, and what to do.

November 5, 2026 · 6 min read

Card fees typically take a couple of percent of every sale, which adds up quickly on thin margins. It is natural to ask whether customers can simply cover that cost. The short answer: sometimes, but only if you follow card-network rules and local law. This explainer walks through the common questions.

Can I charge customers extra for paying by card?

In many places, yes, but not everywhere and not freely. Some countries and states cap or ban surcharges, and card networks add their own conditions. Rules vary by country, state and even card type, so check current rules with your payment processor and a local adviser before you add any line to an invoice. Nothing here is legal advice.

What is the difference between a surcharge, a convenience fee and a cash discount?

Surcharge

A surcharge is an extra amount added to the price when the customer pays by card. Networks usually cap it, often at or below your actual cost of acceptance, and may restrict it on debit or prepaid cards. Some jurisdictions prohibit it entirely or limit it to the real cost.

Convenience fee

A convenience fee is a charge for using a payment channel that is an alternative to your normal one, for example paying online when you usually take payments in person. Networks tend to have strict conditions, such as a flat amount and a genuinely separate channel. Calling a surcharge a “convenience fee” does not make it one.

Cash discount

A cash discount lowers the price for people who pay by cash or bank transfer. Your listed price is the card price, and the discount is offered for the cheaper method. This is often treated more leniently than a surcharge, but it still has to be disclosed clearly and local rules may apply.

What do card networks require?

Networks publish their own acceptance rules, and they can change. Common themes include:

  • Advance notice: you may need to tell your acquirer or processor before you start surcharging.
  • Caps: a maximum percentage, usually tied to your real cost.
  • Card types: restrictions on debit, prepaid or certain regulated cards.
  • Same treatment: you generally cannot single out one brand while accepting others without extra fees.

Your merchant agreement may repeat or tighten these terms, so read it too.

How should I disclose a card fee?

Disclosure is where businesses most often go wrong. Customers should learn about the fee before they commit, not at the last click. Good practice includes:

  • Signage at the entrance and till, or a notice on your website and checkout.
  • A line on every quote and invoice that shows the fee as a separate item.
  • Showing the fee amount or percentage and the total before the customer pays.
  • A receipt that itemizes the fee.

Are there alternatives to charging a fee?

Yes, and many small businesses prefer them because they avoid friction and legal gray areas:

  • Build the cost into your prices. A small, consistent increase is simple, legal almost everywhere and needs no special disclosure.
  • Steer customers to cheaper methods. Bank transfers and ACH or direct debit usually cost far less than cards. A pay link on the invoice makes them as easy as typing in a card number.
  • Offer an early-payment discount. It rewards fast, low-cost payment without penalizing anyone.
  • Negotiate your rates. If your volume has grown, ask your processor to review pricing, or compare providers.

What are the practical steps?

  • Know your real cost. Review a few statements to see your average effective rate, including fixed fees.
  • Check the rules. Confirm what your country, state, card networks and processor allow.
  • Pick one approach. Choose a surcharge, a cash discount or higher prices, and apply it consistently.
  • Update your paperwork. Change your terms, invoices, website and receipts so they match.
  • Tell your processor. Notify them if required and confirm your setup calculates the fee correctly.
  • Train your team. Staff should be able to explain the fee calmly and in one sentence.
  • Review regularly. Rules and costs change, so revisit your policy once or twice a year.

Will a card fee upset customers?

Some, yes. Surprise is the main trigger, so transparency matters more than the amount. Offering a clearly cheaper option, such as a bank transfer, lets customers feel they have a choice. For business-to-business invoices, many buyers already prefer to pay by transfer, so a fee on cards may barely register.

Frequently asked questions

Is it legal to add a surcharge for credit cards?

It depends on your country and state. Some allow it with limits, others ban it. Check local law, your card network rules and your merchant agreement first.

Is a convenience fee the same as a surcharge?

No. A convenience fee is tied to an alternative payment channel and has stricter conditions. Renaming a surcharge does not change how networks treat it.

Is a cash discount easier than a surcharge?

Often it is treated more leniently, but you still must disclose it clearly and follow local rules. Confirm with your processor before launching.

How do I avoid card fees without upsetting customers?

Build the cost into your prices, or offer a bank transfer or ACH pay link on invoices. Customers who prefer low-cost methods can choose them without a penalty.

Do I have to tell customers about the fee?

Yes. Show it before payment on signs, quotes, your checkout and invoices, and itemize it on the receipt so nobody is surprised.
