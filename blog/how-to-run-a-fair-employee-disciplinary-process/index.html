How Do You Run a Fair Employee Disciplinary Process?

A fair disciplinary process is clear, documented and consistent. Learn the steps from verbal warning to written warning, and how to protect everyone involved.

October 13, 2026 · 6 min read

Few moments test a manager like a conduct or performance problem with an employee. Act too late and the problem grows. Act too fast or too harshly and you create resentment, or even a dispute. A fair disciplinary process protects the employee, the team and the company alike. This guide answers the questions managers ask most. It is general guidance, not legal advice: rules differ by country, by contract and sometimes by company size, so always check your local employment law before acting.

What makes a disciplinary process fair?

Fairness rests on a few simple principles. The employee knows the standard they are expected to meet. The facts are checked before conclusions are drawn. The person gets a real chance to respond. Similar cases lead to similar outcomes. And the response is proportionate, so a small lapse is not treated like serious misconduct.

What are the usual steps?

1. Start with an informal conversation

Many issues, such as occasional lateness or a dip in quality, are best handled with a private, honest talk. Describe what you have seen, explain why it matters and agree what needs to change. Note the date and what was said. Often this is all that is needed.

2. Give a verbal warning

If the issue continues, or is serious enough to need a formal response, a verbal warning is the first formal step in many workplaces. State clearly that it is a warning, describe the problem, explain the expected improvement and the timeframe, and say what may happen if nothing changes. Then record it, because “verbal” describes how it is delivered, not whether it is written down.

3. Issue a written warning

If the behaviour repeats, or the first incident was more serious, move to a written warning. It should name the issue, give dates and examples, refer to the relevant policy, set out the improvement required and by when, and explain the possible consequences. The employee should receive a copy.

4. Consider a final warning

Where problems continue, a final written warning makes clear that further issues may lead to more serious action. Many policies also allow warnings to lapse after a set period, for example six or twelve months of good conduct.

5. Decide on any further outcome carefully

Dismissal, demotion or suspension carry real legal weight. Follow your own written policy and take advice from a qualified employment professional or lawyer where needed. Serious misconduct may justify skipping stages, but the investigation and the right to respond still apply.

How should you investigate first?

Never decide on rumour or a first impression. Before any formal step:

  • Gather facts. Collect records, messages, schedules and any relevant data.
  • Speak to witnesses. Keep notes, and keep the conversations confidential.
  • Stay neutral. Where possible, the investigator should not be someone directly involved in the incident.
  • Move promptly. Delays blur memories and make the process feel unfair.
  • Separate fact from opinion. Write down what happened, not what you assume it meant.

What is the right to respond?

Before a warning is issued, the employee should hear the concern and be able to give their side. In practice:

  • Give reasonable notice of the meeting and explain what it is about.
  • Share the evidence you are relying on.
  • Allow a colleague or representative to attend where your policy or local law provides for it.
  • Listen without interrupting, and take any new information seriously, including checking it.
  • Take time to consider before deciding, rather than announcing a result on the spot.

Offering an appeal to someone more senior, or at least not involved in the original decision, adds a further safeguard.

What should you document?

Good records protect everyone, including the employee. Keep:

  • Dates, times and locations of incidents and meetings.
  • The policy or standard that applies.
  • Evidence gathered and who gave it.
  • The employee's response, in their own words where possible.
  • The outcome, the agreed actions and the review date.
  • Confirmation that the employee received the warning.

Store these records securely and limit access, because disciplinary files contain sensitive personal data. Retention rules vary by country, so check what applies to you.

How do you stay consistent?

Inconsistency is the fastest way to lose trust, and it can expose a company to claims of discrimination. Apply the same standard to everyone, whatever their seniority or popularity. Before deciding, ask how similar cases were handled in the past, and write down the reasons if you treat a case differently. A clear written policy, shared with all staff, makes consistency far easier.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Skipping the conversation. A written warning that arrives with no earlier discussion feels like an ambush.
  • Vague wording. “Attitude problem” helps nobody. Describe specific behaviour.
  • Deciding before listening. If the outcome is fixed in advance, the meeting is a formality.
  • Public criticism. Keep every step private.
  • No follow-up. Set a review date and meet again, so improvement is recognised as well as failure.

Making the process easier to manage

A paper trail only works if it can be found. Scattered emails and personal notes make consistency hard. Be clear, check the facts, listen properly, write it down and treat like cases alike. A fair process is not only safer for the company, it gives people the best chance to improve.

Frequently asked questions

Do I always have to give a verbal warning before a written one?

Not necessarily. Many policies use stages, but serious misconduct can justify starting at a later step. Follow your own written policy and check local employment law, as requirements differ.

Can an employee bring someone to a disciplinary meeting?

In some countries this is a legal right, in others it depends on company policy or a collective agreement. Check your local rules and state the position clearly in the meeting invitation.

How long should a warning stay on file?

It depends on your policy and local law. Many companies let warnings lapse after six to twelve months of good conduct. Write the period into the warning itself.

Should I document a verbal warning?

Yes. Record the date, what was discussed and what was agreed, and ideally share a short summary with the employee. A warning that was never recorded is hard to rely on later.

What if the employee disagrees with the warning?

Let them state their view in writing and offer an appeal where possible. Reviewing the facts again, ideally by someone not involved, shows the process is genuine.
