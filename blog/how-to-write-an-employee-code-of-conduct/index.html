How Do You Write an Employee Code of Conduct for a Small Business?

A code of conduct sets out how people are expected to behave at work. Learn what to include, how to keep it clear, and how to roll it out and review it.

November 9, 2026 · 6 min read

A code of conduct answers one question: how do we treat each other, our customers and the business? In a small company, where everyone knows everyone, it is tempting to skip it. But unwritten rules are applied unevenly, and the first serious problem is a bad moment to discover that people had different assumptions.

Code of conduct vs. employee handbook

The two are often confused. A handbook is a reference: leave policies, working hours, expenses, equipment, procedures. A code of conduct is shorter and about principles and behaviour: respect, honesty, safety, confidentiality, and what to do when something feels wrong.

Keep them separate if you can. A two-page code that people actually read does more good than a 40-page document that sits in a shared folder. The code can point to the handbook for details instead of repeating them.

What to include

For a small business, a handful of sections is usually enough:

  • Purpose and scope: why the code exists and who it covers (employees, contractors, owners, interns).
  • Values: three to five principles that describe how you want to work.
  • Respectful behaviour: what is expected, and what harassment, discrimination or bullying looks like in practice.
  • Honesty and conflicts of interest: how to handle gifts, side work and personal relationships with suppliers or customers.
  • Confidentiality and data: how customer, employee and company information must be handled.
  • Company resources: reasonable use of equipment, accounts, vehicles and social media.
  • Health and safety: the basics of looking after yourself and others.
  • Speaking up: who to talk to, how to raise a concern, and a clear promise of no retaliation for raising it in good faith.
  • Consequences: what can happen if the code is broken.

Tone and plain language

A code of conduct is not a courtroom document. Write it so a new hire on their first day understands it without help.

  • Use “we” and “you” rather than “the Employee shall”.
  • Prefer short sentences and everyday words.
  • Say what to do, not only what to avoid. “Treat colleagues with respect” is better than a long list of prohibitions.
  • Add two or three short examples of real situations, such as a gift from a supplier or a joke that crosses a line.
  • Avoid threats. A firm, fair tone builds more trust than a harsh one.

Rolling it out

Do not simply email a PDF. Ask a few employees to read a draft and tell you what is unclear. Then introduce the final version in a short team meeting, explain why it matters, and leave time for questions. Managers should be able to explain it in their own words and give examples, because staff will judge the code by how managers behave.

For new hires, include it in onboarding and talk it through during the first week.

Getting acknowledgment

Ask every person to confirm in writing, on paper or electronically, that they have received and read the code. Keep the record in their file with the date and the version. Acknowledgment shows that nobody can reasonably say they were never told, but it is not a substitute for conversation. Repeat it when the code changes significantly.

Enforcing it consistently

A code that is not applied is worse than none, because it teaches people that the rules are optional. Aim for consistency:

  • Treat similar situations in similar ways, whoever is involved, including senior people.
  • Listen to all sides, look into the facts and document what you find.
  • Match the response to the seriousness: a conversation, a written warning, or in serious cases further action.
  • Protect the person who raised the concern and keep the matter confidential as far as possible.

If a case could have legal consequences, take advice before acting.

Reviewing it

Review the code at least once a year, and whenever the business changes: a new location, remote working, new tools, or an incident that exposed a gap. Ask employees what is unclear or missing, update the version number, and tell people what changed.

This article is general information, not legal advice. Employment rules differ by country and region, so have a qualified professional review your code before you finalise it.

Frequently asked questions

Do small businesses really need a code of conduct?

It is not always a legal requirement, but it is good practice. Even a team of five benefits from agreeing in writing on respectful behaviour, confidentiality and how to raise concerns. Check local rules to see whether any specific policies are required where you operate.

How long should a code of conduct be?

Short enough to be read: usually one to three pages. If a topic needs detail, such as expenses or leave, put it in the handbook and link to it from the code.

Should employees sign the code of conduct?

Asking for a signed or electronic acknowledgment is common and useful. It records that the person received and read the code. Combine it with a conversation, so it is not just a formality.

What if a manager breaks the code?

The same standard applies. If leaders are exempt, the code loses credibility quickly. Give employees a way to raise concerns that does not go only through their own manager.

How often should we update it?

Review it at least once a year and after any major change in the business or any incident that showed the code was unclear. Record the version and date each time.
