What Is an Allowance for Doubtful Accounts and How Do You Calculate It?

An allowance for doubtful accounts is a reserve for invoices you may not collect; learn the percentage-of-sales and aging methods, with a worked example and entries.

October 17, 2026 · 6 min read

An allowance for doubtful accounts, also called a bad debt reserve, is an estimate of the part of your accounts receivable that you do not expect to collect. It sits on the balance sheet as a deduction from receivables (a contra-asset account), so your receivables show the amount you can realistically expect to receive rather than the full face value of every invoice.

Why do businesses keep an allowance?

When you sell on credit you record revenue straight away, but some customers will pay late or never. If you waited until each invoice turned out to be uncollectible, the loss would land in a later period than the sale. The allowance method fixes that by recording an expected loss in the same period as the revenue, which gives a more honest view of profit and of what customers owe you. It is generally expected under accrual-based frameworks such as IFRS and US GAAP once balances are material.

How do you calculate the allowance?

There are two common approaches. Both are estimates, and both should be based on your own collection history rather than a generic rate.

Percentage-of-sales method

You apply a fixed percentage to credit sales for the period. If past experience shows that about 1.5% of credit sales go bad and you made $400,000 of credit sales this month, the bad debt expense is $400,000 x 1.5% = $6,000. It is quick and simple, but it focuses on the income statement and ignores how old your open invoices actually are.

Aging method

You group open invoices by how overdue they are and apply a higher loss rate to older buckets. This looks at the balance sheet, so it is usually more accurate. A worked example with $195,000 of receivables:

  • Current: $120,000 x 1% = $1,200
  • 1-30 days overdue: $40,000 x 3% = $1,200
  • 31-60 days overdue: $20,000 x 10% = $2,000
  • 61-90 days overdue: $10,000 x 25% = $2,500
  • Over 90 days overdue: $5,000 x 50% = $2,500

The required allowance is $1,200 + $1,200 + $2,000 + $2,500 + $2,500 = $9,400. This is the target balance, not the expense. If the allowance already holds a $3,000 credit balance from earlier periods, you only need to add $6,400 this period. The percentages above are illustrations; set yours from your own history and adjust for current conditions.

What are the journal entries?

  • Setting up or topping up the allowance: debit bad debt expense $6,400, credit allowance for doubtful accounts $6,400.
  • Writing off a specific invoice: when a $1,800 invoice is confirmed uncollectible, debit allowance for doubtful accounts $1,800, credit accounts receivable $1,800.
  • A recovery after write-off: reverse the write-off (debit accounts receivable, credit the allowance), then record the cash receipt (debit cash, credit accounts receivable).

Notice that the write-off itself does not touch the income statement. The expense was already recorded when you built the allowance, and net receivables stay the same at the moment of the write-off.

How is the allowance different from a write-off?

The allowance is a general estimate for the portfolio: it is created in advance, covers invoices you cannot yet identify as lost, and is charged to expense. A write-off is a specific decision about one named invoice that you have concluded will not be paid; it removes that invoice from receivables and is charged against the allowance. In short, the allowance is the forecast and the write-off is the confirmed loss. Neither one cancels what the customer legally owes, and tax treatment varies by country, so check the rules with your accountant before relying on a deduction.

Why review the allowance monthly?

An allowance set once a year goes stale. A monthly review keeps it close to reality and avoids a large surprise adjustment at year-end:

  • Re-run the aging report and recalculate the target balance.
  • Compare estimates with actual write-offs. If you keep writing off more than you reserved, your rates are too low, and the reverse is also true.
  • Look at big balances individually. One large customer in trouble should be reserved for specifically rather than hidden in a percentage.
  • Adjust for conditions such as a weak sector, a new customer segment, or changed payment terms.
  • Post the true-up entry so the allowance equals the new target, and keep a note of how you reached it.

Frequently asked questions

What is an allowance for doubtful accounts?

It is a contra-asset account that estimates the portion of accounts receivable you do not expect to collect. It reduces receivables on the balance sheet to the amount you can realistically receive, and it is built up by recording a bad debt expense.

Which is better, the percentage-of-sales or the aging method?

The aging method is usually more accurate because it reflects how overdue each invoice is. The percentage-of-sales method is quicker and works for small or stable portfolios. Some businesses use sales for the monthly estimate and aging as a check.

Does writing off an invoice reduce profit again?

Not under the allowance method. The expense was recorded when you set up the allowance, so the write-off debits the allowance and credits receivables, leaving net receivables and profit unchanged at that moment.

How often should I review the allowance?

Monthly is a good habit, and at minimum at every reporting date. Re-run the aging report, compare past estimates with actual write-offs, and top up or release the allowance to match the new target.
