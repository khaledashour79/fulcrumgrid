What Is Accounts Receivable Turnover and How Do You Calculate It?

Accounts receivable turnover shows how many times a year you collect your average receivables. Learn the formula, a worked example and how to improve it.

November 16, 2026 · 6 min read

You can be profitable on paper and still struggle to pay suppliers if customers take too long to settle their invoices. Accounts receivable turnover is one of the simplest ways to see how quickly credit sales turn into cash. This guide explains the formula, walks through an example and shows what to do when the number starts slipping.

What is accounts receivable turnover?

Accounts receivable (AR) turnover measures how many times, over a period, a business collects its average outstanding receivables. A higher ratio means customers pay faster and less cash is tied up in unpaid invoices. A lower ratio means money is sitting with customers for longer.

How do you calculate it?

The formula is:

AR turnover = net credit sales ÷ average accounts receivable

  • Net credit sales are sales made on credit during the period, minus returns, allowances and credit notes. Cash and card sales paid at the point of sale are excluded.
  • Average accounts receivable is usually the opening balance plus the closing balance, divided by two.

A worked example

Suppose a small wholesaler made 1,200,000 in net credit sales last year. Its receivables were 180,000 at the start of the year and 120,000 at the end.

  • Average AR = (180,000 + 120,000) ÷ 2 = 150,000
  • AR turnover = 1,200,000 ÷ 150,000 = 8

The business collected its average receivables balance eight times during the year.

How do you convert turnover into days?

A ratio of 8 is hard to picture, so most teams convert it into days:

Average collection period = 365 ÷ AR turnover

In the example, 365 ÷ 8 = about 46 days. If the wholesaler's standard terms are net 30, customers are paying roughly two weeks late on average. This figure is closely related to days sales outstanding (DSO), which many finance teams track monthly. The two are calculated slightly differently, but both answer the same question: how long does it take to get paid?

What is a good accounts receivable turnover ratio?

There is no universal target. A good ratio depends on your payment terms and your industry.

  • Compare against your own terms. If you offer net 30, a collection period close to 30 to 35 days is healthy. Net 60 terms naturally produce a lower turnover.
  • Consider your sector. Retail and hospitality businesses with frequent, small invoices often see high turnover. Construction, manufacturing and wholesale, where longer terms and milestone billing are common, usually see lower figures.
  • Watch the trend. A steady decline over several quarters is more telling than any single number.

Why does AR turnover drop?

  • Invoices go out late or contain errors, so the payment clock starts late or stops while issues are fixed.
  • Payment terms are vague or not agreed in writing before work starts.
  • Follow-up is irregular, so overdue invoices sit for weeks before anyone chases them.
  • Credit limits are too generous, or new customers get terms without any checks.
  • A few large customers slow down, which can drag the whole ratio down.

How can you improve it?

  • Set clear terms up front. State the due date, accepted payment methods and late-payment consequences on the quote, contract and invoice.
  • Invoice promptly and accurately. Send the invoice the day work is delivered, with the purchase order number and correct contact details.
  • Use a consistent reminder cadence. For example, a friendly note a few days before the due date, a reminder on the due date, then follow-ups at 7, 14 and 30 days overdue.
  • Set and review credit limits. Base limits on payment history and revisit them when customers start paying late.
  • Make paying easy. Offer bank transfer, card or payment links so customers have fewer reasons to delay.
  • Record promises to pay. When a customer commits to a date, log it and follow up if the payment does not arrive.

What are the common calculation pitfalls?

  • Using total sales instead of credit sales. Including cash sales inflates the ratio and makes collections look better than they are.
  • Relying on two data points in a seasonal business. If receivables peak in spring and are low at year end, a simple opening-and-closing average is misleading. Use an average of monthly balances instead.
  • Mixing periods. If you use quarterly sales, use 91 or 90 days, not 365, when converting to days.
  • Ignoring write-offs. Writing off bad debts lowers receivables and can make turnover look better even though cash was never collected.

Frequently asked questions

Is a higher accounts receivable turnover always better?

Usually, but not always. A very high ratio can mean your credit terms are so strict that you are losing sales to competitors who offer more flexible terms.

What is the difference between AR turnover and DSO?

AR turnover shows how many times you collect receivables in a period, while DSO expresses collection speed in days. Dividing 365 by turnover gives a figure close to DSO.

Can I use total revenue if I do not track credit sales separately?

You can as a rough estimate, but the result will overstate turnover if a meaningful share of sales is paid immediately. Separating credit sales gives a far more accurate picture.

How often should I calculate AR turnover?

Monthly or quarterly works well for most small businesses. Tracking it over time matters more than any single result, because trends reveal problems early.
