What to Do When a Customer Is Acquired and Still Owes You Money

A customer changes ownership with invoices still open? Learn who is liable, how to update billing and get old debts acknowledged before they slip away.

November 21, 2026 · 6 min read

You read the announcement on a Monday: your customer has been bought. The press release talks about growth and synergies, but you have three open invoices and no idea who will pay them. Ownership changes are one of the most common reasons receivables quietly go stale. Contacts leave, finance teams merge, systems are migrated, and invoices addressed to the old company get lost on the way. This Q&A covers the practical steps. It is general information, not legal advice, and the rules vary by country, so check the details with a local adviser when significant sums are at stake.

How do I know a customer has changed ownership?

Sometimes you are told. More often you notice the signs: a new company name or logo on emails, a request to update bank or tax details, a new purchase order format, a contact who has left, or payments suddenly arriving from a different entity. Treat any of these as a trigger to confirm what happened. Check the public company register, ask your contact directly and read any formal notice you received. Find out three things: the legal name of the buyer, the date the deal completed, and whether the company you invoiced still exists.

Who is liable for the debt now?

It depends largely on how the deal was structured.

  • Share purchase. The buyer acquires the company itself. The legal entity you contracted with continues to exist under new owners, so it generally still owes you every invoice, even if its name changes.
  • Asset purchase. The buyer takes over selected assets, such as the business, customers or equipment, but not necessarily the liabilities. Your debt may stay with the old entity, which can be left as an empty shell, unless the buyer agreed to assume it or local law transfers it.
  • Merger. Where two companies combine into one, the surviving entity usually takes on the debts of both.

If you are unsure which applies, ask in writing: which entity will settle invoices issued before the completion date? A clear written answer is worth more than a friendly phone call.

What should I update straight away?

  • Contacts. Identify who now approves and pays invoices. Accounts payable often moves to the buyer's shared service centre.
  • Billing entity. Confirm the correct legal name, address and tax number for future invoices. An invoice to the wrong entity is a classic reason for rejection.
  • Vendor onboarding. Many buyers require existing suppliers to register in their own procurement system. Do it early, because until you are set up, nothing gets paid.
  • Purchase orders. Ask whether new purchase order numbers are needed, and whether open orders carry over.

How do I get old invoices acknowledged?

Send the new finance contact a statement of account listing every open invoice with its number, date, amount and due date, together with copies of the invoices. Ask them to confirm the balance in writing, or to tell you which items they dispute and why. A written acknowledgement does two things: it proves the new owner recognises the debt, and in many countries it can extend the limitation period for collecting it. If invoices were addressed to an entity that no longer exists, ask whether they should be reissued, and agree how before you send credit notes.

Should I review credit terms for the new owner?

Yes. The customer you assessed may no longer be the customer you are dealing with. Acquisitions are often funded with debt, and a strong buyer may sit above a weak subsidiary, or the other way round. Run a fresh credit check on the entity that will actually pay you, review your credit limit and look at its payment behaviour over the first months. Until old invoices are acknowledged, consider holding new orders to your existing limit rather than extending more credit.

What if nobody takes responsibility?

Escalate step by step. Start with the new accounts payable team, then the buyer's finance manager or controller, then the person who signed or manages your contract. Keep every message in writing and refer to your statement of account. If the old entity is being wound up, file a claim with whoever is handling it. If weeks pass without an answer, a formal demand letter and, where justified, professional collection or legal advice are the next steps. Do not let the matter drift: debts tend to get harder to collect the longer an integration runs.

What is a realistic timeline?

  • Week 1. Confirm the deal, the buyer and the deal structure. Identify new contacts.
  • Weeks 2 to 3. Send the statement of account, complete vendor onboarding and update billing details.
  • Weeks 4 to 6. Chase written acknowledgement and resolve disputes. Run a credit review.
  • After 60 days. Escalate any balance that is still unconfirmed or unpaid.

The short version

Confirm who bought the customer and how, find out which entity owes you, get onboarded with the new owner, and obtain written confirmation of the open balance. Act in the first weeks, while people still remember the old invoices.

Frequently asked questions

Does the buyer automatically take on the old company's debts?

Not always. In a share purchase the same legal entity continues and still owes you. In an asset purchase the debt may stay with the seller unless the buyer agreed to assume it or local law says otherwise.

Should I reissue invoices to the new company name?

Only if the legal entity has actually changed or the new owner asks for it. A simple name change usually does not require reissuing. Agree the approach in writing before sending credit notes.

Why is a written acknowledgement of the balance important?

It proves the new owner recognises the debt, reduces later disputes, and in many countries it can restart or extend the limitation period for collecting it.

Should I keep supplying the customer after the acquisition?

Usually yes, but run a fresh credit check on the paying entity and consider keeping new orders within your existing limit until the old invoices are confirmed.
