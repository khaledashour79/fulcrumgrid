How Do You Create a 30-60-90 Day Plan for a New Hire?

How to create a 30-60-90 day plan for a new hire: who writes it, what goes in each phase, how to set goals, an example plan, check-ins and common mistakes.

November 28, 2026 · 6 min read

A 30-60-90 day plan is a short written document that sets out what a new hire should learn, deliver and own during their first three months. It gives the employee a clear picture of what good looks like, and it gives the manager a fair basis for feedback. Here is how to put one together.

What is a 30-60-90 day plan, and how is it different from an onboarding checklist?

An onboarding checklist covers tasks that need to happen: the laptop is set up, the contract is signed, the safety training is done. Most items are ticked off once, often by HR or IT. A 30-60-90 day plan is about the person’s progress in the role. It splits the first three months into three phases, each with a few goals, and it is reviewed and adjusted as you go.

You need both. The checklist makes sure nothing is forgotten; the plan makes sure the new hire knows where they are heading and how their progress will be judged.

Who should write it?

The manager drafts it, ideally before the start date, because they know what the role needs to deliver. But the new hire should co-own it. In the first week, walk through the draft together, invite questions, and let the employee add goals of their own. Experienced hires often spot gaps a manager has missed, and a plan the employee has helped shape is one they are far more likely to follow.

HR can supply a template and keep plans consistent across teams, but should not write them on managers’ behalf.

What goes in each phase?

Days 1–30: learn

The first month is about building context, not output.

  • People: meet the team, key colleagues in other departments, and the main internal customers of the role.
  • Tools: get access to, and basic confidence in, the systems the job relies on.
  • Context: understand how the team works, what its priorities are, and why things are done the way they are.

Days 31–60: contribute

In the second month the new hire starts doing real work, with support.

  • First owned work: a piece of work they lead from start to finish, sized so it can succeed.
  • Routine duties: start covering part of the role’s regular workload.
  • Fresh observations: share what they notice as a newcomer, without being expected to fix everything.

Days 61–90: own

By the third month the employee should be running their area with a normal level of supervision.

  • Full responsibility: the core duties of the role are theirs.
  • Measurable goals: targets that reflect what the role is expected to deliver on an ongoing basis.
  • Next steps: a first view of the goals for the following quarter.

How do you write good goals?

  • Be specific: “reconcile the customer ledger weekly with no unexplained differences” is better than “get to grips with finance”.
  • Keep the list short: two to four goals per phase is plenty. A long list turns into a to-do list and hides what matters.
  • Mix the types: include learning goals (what they should know), relationship goals (who they should be working with) and performance goals (what they should deliver).
  • Make them checkable: at each milestone, both of you should be able to say whether a goal was met, partly met or missed.

Example: a plan for an accounts receivable specialist

Here is how a plan might look for a new accounts receivable specialist. Adapt the details to your own processes.

  • Days 1–30 (learn): complete training on the accounting and collections systems; meet the sales team leads and the finance manager; shadow a colleague through a full month-end close; learn the credit and payment terms policy.
  • Days 31–60 (contribute): take over a defined portfolio of customer accounts; send payment reminders and log follow-ups on the agreed schedule; prepare the weekly aged debt report for review.
  • Days 61–90 (own): manage the full portfolio independently; resolve routine disputes with sales without escalation; meet the agreed target for overdue balances; propose one improvement to the reminder process.

Notice that each phase has a learning, a relationship and a performance element, and that the targets get sharper as the employee gains context.

Check in at each milestone

Short weekly one-to-ones keep things on track, but schedule a more structured conversation at day 30, day 60 and day 90. At each one:

  • Review each goal: met, in progress or not met, with examples.
  • Ask for their view: what has helped, what is getting in the way, and what is still unclear.
  • Adjust the plan: change goals that turned out to be unrealistic or irrelevant, and note why.
  • Write it down: a few lines of notes per check-in, shared with the employee.

How does it link to probation?

If the employee is on a probation period, the 30-60-90 day plan is the natural basis for it. The goals give you agreed, written expectations, and the milestone check-ins can serve as your mid-point and final reviews. If probation is longer or shorter than 90 days, align the review dates with it rather than running two separate processes. Probation rules vary by country, so check what applies where the employee works.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Writing it after the start date: the first weeks are when the new hire needs direction most.
  • Too many goals: if everything is a goal, nothing is a priority.
  • Only performance goals: skipping learning and relationships leaves the new hire trying to deliver in isolation.
  • Set and forget: a plan nobody looks at until day 90 is just paperwork.
  • Surprises at the end: if a goal is off track at day 30, say so at day 30.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a 30-60-90 day plan and an onboarding checklist?

An onboarding checklist lists one-off tasks such as equipment, system access and paperwork. A 30-60-90 day plan sets the new hire's goals for learning, contributing and owning the role over three months, and is reviewed at each milestone.

Who should write a 30-60-90 day plan?

The manager drafts it before the start date, then reviews it with the new hire in the first week. The employee should co-own the plan, ask questions and add goals of their own. HR can provide a common template.

How many goals should each phase have?

Two to four per phase is usually enough. Include a mix of learning, relationship and performance goals, and make each one specific enough that you can both tell at the milestone whether it was met.

Can a 30-60-90 day plan be used for probation?

Yes. Its written goals and milestone check-ins give you a fair basis for probation reviews. Align the review dates with the probation length, and check the local rules on probation where the employee works.
