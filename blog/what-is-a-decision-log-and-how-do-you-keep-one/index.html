What Is a Decision Log and How Do You Keep One?

A decision log is a simple record of what your team decided and why. Here is what to write down, where to keep it and how to review it without extra work.

November 5, 2026 · 6 min read

Ask a team why they chose a vendor, a price or a process, and you will often get a shrug. The decision was made in a meeting, a chat thread or someone's head, and the reasoning left with it. This explainer answers the common questions about decision logs and how to keep one without creating paperwork nobody reads.

What is a decision log?

A decision log is a running list of the meaningful decisions your team has made, each with a short note on who decided, what the options were and why one was chosen. Think of it as a ledger for choices instead of money. It does not replace meeting notes or project plans; it pulls out the one thing people later need to find: what was decided.

Why do teams lose decisions?

  • Decisions happen in passing. A call, a chat message or a hallway conversation settles something, and nothing is written down.
  • Notes bury the outcome. The decision sits on page four of a long meeting summary.
  • People move on. When someone leaves or changes roles, the context goes with them.
  • Memory drifts. Two people recall the same meeting differently, and the same debate gets reopened.

What fields should each entry have?

Keep the template short enough that filling it in takes two minutes. These seven fields cover almost every case:

  • Date. When the decision was made.
  • Decision. One clear sentence stating what was decided.
  • Owner. The single person accountable for carrying it out.
  • Context. The situation or problem that prompted it.
  • Options considered. The realistic alternatives, including doing nothing.
  • Rationale. Why this option won, and what trade-offs you accepted.
  • Review date. When you will check whether it still holds.

What should you log, and what should you skip?

Log decisions that are hard to reverse, cost real money, affect more than one person or set a precedent. Pricing changes, choosing a supplier, hiring plans, policy changes and tool selections all qualify. Skip routine choices, such as who brings lunch or which day a recurring meeting falls on, and anything easily undone within a day. A good test: if a new colleague might ask “why do we do it this way?” in six months, write it down.

Where should you keep it?

The best location is the one your team already opens every day. A shared spreadsheet works well because it is searchable and sortable. A shared document or a page in your wiki also works. Whatever you choose, keep it in one place, give everyone access and avoid scattering entries across personal notes. A dedicated management tool is useful once the log grows, but a simple table is enough to start.

How often should you review it?

  • Weekly or after meetings: add new entries while memory is fresh.
  • Monthly: scan for entries whose review date has arrived.
  • Quarterly: mark decisions as confirmed, changed or reversed, and note what you learned.

Never delete a reversed decision. Mark it as superseded and link to the new entry, so the history stays honest.

What does a sample entry look like?

  • Date: 2026-10-12
  • Decision: Move customer invoicing from monthly to twice-monthly billing starting January.
  • Owner: Dana, finance lead
  • Context: Average payment time has crept up, and month-end cash gaps are straining payroll.
  • Options considered: Keep monthly billing; bill weekly; bill twice monthly; offer early-payment discounts.
  • Rationale: Twice-monthly smooths cash without overloading the team. Weekly billing adds too much admin, and discounts cut margin.
  • Review date: 2027-04-15

What are the common pitfalls?

  • Logging everything. A bloated log is ignored. Be selective.
  • Skipping the rationale. A decision without a reason is hard to revisit fairly.
  • No owner. If everyone owns it, nobody does.
  • Writing it weeks later. Details fade fast, so record it the same day.
  • Never reviewing. A log nobody reads is just an archive.

What should you do next?

Open a spreadsheet, add the seven column headings and record the last three significant decisions your team made. Then add one habit: before ending any meeting, ask “what did we decide, and who owns it?” That single question will do more than any tool.

Frequently asked questions

Is a decision log the same as meeting minutes?

No. Minutes record everything discussed. A decision log records only the outcomes that matter, with the reasoning, so they are quick to find later.

How long should an entry be?

Usually three to six short lines. If you need pages, link to the full document and keep the log entry as a summary.

Who should maintain the decision log?

The decision owner writes their own entry, and one person, such as an operations lead, checks that the log stays tidy and reviews are happening.

Can a small team of two or three people use one?

Yes. Small teams benefit because there is less formal documentation. A single shared table is plenty.

What if we later change our mind?

That is normal. Mark the old entry as superseded, link it to the new one and record why things changed.
