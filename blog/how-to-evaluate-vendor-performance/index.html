How Do You Evaluate Vendor Performance with a Simple Scorecard?

A vendor scorecard turns opinions into numbers. Learn which criteria to score, how to weight them, how often to review and when it is time to replace a vendor.

October 11, 2026 · 6 min read

Almost every business depends on outside vendors, from suppliers and freight carriers to software providers and agencies. Most owners judge them by gut feeling: the last delivery, the last invoice, the last argument. A simple scorecard replaces that feeling with a few numbers you collect the same way every time. This guide answers the questions people ask most: what to score, how to weight it, how often to review and when to walk away.

Why use a vendor scorecard?

Without a record, opinions are shaped by the most recent problem or the friendliest account manager. A scorecard gives you a shared, fair basis for comparing vendors, spotting trends early and bringing facts to a difficult conversation. It also protects you when the person who managed the vendor moves on, because the history stays with the business.

What should a scorecard measure?

Four criteria cover most relationships. Keep the list short so it actually gets filled in.

  • Quality. Did you get what you ordered, free of defects? Measure the share of items or tasks accepted first time, the return or rework rate, and the number of complaints.
  • Delivery. Did it arrive when promised? Measure on-time delivery as a percentage, plus the number of partial or missed deliveries.
  • Cost. Is the total price competitive and predictable? Look beyond the unit price to unexpected fees, price increases and the cost of fixing mistakes.
  • Responsiveness. How quickly and clearly does the vendor answer questions and solve problems? Measure average reply time and how many issues are resolved on the first attempt.

How do you weight the criteria?

Not every criterion matters equally. A weight is the share of the final score that each criterion controls, and the weights must add up to 100%. A common starting point is quality 35%, delivery 30%, cost 20% and responsiveness 15%. Adjust it to the relationship. For a food supplier, quality may deserve 50%. For a software provider, responsiveness may matter more than anything. Decide the weights before you look at any scores, so you do not bend them to reach the answer you wanted.

How do you score a vendor?

Use a scale of 1 to 5 and write down what each number means for every criterion. For on-time delivery, for example, 5 might be 98% or better, 3 around 90% and 1 below 80%. Written definitions stop two people from scoring the same vendor differently.

Here is a worked example. A packaging supplier scores 4 on quality, 3 on delivery, 5 on cost and 4 on responsiveness:

  • Quality: 4 × 35% = 1.40
  • Delivery: 3 × 30% = 0.90
  • Cost: 5 × 20% = 1.00
  • Responsiveness: 4 × 15% = 0.60

The total is 3.90 out of 5, or 78%. The number matters less than the pattern: this vendor is cheap and friendly but late too often, which is exactly what to raise at the next meeting.

How often should you review vendors?

Collect data continuously, but score on a fixed rhythm. Quarterly works for most important vendors, monthly for critical ones where a failure stops your business, and once a year for minor ones. Fix the dates in advance, and compare each score with the previous period, because the direction of travel is often more telling than the level.

How do you talk to a vendor about the results?

Share the scorecard instead of springing it as a complaint. Start with what is going well, then show the weakest criterion with specific examples, such as the three late deliveries in March. Ask for the vendor’s explanation, agree on one or two improvements with dates, and write them down. Vendors often respond well to clear feedback, and the good ones will start asking for their own score.

When should you replace a vendor?

Consider switching when one of these happens:

  • The score stays below your minimum, say 3.0, for two reviews in a row despite an agreed improvement plan.
  • A single failure is serious, such as a safety, compliance or major financial problem.
  • The vendor stops responding or repeats the same mistake after promising to fix it.
  • A clearly better option exists after you account for the cost and disruption of switching.

Switching has real costs, including onboarding, testing and a period of lower efficiency, so do not leave at the first bad month. But do not stay out of habit either. Line up a backup vendor before you need one, so that leaving is a choice and not an emergency.

Common mistakes

  • Too many criteria. Four to six is plenty. Long forms do not get completed.
  • Scoring by feeling. If a score has no data behind it, it is just an opinion with a number attached.
  • Judging on price alone. The cheapest vendor often costs most once delays and rework are counted.
  • Never sharing results. A scorecard the vendor never sees cannot improve anything.
  • Changing the rules mid-year. Keep criteria and weights stable so trends stay comparable.

Frequently asked questions

How many criteria should a vendor scorecard have?

Four to six is enough for most businesses. Quality, delivery, cost and responsiveness cover the essentials, and you can add one or two that matter in your industry, such as compliance or sustainability.

What is a good score for a vendor?

On a 1 to 5 scale, a weighted score of 4.0 or higher is strong, 3.0 to 3.9 is acceptable but worth discussing, and anything below 3.0 calls for an improvement plan. Set these bands before you start scoring.

Should small businesses score every vendor?

No. Focus on the vendors that are critical or take the largest share of your spending. A quick yearly check is enough for minor suppliers.

Should I show the scorecard to the vendor?

Yes. Sharing results and agreeing on specific improvements is how scorecards change behavior. Vendors cannot fix a problem they never hear about.
