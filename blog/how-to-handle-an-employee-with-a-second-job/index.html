How Do You Handle an Employee Who Has a Second Job?

A second job is not automatically a problem. Learn what to check in the contract, when it matters, how to talk about it and how to write a fair policy.

October 31, 2026 · 5 min read

Finding out that an employee has a second job can feel unsettling. Are they tired? Are they working on your time? Are they about to leave? Usually the answer to all three is no, and a calm, fair response protects both the business and the working relationship. This guide covers how to assess the situation, hold the conversation and put clear rules in place. Employment law varies a lot from country to country, so treat this as general guidance and check your local rules.

Is a second job actually a problem?

Often it is not. Many people do weekend work, freelance projects or help in a family business alongside their main job, and in many places there is no general ban on it. What matters is the effect on your business, not the fact itself. It becomes a real concern when it:

  • Creates a conflict of interest. For example, working for a direct competitor, a supplier or one of your clients.
  • Puts confidential information at risk. Customer lists, pricing or know-how can leak, even by accident.
  • Hurts performance or reliability. Missed deadlines, frequent lateness or visible exhaustion.
  • Breaks working-time limits. Combined hours may exceed legal maximums or rest requirements.
  • Uses your resources. Company time, equipment, contacts or data for the other job.

If none of these applies, a second job is usually a private matter.

What should you check first?

Before you speak to the employee, read the paperwork:

  • The employment contract. Is there an exclusivity clause, or a duty to ask permission for outside work? Some clauses are enforceable only within limits.
  • Conflict-of-interest and confidentiality clauses. These can apply even where outside work is otherwise allowed.
  • Company policies. Handbook rules on outside activities, equipment and data.
  • Local law. Some countries restrict or protect secondary employment and limit what an employer may require. If the matter is serious, take legal advice before acting.

How do you have the conversation?

Start with curiosity, not accusation. Ask for a private, unhurried meeting and keep the tone factual.

Open with the facts

“I understand you are doing some work outside the company. I would like to understand what it involves and make sure it works for both of us.” Avoid guessing at motives, and avoid making it sound as though you have been watching them.

Ask the right questions

  • What kind of work is it, and who is it for?
  • Does it overlap with our customers, products or working hours?
  • How many hours a week does it take?
  • Does it affect your energy or availability here?

Agree what happens next

Explain which parts concern you and why, listen to the answers, then agree concrete steps: a written approval, limits on hours, or a promise not to use company resources. Confirm it in writing afterwards. If a clause has genuinely been breached, say so plainly and keep your response proportionate to the harm.

What should a side-work policy include?

The best way to avoid awkward cases is a short written policy that applies to everyone. It should cover:

  • An approval step. Employees tell their manager or HR before starting outside work, and you answer within a set time.
  • Clear red lines. No competitors, no conflicts with clients, no use of company time, tools or data.
  • Confidentiality reminders. What counts as confidential information, and that this duty continues.
  • Availability expectations. Core hours, on-call duties and notice periods still apply.
  • Review and consequences. Approval can be withdrawn if circumstances change, and breaches follow your normal disciplinary process.

Keep it reasonable. A policy that bans everything pushes people to hide things; a simple approval route encourages honesty.

What should you watch for in performance and availability?

Judge the work, not the second job. Set clear, measurable expectations and give feedback in the usual way. If quality drops, deadlines slip or lateness becomes a pattern, treat it as a performance issue with specific examples, whatever the cause. Do not assume the side work is to blame without evidence: there may be other reasons, and the employee may need support rather than a warning.

How do working-time limits apply?

Many countries cap weekly working hours and require daily and weekly rest, and those limits can apply to the total across all jobs, not to each one separately. Employers are often responsible for not pushing someone beyond them. Ask how many hours the second job takes, record the answer and check what your local rules require. Fatigue is also a safety risk in physical or driving roles.

Keeping records

Keep the policy, approvals and meeting notes in the employee’s file. A spreadsheet is enough for a small team. Handle it early, fairly and in writing, and a second job usually turns out to be a non-issue.

Frequently asked questions

Can I forbid an employee from having a second job?

It depends on the country and the contract. Exclusivity clauses are often enforceable only within limits, for example to protect against competition or confidentiality risks. Check local law and take advice before acting.

Do I have the right to know about my employee's second job?

Not always. Where the contract or local law requires disclosure or approval, yes. Otherwise it may be private unless it creates a conflict, affects performance or touches working-time limits.

What if the second job is with a competitor?

That is the clearest conflict of interest. Review the contract, talk to the employee promptly and consider legal advice. Depending on the facts, options range from agreed safeguards to ending the arrangement.

Can I discipline someone for being tired at work?

Treat it as a performance or conduct issue based on specific, documented examples. Avoid blaming the second job without evidence, and check whether working-time limits are being exceeded.
