How Do You Write a Job Offer Letter?

A job offer letter turns a hiring decision into reality. Learn its purpose, what every offer should contain, and how to keep it clear, conditional and compliant.

October 9, 2026 · 6 min read

A job offer letter is the moment a hiring decision becomes real. It tells a candidate they have the job, on what terms, and what to do next. Get it right and the candidate signs with confidence; get it vague and you invite confusion, renegotiation or a dispute later. This guide covers what an offer letter is for, what every one should contain, how to keep it conditional and compliant, and the mistakes that quietly create risk. It is general guidance, not legal advice.

What is an offer letter, and how is it different from a contract?

An offer letter is a short, friendly document that formally offers a role and summarises the headline terms: the job, the pay, the start date and the main conditions. Its job is to confirm the decision and give the candidate enough to say yes. An employment contract is the longer, binding agreement that sets out the full legal relationship — duties, notice periods, confidentiality, policies and more. In many places the offer letter itself can become binding once accepted, so treat every line as something you may have to honour. Keep the letter warm but precise, and refer to the contract for the detail.

What should every offer letter contain?

A good offer letter is complete without being a contract. Use it as a checklist and adapt the wording to your organisation:

  • Job title and reporting line. The exact role and who the person reports to.
  • Start date. The intended first day, noted as subject to change by agreement.
  • Compensation and pay frequency. The salary or rate, the currency, and whether it is paid monthly, fortnightly or weekly.
  • Benefits summary. A short list of the main benefits — leave, pension or retirement plan, insurance — with a note that full terms apply.
  • Employment type and probation. Full-time or part-time, permanent or fixed-term, and any probationary period and its length.
  • Working hours and location. Expected hours and whether the role is on-site, hybrid or remote.
  • Conditions and contingencies. Anything the offer depends on, such as satisfactory references, proof of the right to work, or background checks.
  • Acceptance deadline and how to accept. A clear date to respond by, and the way to confirm — a signature, a reply or a returned copy.

How do you make an offer conditional and compliant?

Most offers should be conditional until the checks are complete. State plainly that the offer is subject to those conditions, and that it is withdrawn automatically if a condition is not met. Spell out each contingency rather than hiding it in a footnote, so no one is surprised if an offer is rescinded. On compliance, confirm the right to work before the start date, apply the same template and criteria to every candidate so you can show you treat people equally, and avoid wording that could later be read as a promise you did not intend — for example guaranteeing employment for a set number of years or a bonus you cannot commit to. When in doubt, have a qualified adviser review your standard letter.

How should it read?

Write it the way you would want to receive it: clear, warm and specific. Open by congratulating the candidate and naming the role. Keep sentences short and avoid jargon and internal shorthand the person has not learned yet. Put the numbers — pay, hours, start date — where they are easy to find rather than buried in prose. Close with a genuine welcome and a named contact for questions. A letter that is both precise and human does more to secure a signature than one that reads like a legal notice.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Promises that bind you. Casual phrases like “a job for life” or “annual bonus” can create obligations you did not mean to make.
  • Vague compensation. Leaving out the currency, the pay frequency or whether a figure is gross invites a dispute on day one.
  • No acceptance deadline. Without a date, offers drift and you cannot plan the next candidate.
  • Missing contingencies. If you forget to make the offer conditional, withdrawing it after a failed check becomes far harder.
  • Confusing the letter with the contract. Trying to cram every legal term into the letter makes it unreadable and still incomplete.
  • Inconsistent letters. Different wording for similar roles makes it hard to show fair, equal treatment.

Treat your offer letter as a template you refine over time: start from a clean, compliant base, tailor the few fields that change for each hire, and review the wording periodically with someone qualified. A clear offer is the first thing a new hire reads about working with you — make it count.

Frequently asked questions

Is a job offer letter legally binding?

In many places it can become binding once accepted, especially if it states clear terms. Treat every clause as a possible obligation and get qualified advice if you are unsure.

What is the difference between an offer letter and a contract?

The offer letter summarises the headline terms and confirms the decision; the contract sets out the full legal relationship. The letter usually comes first and refers to the contract.

Should the offer be conditional?

In most cases, yes. Make it subject to things like references and proof of the right to work, and state that it is withdrawn if a condition is not met.

How long should I give someone to accept?

Set a clear date; a few working days is often enough. It gives the candidate time to consider and lets you plan ahead if they decline.
