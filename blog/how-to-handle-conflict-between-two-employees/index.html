How Do You Handle Conflict Between Two Employees?

Two people on your team are clashing. Learn when to step in, how to talk to each person, run a fair conversation, agree next steps and know when to involve HR.

October 30, 2026 · 6 min read

Sooner or later, two people on your team will not get along. Maybe it is a sharp email, a missed handover or a long-running irritation that finally boils over. As a manager you are the one expected to sort it out, often with no training. This guide answers the questions managers ask most: when to step in, what to say, and when the matter is bigger than a conversation.

When should you step in?

Not every disagreement needs a manager. Healthy teams argue about priorities and methods, and most of it resolves itself. Step in when any of these is true:

  • The work is suffering: deadlines slip, information is withheld or customers notice.
  • Other colleagues are being pulled into taking sides.
  • The same issue keeps returning after the two have tried to settle it.
  • Either person asks for help or seems distressed.

The earlier you act, the smaller the intervention needed. A ten-minute chat in week one beats a formal process in month three. If you hear anything that sounds like harassment, discrimination or threats, skip straight to the escalation section below.

Talk to each person separately first

Before bringing anyone together, meet each employee on their own. Choose a private place and keep the tone curious, not accusing. The aim is to understand, not to decide.

  • Ask open questions: “Can you walk me through what happened?” and “What impact is this having on your work?”
  • Listen without interrupting, and note specific examples rather than general feelings.
  • Ask what they would like to see change.
  • Be clear that you will not take sides and that you will hear the other person too.

Do not promise to keep everything confidential. You can promise to handle it discreetly, but if you hear about a serious issue you may need to act on it.

Run a facilitated conversation

If the issue is a working relationship problem and both are willing, bring them together. Your role is to referee the process, not the argument.

  • Set ground rules: one person speaks at a time, no interrupting, no personal attacks.
  • Let each person speak for a few minutes about how they see things.
  • Reflect back what you heard so each feels understood.
  • Look for common ground: both usually want the project to succeed and the team to function.

If emotions run high, pause and reschedule rather than push on. Some people need a day to cool down.

Focus on behavior and work impact

Keep the discussion on what people did and what effect it had, not on who they are. “You were not copied on the update, and the client received two different answers” can be solved. “You are difficult and secretive” cannot.

When someone drifts into labels or old grievances, gently bring them back: “Let us stay with what happened last Tuesday and what would help next time.” Ask each person to describe their request as a specific behavior, such as “please send me the file by noon”, rather than a vague wish for respect.

Agree actions and follow up

A good conversation without an outcome rarely lasts. Before the meeting ends, agree on:

  • Two or three concrete actions, with who does what and by when.
  • How the two will communicate day to day, for example a weekly check-in.
  • A date when you will review how things are going, usually two to four weeks later.

Then keep that date. At the check-in, ask what has improved and what has not. Praise progress openly; it reinforces the change.

Document what happens

Keep short, factual notes: dates, who attended, what was said, what was agreed. Stick to observable facts and avoid opinions or guesses about motives. Notes protect everyone, because they show that the issue was handled fairly and give you a record if it returns. Store them where only the right people can see them, and share your notes with the employees if they ask about their own records.

When to escalate to a formal process or HR

Involve HR or follow your formal procedure when:

  • There are allegations of bullying, harassment, discrimination or retaliation.
  • Behavior continues after you have agreed on changes.
  • One person is afraid of the other, or safety is at risk.
  • You are personally involved or have a conflict of interest.

Rules differ by country and company, so check your policy and get HR or legal advice before taking disciplinary steps. Escalating is not a failure; it is how you protect both employees and the organization.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Taking sides. Even a casual comment can destroy your credibility with one person. Stay neutral on the people and firm on the standards.
  • Ignoring it. Conflict rarely fades on its own. It usually spreads to the rest of the team and costs you your best people.
  • Fixing it publicly. Never discuss it in a team meeting or by group chat.
  • Solving it for them. Impose a solution and they will not own it. Help them find one.
  • Skipping the follow-up. Without a review, old habits return.

Handled early and fairly, a conflict can even strengthen a working relationship. The key is to act promptly, listen to both sides and keep everyone focused on the work.

Frequently asked questions

Should I always get involved when two employees disagree?

No. Minor differences of opinion usually resolve themselves. Step in when the work, the team atmosphere or someone's wellbeing is affected, or when the same issue keeps coming back.

Is it better to meet each person separately or together first?

Separately first. It lets each person speak freely and helps you understand both views before you decide whether a joint conversation is appropriate.

What if one employee refuses to take part?

Explain the impact on the work and that you expect professional cooperation. If they still refuse, set clear behavior expectations in writing and consider involving HR.

When does a conflict become an HR matter?

When it involves bullying, harassment, discrimination, threats or retaliation, or when behavior continues after agreed changes. Check your company policy and local rules.
