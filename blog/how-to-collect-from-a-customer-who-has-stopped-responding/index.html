How Do You Collect Payment From a Customer Who Has Stopped Responding?

When a customer goes silent on an overdue invoice, work through a clear sequence: verify contact details, change channels, escalate, send a final notice, then decide when to stop.

November 9, 2026 · 6 min read

Few things are more frustrating than an overdue invoice and a customer who no longer answers. The emails go unread, the calls ring out, and every day of silence makes the debt harder to recover. The good news is that most silent customers are not hiding. They are busy, disorganised or embarrassed. A calm, structured sequence recovers far more money than angry messages sent at random.

Step 1: Check that your message is actually arriving

Before you assume you are being ignored, rule out the boring explanations. Contact details go stale quickly, and an invoice sent to a former employee’s inbox can sit unseen for months.

  • Confirm the email address on the original quote, contract or order, and compare it with the one you used.
  • Check for bounces and delivery receipts. A silent bounce or a spam folder is a common culprit.
  • Look for an accounts payable contact. Many companies route invoices through a shared finance mailbox or a portal, not through the person who ordered.
  • Verify the company still exists and has not changed its name, address or ownership.

If the invoice never reached the right person, the fix is simple: resend it to the correct contact, with a short note and the due date, and keep a record of when you did so.

Step 2: Switch channels

If one channel has failed, do not simply send the same email again. People ignore a message type for many reasons, so vary how you reach them.

  • Phone call. A short, polite call often breaks the deadlock in minutes. Ask whether anything is wrong with the invoice.
  • Text or messaging app, if you already use it with this customer and they have agreed to it.
  • A different person. Try the accounts payable team, an office manager or the owner, depending on the size of the business.
  • Letter. A signed paper letter feels more serious and is easy to prove later.

Keep the tone helpful. Something like “I want to make sure this invoice did not slip through the cracks” invites a reply far more than an accusation does. Offer to resend the invoice, answer questions or agree a payment plan.

Step 3: Escalate in clear stages

Escalation works best when each step is predictable and the customer can see what happens next. A simple ladder looks like this:

Friendly reminder (days 1 to 7 overdue)

A short message with the invoice attached, the amount and the due date. Assume an oversight.

Firm follow-up (days 8 to 21)

Mention the earlier reminder, ask for a payment date, and say whether late fees or terms in your agreement now apply. Add a phone call.

Formal demand (days 22 to 35)

Write to the decision maker, copy a senior contact on your side, and state the deadline and the consequences, such as pausing further work or deliveries. Pausing service is often the single most effective step, as long as your contract allows it.

Step 4: Send a final notice

A final notice is a written, dated statement that you are about to take further action unless payment arrives by a fixed date. Make it factual, not emotional. It should include:

  • The invoice number, amount and original due date.
  • A list of previous attempts to reach them, with dates.
  • Any interest or fees you are entitled to charge under the agreement.
  • A firm deadline, typically 7 to 14 days.
  • What you will do next: pass the account to a collection agency, start legal proceedings or close the account.

Send it by a method that proves delivery, such as registered post or an email with a read receipt, and keep a copy. Do not threaten anything you are not prepared to do, and check local rules on debt collection, which can limit contact frequency and wording, especially for consumers.

Step 5: Know when to stop

Chasing forever has a cost too: your time, your cash flow and your focus. Decide in advance where the line is.

  • Compare cost and amount. For small balances, formal action may cost more than it recovers. Writing the debt off can be the rational choice.
  • Consider a collection agency for mid-sized debts. They usually take a percentage, but you pay little or nothing up front.
  • Consider small claims or legal action for larger sums, when you have a signed agreement and good records.
  • Watch for insolvency. If the customer is bankrupt or closing down, register your claim quickly instead of sending more reminders.
  • Stop extending credit. Whatever you decide, do not supply more goods or services on account until the old balance is settled.

Once you stop, record the decision, update the customer’s credit terms and learn from it. Deposits, shorter payment terms and credit checks for new customers prevent most of these problems before they begin.

Keep records of everything

Whatever the outcome, a clean paper trail protects you. Log every call, message and letter with its date and result. If the matter reaches an agency or a court, those notes are your evidence, and they also show you acted fairly and consistently.

Frequently asked questions

How long should I wait before chasing a late invoice?

A friendly reminder a few days after the due date is normal and rarely causes offence. Waiting weeks usually just lets the invoice slip further down the customer&rsquo;s priority list.

Can I stop working for a customer who has not paid?

Often yes, if your contract or terms allow it. Give written notice first, so the pause is not a surprise, and check your local rules before suspending anything.

Is it worth hiring a collection agency?

For mid-sized debts, often yes, because you pay mostly on results. For very small balances the fee may outweigh the recovery, so compare both numbers first.

What if the customer says they will pay but never does?

Ask for a specific date and amount in writing, and follow up the day after. Repeated promises without payment are a signal to move to a formal demand.
