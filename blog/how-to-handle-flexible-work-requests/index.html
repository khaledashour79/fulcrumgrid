How Do You Handle a Flexible Work Request from an Employee?

A practical guide for managers and HR: set a clear policy, assess requests fairly, use trial periods and document every decision.

October 8, 2026 · 6 min read

An employee asks to work from home two days a week, shift their hours around school drop-off, or move to four days. Many managers answer on instinct, and that is where problems start: inconsistent answers, resentment and, sometimes, legal risk. A simple, repeatable process makes the conversation easier for everyone. This guide covers the policy, the criteria, trial periods, fairness and documentation. It is general guidance, so check the rules that apply where you operate.

Start with a written policy

Handle requests the same way every time, which means deciding the rules before the first request lands. A good policy is short and answers a few practical questions.

  • What counts as flexible work. Remote or hybrid days, changed start and finish times, compressed weeks, part-time hours and job sharing.
  • Who can ask. Any employee, or only after a probation period or a minimum length of service.
  • How to ask. A short written request stating what change is wanted, from when, and how the work will still get done.
  • How fast you will answer. For example, a meeting within ten working days and a written decision within five more.
  • Who decides. Usually the line manager, with HR reviewing refusals.

Listen first, then assess

Meet the employee before deciding anything. Ask what problem the change would solve and whether there are other ways to solve it. You do not need to know the reason in detail, but understanding the need often shows a workable alternative, such as a shifted schedule instead of fewer days.

Use clear, business-based criteria

Judge the request against the job, not against the person. Useful questions include:

  • The role. Can the tasks be done at other times or places? Does the job need physical presence, fixed hours or live cover?
  • The team. Will colleagues carry extra load? Can meetings and handovers still happen at sensible times?
  • Customers and quality. Will response times, service or output suffer?
  • Cost and practicalities. Equipment, security, data protection and any change to pay or benefits.
  • Performance. Is the employee currently meeting expectations, and can results be measured by output rather than hours at a desk?

Reasons that hold up, and reasons that do not

“We have always done it this way” or “I like to see people in the office” are not business reasons. A solid refusal points to something specific, such as “the front desk must be staffed from 8 to 6, and the proposed hours would leave a gap we cannot cover.” If you cannot explain the refusal in one clear sentence, reconsider it.

Consider a trial period

When you are unsure, a trial turns a yes-or-no decision into a low-risk experiment. It also reassures managers who worry that a change will be hard to reverse.

  • Set a fixed length. Eight to twelve weeks suits most arrangements.
  • Agree the measures in advance. Output, responsiveness, team feedback and customer impact, not impressions.
  • Schedule check-ins. One after a few weeks and one near the end.
  • Say what happens at the end. Confirm it, adjust it or return to the old pattern, with notice.

Be careful not to use a trial as a polite way to say no. If you start one, be ready to make it permanent when it works.

Keep decisions fair and consistent

Fairness is about process more than outcome. Two employees in different roles can get different answers, but each should be able to see why.

  • Apply the same criteria to everyone, including senior staff and people without caring responsibilities.
  • Do not rank reasons. A request to study, volunteer or simply work better is as valid to assess as one for childcare.
  • Watch for patterns. If requests from one group are refused more often, find out why.
  • Stay aware that some requests are protected by law in many places, for example those linked to disability, caring duties or religion. When in doubt, take advice before refusing.
  • Offer a right to appeal to someone other than the original decision maker.

Document everything

Good records protect the employee, the manager and the company. For each request, keep:

  • The written request and the date it arrived.
  • Notes from the meeting, including options discussed.
  • The decision, the reasons and the criteria used.
  • Any agreed changes, such as hours, days, location and review date.
  • Trial terms, check-in notes and the final outcome.

If the arrangement is approved, confirm it in writing and, where the change is lasting, update the employment terms. Store everything in one place with limited access, because these records can include personal information.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Deciding on the spot in a corridor conversation.
  • Granting informal favours to some people and not others.
  • Approving without defining how success will be checked.
  • Forgetting the review date, so the arrangement drifts.
  • Refusing without offering any alternative.

Handled well, flexible work requests build trust and help you keep good people. Handled badly, they become the reason those people leave.

Frequently asked questions

Do I have to say yes to a flexible work request?

Not always. Many places give employees a right to ask, and sometimes a right to a fair hearing, rather than a right to get what they want. You should consider each request properly and refuse only for genuine business reasons. Check the rules where you operate.

How long should a trial period last?

Eight to twelve weeks works for most arrangements, long enough to see a real pattern and short enough to adjust quickly. Agree the measures and review dates at the start.

Can two employees in similar jobs get different answers?

Yes, if the reasons are business-based and you can explain them. The risk comes from unexplained differences, so apply the same criteria and write down why each decision was made.

What should I do if a flexible arrangement is not working?

Discuss it early, using the measures you agreed. Try adjustments first. If the arrangement still does not work, give reasonable notice, explain why, and record the outcome.

What should be recorded about a flexible work request?

The written request, meeting notes, the decision and reasons, any agreed terms, trial and review dates, and the final result. Keep it in one secure place.
