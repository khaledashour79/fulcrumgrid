Should You Rehire a Former Employee? How to Decide

Boomerang hires can ramp up fast and fit your culture, but they carry risks. Use this checklist to decide, set fair pay and re-onboard returners well.

November 14, 2026 · 6 min read

A former employee emails to ask whether there is a place for them again. It is tempting to say yes straight away: you know them, they know you, and hiring is slow and expensive. Saying no on principle is just as tempting. Neither reflex is a decision. Rehiring a former employee, often called a boomerang hire, can be one of the best hires a small business makes, or a quiet repeat of an old problem. The difference is in how you decide.

What are the benefits of rehiring a former employee?

  • Faster ramp-up. A returner already knows your products, customers, tools and unwritten rules. Many are productive in weeks rather than months.
  • Known culture fit. You have seen how they work under pressure, how they treat colleagues and how they handle feedback.
  • Lower hiring cost. There is often no agency fee, a shorter search and less time spent by managers screening strangers.
  • New skills and perspective. People who worked elsewhere come back with fresh methods, contacts and a clearer view of what your business does well.
  • A signal to the team. When good people choose to return, it tells everyone the grass was not greener.

What are the risks?

  • The reason they left may still exist. If they left because of a manager, a workload or a pay ceiling that has not changed, expect them to leave again.
  • Team resentment. Colleagues who stayed through a hard period may feel overlooked, especially if the returner comes back more senior or better paid.
  • Pay re-negotiation. A returner who earned more elsewhere may expect a jump that creates pay gaps with loyal staff in the same role.
  • Old problems returning. Performance or behaviour issues rarely disappear on their own.
  • Assuming nothing changed. Your processes, systems and team have moved on. A returner who expects the old company can struggle.

A checklist for deciding whether to rehire

Work through these five questions before you invite anyone back:

  • Why did they leave? Read the exit interview notes, not just your memory. A career step or relocation is very different from a dispute or dismissal.
  • What do the rehire-eligibility notes say? If your records flagged them as eligible or not eligible at exit, start there and understand the reasoning.
  • What was their performance record? Look at reviews, goals and any formal warnings, not general impressions.
  • What does their former manager say? Ask direct questions: would you hire them onto your team again, and what would you need to see this time?
  • What has changed? On both sides. Has the cause of their departure been fixed? What have they learned?

If the honest answer to the first and last questions is "nothing has changed", treat that as a strong warning sign.

How to run the interview and set pay, title and seniority

Do not skip the interview because you already know the person. Run a shorter but real process. Ask about what they did while away, why they want to return now, what would make them leave again and how they would handle the specific issues that led to their exit.

For pay and title, place them against the role, not against their history. Use the same salary band and level criteria you would use for an external hire with the same skills. If they have genuinely grown, a higher level is fair; if they simply negotiated a big offer elsewhere, compare it with what your current team earns before matching it. Decide up front how you will treat their earlier service for seniority, holiday and notice periods. Rules on continuity of service, probation and benefits vary by country, so check local requirements or take advice.

Onboarding a returner: re-onboard, do not skip it

The most common mistake is treating a boomerang hire like someone back from a long holiday. Plan a lighter but deliberate re-onboarding:

  • Walk through what has changed: new tools, processes, policies, customers and people.
  • Repeat required compliance, safety and security training, and reissue system access properly.
  • Introduce them to colleagues who joined after they left, as a new colleague, not a returning legend.
  • Agree goals for the first 30, 60 and 90 days and hold regular check-ins, just as you would for any new starter.

Communicating the rehire to the team

Tell the team before the person arrives, and keep it factual: who is returning, in which role, who they report to and what they will work on. If someone on the team applied for the same role, speak to them privately first. Make it clear that returning does not come with special treatment, and that the door is open to anyone who leaves on good terms.

Building a rehire-eligibility policy

A short written policy keeps these decisions consistent and defensible. Cover:

  • Eligibility at exit. Managers record whether a leaver is eligible for rehire, with a brief, factual reason, at the time they leave.
  • Clear exclusions. For example, dismissal for serious misconduct, or a minimum waiting period after leaving during probation.
  • Who decides. HR plus the hiring manager, not one person's memory.
  • Pay and seniority rules. How earlier service and previous pay are treated, applied the same way to everyone.
  • Fairness and privacy. Eligibility decisions must be based on conduct and performance, never on protected characteristics, and exit records should be kept only as long as your local data rules allow.

The bottom line

Rehire a former employee when they left for good reasons, performed well, are endorsed by their former manager and something meaningful has changed. Then treat them as a new hire with a head start: a real interview, fair pay against the role, proper re-onboarding and an open conversation with the team.

Frequently asked questions

Is it a good idea to rehire a former employee?

Often, yes, if they left for positive reasons, performed well and the cause of their departure has been addressed. Check exit notes, performance records and their former manager's view before deciding.

Should a rehired employee get their old salary back?

Set pay against the current role and your salary bands, the same way you would for an external hire with the same skills, then check it against what your existing team earns.

Do boomerang employees need onboarding?

Yes. Re-onboarding can be shorter, but it should cover what has changed, required training, system access, new colleagues and clear 30, 60 and 90 day goals.

What should a rehire-eligibility policy include?

Who records eligibility at exit and why, clear exclusions, who makes the decision, how earlier service and pay are treated, and fair, lawful handling of exit records.
