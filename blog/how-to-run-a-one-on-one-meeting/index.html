How Do You Run an Effective One-on-One Meeting with an Employee?

A good one-on-one surfaces problems early and builds trust. Learn how often to meet, how to prepare, which questions to ask and the mistakes to avoid.

October 7, 2026 · 5 min read

A one-on-one is a regular, private conversation between a manager and one employee. Done well, it is the most useful half hour of the week: problems surface early, people feel heard, and small frustrations never grow into resignations. Done badly, it becomes a status update that could have been an email. This guide covers how to run one that is worth everyone’s time.

Why are one-on-ones worth the time?

Team meetings are for shared work. A one-on-one is for the person. It is where an employee can raise a concern they would never mention in front of colleagues, ask for feedback, or admit they are stuck. For you, it is the best early-warning system you have for workload problems, unclear priorities and disengagement.

How often and how long should they be?

For most roles, 30 minutes every week or every two weeks works well. Weekly suits new hires and people going through change; every two weeks is enough for experienced staff. Pick a fixed slot and protect it. Cancelling repeatedly tells the employee they are a low priority, however busy you really are.

How do you prepare?

  • Review last time. Check your notes and the actions you both agreed.
  • Collect feedback to give. Pick one or two specific, recent examples, positive or constructive.
  • Let the employee set the agenda. Ask them to add topics beforehand, and add yours after theirs.
  • Remove distractions. Close the laptop and silence notifications.

What does a good structure look like?

A loose structure keeps the conversation from drifting without making it feel like an interrogation. For a 30-minute meeting, one pattern is:

  • Check-in (5 minutes). Ask how they are, as a person first.
  • Their topics (15 minutes). Their priorities, obstacles and questions come first.
  • Your topics and feedback (5 minutes). Share updates and give feedback.
  • Actions and close (5 minutes). Confirm who will do what, and by when.

Which questions work well?

About the work

“What is taking most of your energy this week?” “Is anything blocking you that I could remove?” Open questions get better answers than “Is everything on track?”, which invites a quick “yes”.

About growth

“What would you like to learn this year?” “Which part of your job do you enjoy most?” These show you care about a career, not only output.

About you as a manager

“What could I do differently to help you?” Ask it sincerely, then listen without defending yourself. If the answer stings, say thank you.

How should you behave during the meeting?

Aim to listen about 70% of the time. Ask a follow-up question before offering advice, and stay quiet a few seconds longer than feels comfortable; people often say the real thing after a pause. Keep it confidential: what is said in the room stays there unless there is a safety or legal reason to act, and if so, tell the employee first. Take brief notes, and be open about what you write down.

Common mistakes

  • Turning it into a status report. Project updates belong in other channels.
  • Cancelling or shortening it. Reschedule instead of dropping it.
  • Doing all the talking. If you speak for most of the time, it is your meeting, not theirs.
  • Saving bad news for the annual review. Feedback is most useful soon after the event.
  • Forgetting the actions. Nothing damages trust faster than a promise that quietly vanishes.

Following up

After each meeting, write down the actions with owners and dates, and open those notes at the start of the next one. A shared document is enough to begin with. It becomes useful once you have more people than you can comfortably remember, but the habit matters far more than the software.

Start small: one regular slot, a shared agenda and a promise to follow through. The meeting does not have to be perfect. It has to happen, and the employee has to leave feeling it was theirs.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a one-on-one meeting last?

Thirty minutes suits most roles. Go to 45 or 60 minutes for senior staff or when a lot is happening, but a short, regular meeting beats a long, rare one.

What if the employee has nothing to talk about?

Silence is common at first. Offer a few open questions, such as what is going well, what is frustrating and what they would change. Over time, as trust grows, people bring their own topics.

Should one-on-ones include performance feedback?

Yes, in small, specific doses soon after the event. Regular feedback means the annual review holds no surprises. Keep formal performance discussions separate and planned.

Can one-on-ones be held remotely?

Yes. Use video where possible, keep the same fixed slot and avoid multitasking. Remote employees often need these conversations even more, as there are fewer informal moments.
