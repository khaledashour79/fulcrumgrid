What Is FMEA and How Do You Run One? A Step-by-Step Guide

FMEA helps you find and rank what could go wrong in a process before it does. Learn its origin, the steps, RPN scoring, a worked example and common mistakes.

November 27, 2026 · 6 min read

The wrong price goes on an invoice. An order ships to last year's address. A customer pays a fraudster who sent “new bank details” by email. Looking back, someone could usually have predicted each of these, if anyone had sat down and asked. Failure mode and effects analysis, or FMEA, is a structured way of asking that question, one process step at a time, before the failures reach your customers.

What is FMEA?

FMEA is a method for identifying the ways a process, product or service could fail, understanding the effects of each failure and ranking them so you fix the most important first. A failure mode is the way something goes wrong, such as “invoice sent with the wrong price”. The effect is what that failure does to the customer or the business. Each failure mode is scored for severity, likelihood and how easily it would be caught, which turns a vague sense of risk into a prioritised list of actions.

Where does FMEA come from?

FMEA began as a US military procedure, MIL-P-1629, issued in 1949 to evaluate the effects of system and equipment failures. Aerospace programmes, including NASA in the 1960s, adopted it, and the automotive industry made it standard practice from the 1970s onward. In 2019 the AIAG-VDA FMEA handbook replaced the traditional risk priority number with a method called Action Priority.

When should you use FMEA?

  • Designing a new process. Before you launch a new fulfilment flow, billing routine or customer onboarding.
  • Changing an existing one. A new supplier, software tool or team structure introduces new ways to fail.
  • Recurring problems. When the same kind of error keeps coming back, FMEA makes you look at the whole process, not just the latest incident.

How do you run an FMEA step by step?

  • 1. Scope the process. Pick one process with a clear start and end, such as “approved order to payment received”. Invite three to six people who actually do the work.
  • 2. List the steps. Write each step down in order.
  • 3. Brainstorm failure modes. For each step, ask how it could go wrong: skipped, late, duplicated or done incorrectly.
  • 4. Describe the effects. Note what each failure would do to customers, cash flow, safety or compliance.
  • 5. Identify causes. Ask why each failure could happen.
  • 6. Record current controls. Note what already prevents the failure or catches it, such as a checklist, a system validation or a second review.
  • 7. Score severity, occurrence and detection from 1 to 10. Severity is how bad the effect is. Occurrence is how likely the cause is. Detection is how likely the failure is to slip past your current controls, so a high score means it is hard to catch.
  • 8. Calculate the risk priority number. RPN = S × O × D, giving a value from 1 to 1,000.
  • 9. Assign actions. For the top risks, agree a specific action, a named owner and a due date.
  • 10. Re-score. Once the actions are done, score again to confirm the risk really fell.

What are the limitations of RPN?

RPN is useful but blunt. Very different risks can produce the same number, and a rare but serious failure can score lower than a frequent nuisance. That is why the AIAG-VDA handbook moved to Action Priority, which gives the most weight to severity. A simple rule works well for small teams: any failure mode with a severity of 9 or 10 gets an action, whatever its RPN.

What does an FMEA look like in practice?

Take a small wholesaler that sends around 400 invoices a month. The team scored three failure modes in its invoicing process:

  • Wrong price on the invoice. Cause: prices typed by hand from an outdated price list. Effect: disputes, credit notes and late payment. S 6, O 5, D 4, so RPN 120.
  • Invoice never sent. Cause: drafts left unsent when the person responsible is away. Effect: payment arrives weeks late and nobody notices until month end. S 5, O 4, D 7, so RPN 140.
  • Customer pays a fraudulent account. Cause: a spoofed email announces new bank details. Effect: the customer's money is lost and the relationship is damaged. S 9, O 2, D 6, so RPN 108.

Ranked by RPN alone, the fraud risk comes last. Because its severity is 9, the team acted on it anyway. The actions: prices pulled automatically from the system price list (finance lead, two weeks); a weekly report of unsent drafts (office manager, one week); and a line on every invoice stating that bank details are never changed by email, backed by a call-back check (owner, one week). After re-scoring, the three RPNs fell to 48, 60 and 18.

How is FMEA different from a pre-mortem or root cause analysis?

A pre-mortem imagines that a whole project has already failed and works backward to the reasons. It is quick and suits plans and decisions. Root cause analysis, such as the 5 Whys, investigates a problem that has already happened. FMEA sits between the two: it is proactive like a pre-mortem, but it walks through a process step by step and scores every risk. Our separate guides cover the other two methods in detail.

What are common FMEA mistakes?

  • Scope too broad. Analysing “the whole business” produces a spreadsheet nobody finishes. Start with one process.
  • Inconsistent scoring. Agree in writing what a 3 or an 8 means before anyone scores.
  • Actions without owners. A recommendation with no name and no date rarely happens.

Done well, an FMEA takes a few hours and pays for itself by preventing failures you would otherwise discover one complaint at a time.

Frequently asked questions

What does FMEA stand for?

FMEA stands for failure mode and effects analysis, a structured method for identifying how a process or product could fail, what the effects would be and which risks to tackle first.

How is the risk priority number calculated?

The risk priority number is severity × occurrence × detection, each scored from 1 to 10, giving 1 to 1,000. A higher number means higher priority, but high-severity risks need action regardless.

What is Action Priority in FMEA?

Action Priority was introduced in the 2019 AIAG-VDA FMEA handbook. It rates each risk high, medium or low using tables that weight severity most, then occurrence, then detection.

Can small businesses use FMEA outside manufacturing?

Yes. FMEA works for invoicing, order fulfilment, onboarding or IT changes. A focused session of a few hours on one process is usually enough to find the biggest risks.
