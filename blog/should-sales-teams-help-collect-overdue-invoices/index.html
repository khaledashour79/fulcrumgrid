Should Your Sales Team Help Collect Overdue Invoices?

When should sales reps step in on late payments? Learn how to split roles with finance, set handoff rules and avoid giving away discounts just to get paid.

November 22, 2026 · 6 min read

A key customer is 45 days late, and your finance team has sent three reminders without a reply. Meanwhile the sales rep who manages the account had lunch with the buyer last week. Should that rep be asked to bring up the invoice? For most small and mid-sized businesses the honest answer is yes, sometimes, and within clear limits. Involving sales can unlock payments that reminders never will, but done carelessly it blurs responsibility, strains relationships and quietly gives away margin. This Q&A explains how to divide the work so both teams pull in the same direction.

Why involve sales in collections at all?

Sales reps usually know the customer better than anyone in finance. They know who really makes decisions, which projects are under pressure and whether the customer is happy with what they bought. That context matters when an invoice stalls. In a single call, a rep can often find out that the invoice is stuck because a purchase order was never approved, or that the customer is unhappy with a delivery and is holding payment until someone listens. Customers also tend to respond to the person they trust. And there is a simple business argument: a sale is not finished until the cash arrives, so the person who made the sale has a stake in seeing it paid.

What are the risks?

  • Strained relationships. Reps worry that asking for money will hurt the next deal. Pushed into the role without preparation, they may handle it awkwardly or avoid it altogether.
  • Discounts given away. A rep who wants the matter closed may offer a credit, a free month or a price cut to get paid, often without telling finance.
  • Mixed messages. If finance says payment is due today and the rep says "don't worry about it", the customer will listen to the rep.
  • Lost selling time. Every hour a rep spends chasing routine invoices is an hour not spent on new business.

Who should own collections?

Finance, or whoever handles accounts receivable, should own the process from start to finish: sending invoices and reminders, tracking balances, recording promises to pay, agreeing payment plans and deciding when to put an account on hold. Sales should act as a supporting resource, not a parallel collections team. A useful rule of thumb: finance owns the process and the decisions, sales owns the relationship and the context. Write this down in a short one-page policy so nobody has to guess.

When should a sales rep step in?

Not for every late invoice. Most overdue payments are resolved by a polite, well-timed reminder from finance. Bring in sales when the relationship itself can unlock the payment:

  • Silence after several reminders. The usual contacts are not answering, and the rep knows someone who will.
  • Disputes about what was delivered. If the customer says the work was incomplete or the quantities were wrong, the rep often knows what was promised and can help settle the issue quickly.
  • Strategic accounts. For your largest or most valuable customers, keep sales informed early, even before they need to act, so there are no surprises.
  • Pending renewals or new orders. If a new deal is in progress while old invoices are unpaid, sales and finance must agree on a joint position before anyone talks to the customer.

What does a simple handoff rule look like?

Use clear triggers based on days past due, adjusted to your payment terms:

  • Up to 15 days late. Finance handles reminders alone. Sales can see the status but takes no action.
  • 15 to 30 days late. Finance informs the account owner, who checks there is no hidden problem such as a complaint or a change of contact.
  • 30 to 45 days late. The rep makes one relationship call, focused on finding out what is blocking payment, and reports back to finance the same day.
  • Beyond 45 to 60 days. Finance decides the next step, such as a payment plan, holding new orders or a formal demand, and tells sales before acting.

Disputes skip the queue: as soon as a customer raises one, finance brings in the rep, whatever the age of the invoice.

How do you stop reps from giving discounts to get paid?

Make the rule explicit: sales can listen, explain and gather information, but cannot agree credits, discounts, write-offs or new payment terms. Those decisions belong to finance, with a named approver for anything above a set amount. Give reps a simple line to use: "I understand. Let me take this back to our finance team, and we will come back to you by Friday." It protects the relationship and your margin at the same time.

How should incentives be aligned?

If commission is paid when a deal is booked, reps have little reason to care what happens after invoicing. Many businesses pay commission, or at least part of it, only when the customer actually pays. Others claw back commission on invoices that are written off, or add a cash collection target to quarterly goals. Keep it simple and fair: reps should not be penalised for delays caused by your own billing errors, but they should share responsibility for customers they signed on unrealistic terms.

How do sales and finance stay on the same page?

The biggest source of friction is usually not the policy but the information. Finance calls without knowing about the complaint the rep heard last week, and the rep promises a new order without knowing the account is on hold. Agree on one shared record per account: open invoices, ageing, every contact made, promises to pay and disputes. A ten-minute weekly review of overdue strategic accounts is often enough. A tool such as FulcrumGrid Collection can give both teams the same view of overdue accounts, with notes and next steps attached, so nobody works from an outdated spreadsheet or a forwarded email.

The short version

Let finance own collections, bring in sales at agreed points and for disputes or strategic accounts, never let reps negotiate discounts on their own, link part of commission to cash received, and keep both teams working from one shared record.

Frequently asked questions

Should sales reps send payment reminders themselves?

Generally no. Routine reminders should come from finance so the process stays consistent and the rep keeps a relationship role. Reps step in at agreed points or when a dispute arises.

How many days past due should sales get involved?

A common approach is to inform the account owner at 15 to 30 days late and ask for a relationship call at 30 to 45 days. Adjust this to your terms, and involve sales immediately in any dispute.

Can a sales rep agree a payment plan with the customer?

Only with finance's approval. Reps can gather information and pass on proposals, but credits, discounts and new payment terms should be approved by finance.

Should sales commission depend on the customer paying?

Paying at least part of commission on cash received aligns sales with cash flow. Make sure reps are not penalised for delays caused by your own billing mistakes.
