What If Your Customer Contact Leaves While Invoices Are Unpaid?

Your contact at a customer has left and invoices are still open. Learn the warning signs, how to restart approvals, the fraud risks and how to prevent it.

November 28, 2026 · 6 min read

You have a good working relationship with the finance manager at one of your customers. Invoices go to her, she approves them and payment arrives. Then one day your reminder comes back with an automatic reply: she has left the company. Several invoices are still open, and nobody on the other side seems to know about them. This situation is common and it is fixable, but only if you act before those invoices quietly age into a real problem.

Why is a departed contact a collection risk?

When one person leaves, the processes that ran through them often stop without anyone noticing.

  • Approvals stall. If your invoices needed that person’s sign-off, they may now be sitting in an approval queue with no owner.
  • Invoices sit in a dead inbox. Emails sent to a deactivated or unmonitored address can vanish entirely, or be forwarded to someone who does not know what to do with them.
  • Context is lost. Promises to pay, agreed payment plans or open queries may have existed only in that person’s memory and email history.
  • The handover is incomplete. A successor usually picks up urgent internal work first. Supplier invoices tend to come last.

What are the early warning signs?

  • Bounced emails or automatic replies saying the person no longer works there.
  • Sudden silence from a contact who normally answered within a day or two.
  • A new name or job title appearing in email signatures, professional networking updates or the customer’s website.
  • Invoices that were approved but have not been paid, or replies such as “I’ll need to check who handles this now.”
  • A reorganisation, acquisition or new finance system announced by the customer.

Any one of these is a reason to check the account straight away rather than waiting for the next reminder cycle.

What should you do, step by step?

1. Confirm who now owns accounts payable and approvals

Call the customer’s main number or reach out to another contact you know there. Ask two specific questions: who now processes supplier invoices, and who approves them? These are often different people. Get names, roles and direct contact details for both.

2. Resend open invoices with a statement of account

Send every open invoice to the new owner, together with a statement of account listing invoice numbers, dates, amounts and due dates. Keep the original invoice numbers and dates. The statement gives the newcomer the complete picture in one document and makes it harder for any single invoice to slip through.

3. Re-verify the purchase order and approval chain

Check that each invoice still matches a valid purchase order and that the approval route still exists. If the departed contact was the named approver or budget holder on a purchase order, ask the customer to reassign it. Otherwise an invoice can be perfectly correct and still stuck.

4. Update your contact records and billing email

Replace the old contact in your system immediately and note the date and source of the change. Wherever possible, send invoices to a shared accounts payable mailbox, such as an ap@ or invoices@ address, rather than to a named person. People change jobs; a role-based mailbox stays.

5. Restate any open commitments

If the previous contact promised payment by a certain date or agreed a payment plan, set it out in writing for the new owner and ask them to confirm they will honour it.

How do you guard against fraud during staff turnover?

Staff changes are a favourite moment for payment fraud. Routines are disrupted and new people are unsure what is normal, which is exactly what fraudsters look for. Be alert to:

  • Requests to redirect invoices or statements to a new, unfamiliar email address.
  • Bank-detail change requests. Fraudsters may write to your customer pretending to be you, announcing new bank details. Tell the new contact plainly that your details will never change by email alone.
  • Look-alike domains where a single letter in the sender’s address has been changed.

Verify any change to contact or bank details by calling a phone number you already have on file, never a number supplied in the message announcing the change.

What if payment is still stalled?

Give the new contact a reasonable window, typically about a week, to get up to speed. If invoices remain unpaid after that, escalate politely: contact the financial controller or finance director, explain that the invoices were caught up in a staff change and ask for a firm payment date. If that does not work, follow your normal escalation process, including a formal reminder and, where justified, a review of credit terms or a hold on new orders. Stay courteous throughout. The new contact did not create the backlog, and they may become your most useful ally.

How can you prevent this from happening again?

  • Keep several contacts per account. Record at least one person in accounts payable, one approver and one business contact, so no single departure leaves you without a way in.
  • Use role-based email addresses. Ask for a shared billing mailbox during onboarding and copy individuals only where it helps.
  • Send regular statements. A monthly statement reaches the account even when people change and surfaces forgotten invoices early.
  • Review contacts periodically. Recheck billing details for key accounts at least once a year and whenever you notice a change.
  • Keep history on the account, not in inboxes. Notes, promises to pay and dispute details should live in a shared system your whole team can see.

That last point applies to your own side too. FulcrumGrid Collection keeps contact history, reminders and promises to pay on the customer account rather than in one person’s inbox, so a staff change at either company does not erase what was agreed.

A departing contact is a routine business event, not a crisis. Act quickly, rebuild the approval path, verify every change and spread your relationships across more than one person, and the invoices will get paid.

Frequently asked questions

Should I reissue invoices to the new contact?

No. Resend the original invoices with the same numbers and dates, plus a statement of account. Reissuing risks duplicate payment and blurs the record of when payment was due.

Do the due dates change because my contact left?

No. Payment terms are agreed between companies, not individuals. You can allow a short handover window before escalating, but the original due dates still apply.

How do I verify a request to change billing or bank details?

Call a phone number you already have on file, confirm with a second known contact and never rely on contact details included in the request itself.

How many contacts should I keep for each customer?

At least three: someone in accounts payable, the person who approves invoices and a business contact, plus a shared billing mailbox that does not depend on any one person.
