How Do You Run a Quarterly Business Review for a Small Team?

A quarterly business review keeps a small team honest about results. Learn the agenda, metrics, preparation, decisions and follow-up that make it work.

October 22, 2026 · 5 min read

Small teams live in the week. Tasks, customers and fires fill the calendar, and months pass without anyone stepping back to ask whether the work is pointing in the right direction. A quarterly business review (QBR) is the scheduled moment to do exactly that. This guide answers the questions people ask most: what a QBR is, how to prepare, what to put on the agenda and how to make sure it changes something.

What is a quarterly business review?

A QBR is a structured meeting, held once every three months, where a team looks at the quarter just finished, checks it against its goals and decides what to do in the next one. It is different from a weekly operations review, which keeps the current week on track. The QBR is about direction: what worked, what did not, and where to put time and money next.

Who should attend and how long should it take?

Keep the room small: the people who own results or can approve changes. For a team of five to twenty, that usually means the owner or manager plus the leads of each area. Plan for two to three hours, or half a day if you also set goals for the next quarter. Longer than that and attention fades; shorter and you only skim the numbers.

How do you prepare?

Collect the numbers a week early

Ask each owner to submit their figures at least five days before the meeting. Reviewing data in the meeting itself wastes the one hour you have for discussion.

Compare against a target and a baseline

A number alone means little. Show every metric against the target you set and against the previous quarter, so the room sees both the gap and the trend.

Write a one-page summary

Circulate a short document: three wins, three problems and the questions you want answered. People who have read it in advance arrive ready to decide rather than to listen.

Gather outside views

Add two or three customer comments, or a short survey of the team. Numbers say what happened; people explain why.

What should the agenda include?

  • Results versus goals (30 minutes). Each owner presents their metrics in five minutes, without slides if possible.
  • What we learned (30 minutes). Why did the biggest wins and misses happen? Focus on causes, not blame.
  • Customers and market (20 minutes). What changed outside the company: demand, competitors, prices, regulation.
  • Priorities for next quarter (40 minutes). Pick the few goals that matter and what to stop doing to make room for them.
  • Decisions and owners (20 minutes). Read back every decision, who owns it and by when.

Which metrics belong in a QBR?

Choose five to eight numbers that together describe the health of the business. A typical mix is revenue or sales, margin or cost, customer retention or satisfaction, delivery quality, and one measure each for people and cash. Add the progress of your two or three main projects as a simple status: on track, at risk or late. Resist the urge to show everything. A metric that nobody would act on does not belong in the room.

How do you reach real decisions?

The test of a good QBR is that something changes afterwards. Use three questions for each major topic: should we continue, change or stop? Push the group to choose, even if the choice is a small experiment. Limit next quarter to three priorities; a team of ten cannot chase eight. When a topic needs more data, assign a person and a date instead of leaving it open.

What happens after the meeting?

Send the notes within 24 hours, listing decisions, owners and deadlines in a table. Turn each priority into measurable goals and break them into tasks. Check progress briefly in your weekly review so the plan does not wait three months to be noticed. If you already track KPIs and projects in FulcrumGrid Command Center, you can pull the quarter’s figures from one place before the meeting and keep the follow-up actions visible afterwards, but a shared spreadsheet and a clear owner for each action will also do the job.

Common QBR mistakes

  • Turning it into a status report. If people only read numbers aloud, send them in advance and spend the meeting on decisions.
  • Too many priorities. Everything marked important means nothing is.
  • Hiding bad news. A review that only celebrates teaches people to stop being honest.
  • No follow-up. Decisions without owners and dates quietly disappear.
  • Skipping it when busy. The quarters when you feel too busy are usually the ones that most need a review.

Keep it short, keep it honest and end with a list of decisions. Four good reviews a year can steer a small team better than a dozen unfocused meetings.

Frequently asked questions

How is a quarterly business review different from a weekly review?

A weekly review keeps current work on track and clears blockers. A quarterly review steps back to judge results against goals and decide priorities for the next three months.

Does a very small team need a QBR?

Yes, in a lighter form. Even a team of three benefits from two hours each quarter to compare results with goals, drop what is not working and pick the next priorities.

How many metrics should we present?

Five to eight is plenty. Choose numbers you would actually act on, and show each against its target and the previous quarter.

What if we missed most of our goals?

Treat it as information. Ask whether the goals were unrealistic, the plan was wrong or something external changed, then adjust the plan or the targets rather than repeating them.
