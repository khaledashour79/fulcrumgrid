How Do You Set Up a Purchase Approval Workflow for a Small Team?

A purchase approval workflow keeps spending visible without slowing a small team down. Learn what to approve, who decides, and how to keep it light.

November 7, 2026 · 6 min read

Small teams usually buy things the informal way: someone messages the boss, the boss says “sure,” and a receipt turns up at the end of the month. It works until it does not: a duplicate subscription, a surprise invoice, a purchase nobody remembers approving. A purchase approval workflow fixes this without turning a ten-person team into a bureaucracy. Here are the questions people ask most.

What is a purchase approval workflow?

It is the agreed path a spending request follows before money leaves the business: who asks, who decides, how the decision is recorded, and how the purchase is later matched to an invoice and paid. The goal is simple. Nobody spends company money without a second pair of eyes, and you can always answer the question “who approved this, and why?”

Does a small team really need one?

Yes, but a light one. Without any process, small teams tend to run into the same problems: two people paying for the same tool, spending that drifts past the budget unnoticed, suppliers chosen out of habit rather than comparison, and awkward conversations about who reimburses what. With five or ten people, a clear paper trail protects relationships as much as it protects cash.

How do you set one up?

1. Decide what needs approval

Not everything does. Set a threshold: for example, team members may buy anything under $100 on their own, purchases between $100 and $1,000 need their manager, and anything above needs the owner or finance lead. Treat recurring subscriptions as a special case that always needs approval, whatever the price, because they repeat every month.

2. Name the approvers, and a backup

Assign one approver per tier, and name a deputy for holidays and sick days. If the only approver is away for a week, requests pile up and people quietly find ways around the process.

3. Standardize the request

Use a short form with five or six fields: what is being bought, the supplier, the cost, why it is needed, which budget it comes from and when it is needed. If a request takes more than two minutes to write, people will skip it.

4. Promise a response time

Commit to answering within one business day, for example. Slow approvals are the number one reason people bypass the process, so speed matters as much as control.

5. Record the decision

Every request ends as approved, rejected or sent back for changes, with a date and a name. Keep the record somewhere everyone can find it, not buried in a chat thread.

6. Match the invoice to the approval

When the invoice arrives, compare it with the approved request: supplier, amount and quantity. If they differ, ask before paying. This one step catches most billing errors.

What does a simple setup look like?

A ten-person design studio might use three tiers:

  • Under $100: the buyer decides, then logs the purchase the same day.
  • $100 to $1,000: the team lead approves within one business day.
  • Over $1,000, or any new recurring subscription: the owner approves, ideally after comparing two quotes.

Three tiers, three approvers and one short form. That is enough for most small teams, and it takes an afternoon to put in place.

What tools do you need?

A shared form that feeds a spreadsheet is enough to start. Email and chat work poorly for approvals because decisions get buried, nobody can see what is still waiting, and the history is hard to find when the accountant asks. As volume grows, move to a tool where each request has a status, an owner and a due date. What mistakes should you avoid?

  • Approving everything. If every coffee order needs a signature, people stop asking. Keep the threshold meaningful.
  • One approver for everything. It creates a bottleneck and a single point of failure.
  • Approving after the purchase. A request that arrives with the receipt is a report, not an approval.
  • Forgetting renewals. Subscriptions that renew automatically are where most quiet waste hides.
  • Never reviewing the rules. A threshold that suited a team of four may not suit a team of twelve.

How often should you review the process?

Look at it every quarter. Check how long requests took, how many were rejected, and whether anyone is buying outside the process. If people keep bypassing a step, the step is probably too heavy, so simplify it instead of scolding people.

Start with a threshold, a name and a form. A light process that people actually follow beats a perfect one that nobody uses.

Frequently asked questions

What is a sensible approval threshold for a small team?

Look at your last three months of spending and choose a limit that covers most routine purchases, so only the meaningful ones need a second opinion. Many small teams start somewhere between $100 and $250.

Who approves when the owner is the one buying?

Someone else if possible, such as a business partner, an accountant or a board member. If nobody is available, write a rule down: the owner logs the purchase before buying and reviews all spending monthly with the accountant.

Should subscriptions follow the same process?

Yes, and a stricter one. Approve each subscription when it starts, record its renewal date and check again at every renewal, because recurring costs add up quietly.

How do you handle urgent purchases?

Define an emergency exception in advance: any approver may agree by message, and the buyer logs the request within one business day. Having the exception written down stops it from becoming the normal route.
