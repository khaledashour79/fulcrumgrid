What Is a Remittance Advice and Why Should You Ask for One?

A remittance advice tells you which invoices a customer paid. Learn what it contains, how to request one and how it speeds cash application and cuts disputes.

November 18, 2026 · 6 min read

A payment of 4,860 lands in your bank account. The reference says only “INV PAYMENT”. The customer has three open invoices, and none of them is for 4,860. Someone on your team now has to work out what was paid, and until they do, the money sits unapplied and the customer may receive a reminder they do not deserve. A remittance advice is the simple document that prevents this. Here is what it is and why it is worth asking every customer for one.

What is a remittance advice?

A remittance advice is a note from the customer to you, the supplier, explaining a payment they have made or are about to make. It says which invoices the payment covers, how much goes to each and why any amount differs from what you billed. It can be an email, an attachment, a page in a customer portal, a line in a bank transfer or a slip sent with a cheque. The format matters less than the content: it turns an anonymous amount into specific items you can match.

What does a remittance advice contain?

A useful one usually includes:

  • Payer details. The paying company’s name and, if they know it, the customer account number you use for them.
  • Payment details. Date, total amount, currency, payment method and the bank reference used.
  • Invoice list. Each invoice number being paid and the amount applied to it.
  • Adjustments. Any credit notes used, discounts taken or deductions made, with a short reason.
  • Contact. A person or mailbox to ask if something does not add up.

How is it different from an invoice, a receipt or a statement?

These documents are easy to mix up because they mention the same amounts. The difference is who sends them and why.

  • Invoice. Sent by you to request payment.
  • Statement. Sent by you to summarise everything open on an account.
  • Remittance advice. Sent by the customer to explain what they paid.
  • Receipt. Sent by you to confirm that payment was received.

The remittance advice is the only one of the four that carries the customer’s own view of the payment, which is exactly the information you need to apply it.

Why should you ask customers for one?

Faster cash application

With a clear list of invoices and amounts, matching a payment takes seconds instead of a search through open items, emails and old notes. Payments that cover many invoices, the hardest to match by guesswork, become routine.

Fewer unapplied payments

Most unapplied cash is simply a payment without an explanation. When remittances arrive reliably, the pile of unidentified money shrinks, customer balances stay accurate and your aging report reflects reality.

Fewer disputes and wrong reminders

A remittance advice shows deductions up front, with reasons, so you can approve or question them while the facts are fresh. It also stops the awkward cycle of chasing a customer for an invoice they have already paid, which damages goodwill faster than almost anything else in collections.

How do you request a remittance advice?

Make it easy and make it routine:

  • Put it on the invoice. Add one line near the payment details, for example: “Please quote the invoice number as your payment reference and send remittance advice to remittances@yourcompany.com.”
  • Set up a dedicated mailbox. A shared address used only for remittances keeps them out of personal inboxes and lets anyone on the team pick them up.
  • Ask at onboarding. When a new customer sets you up as a supplier, ask how their payment run sends remittances and confirm the address it should use.
  • Use their portal. Many larger customers publish remittances in a supplier portal instead of emailing them. Get access early and check it on their payment days.
  • Repeat it on statements and reminders. A short line with the remittance address costs nothing and reinforces the habit.

What if a payment arrives without one?

It will happen. Treat it as a short, time-bound task rather than a mystery to park:

  • Try the obvious matches first: the exact amount of one open invoice, or a total that equals several.
  • If it still does not match, contact the payer the same day with the date, amount and reference, and ask which invoices it covers.
  • Hold the amount as unapplied on the right customer account, not in a general suspense account, and note who is following it up.
  • If the same customer keeps paying without remittance, raise it with their accounts payable team as a process fix.

Avoid applying the money to the oldest invoice by default. It feels tidy, but it can settle a disputed invoice by accident.

A simple remittance advice template

If customers ask what you need, send them this list:

  • Payer name and customer account number
  • Payment date, amount and currency
  • Payment method and bank reference
  • Invoice numbers paid, with the amount for each
  • Credit notes applied
  • Deductions or discounts, with a reason for each
  • Contact name and email for questions

Frequently asked questions

Who sends a remittance advice, the buyer or the seller?

The buyer, meaning the customer who is paying. It is sent to the supplier to explain which invoices a payment covers. The supplier may then send a receipt confirming that the money arrived.

Is a remittance advice the same as a payment receipt?

No. A remittance advice comes from the payer and explains a payment. A receipt comes from the person who was paid and confirms the money was received. They often show the same amounts but serve opposite purposes.

Are customers required to send a remittance advice?

Usually not by law, but it is standard business practice and most accounting systems can produce one automatically. Asking clearly on your invoices and at onboarding is normally enough to get it.

What should I do with a payment that has no remittance advice?

Check for an obvious match by amount and customer, then contact the payer the same day to ask which invoices it covers. Until you know, hold it as unapplied on the customer’s account rather than guessing.
