What Is a Chargeback vs a Payment Dispute, and How to Respond?

Learn how a payment dispute differs from a chargeback, what evidence to gather, how to meet deadlines, and how to prevent both as a small business.

November 8, 2026 · 6 min read

When a customer questions a card payment, the money can be pulled back before you have a chance to explain. For a small business that is stressful, but it is manageable if you know the process. This guide explains the terms, what to gather, and how to respond.

What is a payment dispute, and what is a chargeback?

A payment dispute is the broad term for a customer challenging a payment. It might start as a question to you, or as a complaint to their card issuer or bank. A chargeback is the formal step that follows when the bank reverses the payment and takes the funds back from you while the matter is reviewed.

In practice, the dispute is the claim and the chargeback is the reversal. Terminology differs between card networks, banks, and payment providers, so check your provider's own documentation for the exact stages, names, and rules that apply to you.

Why customers file them

  • Unrecognised charge: the customer does not recall the purchase or your business name on the statement.
  • Goods or services not as described, or not received.
  • Unauthorised use: someone else used the card.
  • Duplicate or incorrect amount: the customer believes they were charged twice or too much.
  • Cancelled or refunded, but still charged.

How is it different from an invoice dispute?

An invoice dispute happens between you and your customer. They tell you the invoice is wrong, for example a price, quantity, or delivery issue, and you resolve it directly, often with a corrected invoice or a credit note. A chargeback involves a third party: the customer's bank or card issuer, which decides the outcome using the evidence submitted.

  • Who decides: in an invoice dispute, you and the customer. In a chargeback, the bank or card network process.
  • Money movement: an invoice dispute usually delays payment. A chargeback can remove money you have already received.
  • Timing: invoice disputes follow your payment terms. Chargebacks follow strict response windows set by your provider.
  • Evidence: invoice disputes rely on your agreement and conversation. Chargebacks require a formal, documented response.

A customer who is unhappy with an invoice sometimes goes to their bank instead of contacting you. Answering invoice complaints quickly is one of the best ways to keep them from becoming chargebacks.

How do you respond to a chargeback?

1. Check the deadline immediately

Your payment provider will notify you and state a deadline for responding. Deadlines are usually short and are not extended just because you were busy. Missing one typically means the dispute is decided against you by default. Read the notice carefully and note the date.

2. Understand the reason

The notice usually gives a reason category. Build your response around that specific claim rather than a general defence. If the claim is valid, for instance a genuine duplicate charge, accepting it and issuing a refund may be the sensible, cheaper choice.

3. Gather your evidence

  • The invoice or order confirmation, with date, amount, and description.
  • The signed quote, contract, or the terms and conditions the customer accepted.
  • Proof of delivery, completion, or access, such as tracking details, a signed receipt, or usage logs.
  • Communication with the customer: emails, chat messages, and support tickets.
  • Your refund or cancellation policy, and proof it was shown at the time of purchase.
  • Records that link the customer to the payment, such as account details and the billing name.

4. Submit a clear, factual response

Keep it short and organised. State what was bought, when, what was delivered, and how the evidence supports it. Attach documents in the format your provider asks for. Avoid emotional language; the reviewer is looking for facts. Keep copies of everything you submit.

5. Learn from the outcome

Outcomes, timelines, and any fees vary by provider and network. Whether you win or lose, note why the dispute arose and what you could change.

How can you prevent payment disputes?

  • Use a recognisable billing descriptor so customers can identify your charge on their statement.
  • Be clear before payment: describe what is included, the price, delivery times, and your refund policy.
  • Send invoices and receipts promptly, with clear line items.
  • Keep delivery records: tracking, completion sign-offs, and access logs.
  • Reply to complaints fast. A quick refund or credit note is often cheaper than a dispute.
  • Make support easy to reach, so customers contact you first.
  • Keep a tidy paper trail for every sale, so evidence is ready when you need it.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a payment dispute and a chargeback?

A payment dispute is a customer challenging a charge, either with you or with their bank. A chargeback is the formal reversal that happens when the bank takes the funds back while it reviews the claim.

How long do I have to respond to a chargeback?

Your payment provider sets the deadline and it is usually short, so check the notice as soon as it arrives. Missing it generally means the dispute is decided against you.

What evidence should I submit?

Send the invoice or order record, the agreed terms, proof of delivery or completion, your communication with the customer, and your refund policy. Match the evidence to the reason the customer gave.

How is a chargeback different from an invoice dispute?

An invoice dispute is settled between you and the customer, often with a corrected invoice or credit note. A chargeback is decided by the customer's bank or card network based on submitted evidence.

Should I fight every chargeback?

Not necessarily. If the claim is valid, a refund may cost less than contesting it. Contest when you have clear evidence that the customer received what they paid for.
