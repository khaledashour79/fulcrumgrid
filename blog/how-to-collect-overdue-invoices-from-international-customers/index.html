How Do You Collect Overdue Invoices from International Customers?

A practical guide to chasing overdue invoices across borders: time zones, currency and bank fees, invoice details, follow-up timing and when to escalate.

November 12, 2026 · 6 min read

Selling abroad is a fast way for a small business to grow, and an easy way to end up with invoices nobody seems to pay. A customer in another country can be perfectly honest and still pay late: the invoice went to the wrong office, the wire arrived short, or your reminder landed at 3 a.m. their time. The basics are the same as collecting at home, but details matter more and your fallback options are weaker. Here is what makes it harder, how to prevent most problems, and what to do once an invoice is overdue.

Why are international invoices harder to collect?

  • Time zones. With a six- or nine-hour gap, a single question can take two days to answer.
  • Language. A reminder full of idioms can read as vague or rude, and the accounts payable team may not read your language well.
  • Currency and bank fees. Whoever is not invoiced in their own currency carries the exchange risk, and intermediary banks often deduct fees along the way, so a wire for 5,000 arrives as 4,960.
  • Payment habits. In some markets 60 or 90 days is normal, and payment runs happen only once or twice a month.
  • Legal systems. Late payment interest, required invoice content and the route to court all depend on the jurisdiction, and suing abroad is slow and expensive.

How can you prevent late payment before it starts?

Most cross-border collection problems are created when you quote and invoice, where fixing them costs almost nothing.

  • Agree terms in writing. Put the due date, currency, payment methods, late payment interest and governing law in the contract or order confirmation, and have the customer accept it.
  • Choose the currency deliberately. Invoicing in the customer's currency often speeds up payment because their team does not have to arrange a foreign transfer. If you do, build a margin for exchange movements into the price.
  • Say who pays bank charges. Ask for transfers with all fees paid by the sender, or include expected charges in your price. Otherwise you will spend weeks chasing small shortfalls.
  • Take a deposit from new customers. For a first or unusually large order, 30 to 50% upfront limits your exposure while you learn how the customer pays.
  • Offer a local way to pay. A local bank account or a card payment link removes the step that delays many foreign payments.
  • Get the invoice details right. Include the customer's exact legal name and address, their VAT or tax ID where required, their purchase order number, and your full bank details including IBAN and SWIFT code. An invoice without a PO number or tax ID is often rejected silently.

What follow-up cadence works across time zones?

  • Five days before the due date: a short note confirming the invoice was received and approved. It surfaces a missing PO number or wrong company name while there is still time to fix it.
  • Three days after: a friendly reminder with the invoice attached and payment details repeated.
  • Ten to fourteen days after: a firmer email asking for a payment date, sent to accounts payable and your main business contact.
  • Three to four weeks after: a call booked by email in their time zone, followed by a written summary of what was agreed.

Time emails to arrive early in the customer's working day, not yours. Keep sentences short, avoid idioms and put the invoice number, amount, currency and due date at the top. A reminder in the customer's own language often gets a faster reply. Check local public holidays before reading too much into silence.

What if the payment arrives short?

Compare what you received with the invoice and check the bank details for deducted fees. If the gap is a bank charge and your terms say the customer pays fees, ask for the difference to be added to their next payment. If the gap is larger, treat it as a possible deduction or dispute and ask what it relates to.

How do you escalate if the customer still does not pay?

  • A formal demand letter. State the amount, the agreed terms, a final deadline and your next step. Sending it by email and tracked post, in the customer's language or with a translation, makes it harder to ignore.
  • An international collection agency. Agencies with partners in the debtor's country can contact them locally, in their language, under local practice. Fees are usually a percentage of what is recovered, and higher for cross-border cases.
  • Credit insurance. If export sales matter to your business, trade credit insurance can cover non-payment and often includes collection help. It must be in place before the sale.
  • Legal action abroad. Worth considering only when the amount is large, the customer has assets and your contract says where disputes are settled. Some regions, such as the European Union, offer simplified procedures for small cross-border claims, but for most small debts the cost and delay exceed what you would recover.

Rules on interest, collection and court procedure differ by country. This article is general information, not legal advice; for large or contested sums, speak to a lawyer who knows the customer's jurisdiction.

How do you keep track of it all?

International receivables slip through the cracks because every account is a little different: other currencies, longer terms, contacts asleep while you work. A spreadsheet works for a handful of customers. Frequently asked questions

Should I invoice international customers in my currency or theirs?

Invoicing in their currency usually gets you paid faster, but you take on the exchange risk. Many small businesses do it for their main markets and add a small margin to cover rate movements.

Who pays the bank fees on an international transfer?

Whoever your agreed terms say. Specify that the sender pays all charges, or build expected fees into your price; otherwise intermediary banks may deduct them and leave you short.

Can I charge late payment interest to a foreign customer?

Often yes, if it is in your agreed terms and allowed under the governing law. Some regions, such as the EU, set statutory interest on late business invoices. Check the rules that apply to your contract.

Is it worth suing an overseas customer?

Usually only for large amounts, when the customer has assets and your contract names where disputes are settled. For smaller debts, a formal demand and an international collection agency are generally more cost-effective.
