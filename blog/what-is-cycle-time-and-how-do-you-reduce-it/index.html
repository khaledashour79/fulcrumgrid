What Is Cycle Time and How Do You Reduce It?

Cycle time is how long one unit of work takes from start to finish. Learn how it differs from lead time and takt time, how to measure it, and how to cut it.

November 9, 2026 · 6 min read

Ask most teams why work takes so long and they will say people are too busy. Look closer and you usually find something else: the work itself takes hours, but it sits for days. Cycle time is the measure that makes this visible. This guide explains what it is, how it relates to similar terms, how to measure it and how to bring it down without burning people out.

What is cycle time?

Cycle time is the elapsed time between starting a piece of work and finishing it. In a factory it might be the minutes needed to assemble one unit. In an office it might be the days between picking up an invoice and paying it, or between starting a support ticket and closing it. The unit can be anything repeatable: an order, a ticket, a hire, a report.

Cycle time vs lead time vs takt time

These three terms are often mixed up, yet each answers a different question.

  • Cycle time. How long does the team take to work on one item, from start to finish?
  • Lead time. How long does the customer wait, from the moment they ask until they receive it? It includes the time the request sat in a queue before anyone started.
  • Takt time. How often must we finish one item to meet demand? It is available working time divided by the number of items customers need.

A simple rule: lead time is what the customer feels, cycle time is what the process does, and takt time is the pace demand requires. If your cycle time is longer than your takt time, you will fall behind no matter how hard everyone works.

How do you measure cycle time?

  • Define the start and end. Pick one clear event for each, such as “work begins” and “delivered to the requester”, and never change them mid-way.
  • Timestamp every item. Record the start and finish automatically in your tool where you can. Manual stopwatch studies are fine for a quick baseline.
  • Use the median and a high percentile. The average hides outliers. Report the median and the 85th or 90th percentile so you can say “most items finish within X days”.
  • Segment by type. A small fix and a large project should not share one number.

Wait time vs work time

Split cycle time into work time, when someone is actively doing something, and wait time, when the item sits in a queue, an inbox or an approval. Divide work time by total cycle time to get process efficiency. In many office processes that figure is below 10 percent, which means 90 percent of the clock is spent waiting. That is good news: waiting is usually easier to remove than work is to speed up.

What are the main levers to reduce it?

  • Limit work in progress. The more items in flight, the longer each one takes. Finish what is started before starting more.
  • Shrink batch size. Pass work on in small pieces instead of waiting to hand over a big lot.
  • Remove handoffs. Every handoff adds a queue and a chance for misunderstanding. Let one person or small team own an item end to end.
  • Cut approvals. Set a threshold below which people can decide without sign-off, and give approvers a response deadline.
  • Fix rework. Items sent back for corrections go through the loop twice. Clear requirements at the start are cheap prevention.
  • Balance the bottleneck. Find the stage with the longest queue, then add capacity, simplify it or move easy tasks away from it.
  • Standardise and automate. Templates, checklists and automated notifications remove small delays that add up.

Which pitfalls should you avoid?

  • Speeding up the wrong step. Improving a stage that is not the bottleneck changes nothing for the customer.
  • Cutting quality to cut time. If rework rises, total cycle time rises too.
  • Comparing unlike items. Mixed work types make averages meaningless.
  • Using it to judge individuals. Cycle time describes the process. If people feel judged, they will game the numbers.
  • Ignoring variation. A process that takes two days on average but sometimes twenty is not reliable.

How do you keep tracking it?

Choose one or two flow metrics, review them at a regular meeting, and look at the trend rather than a single week. Change one thing at a time so you can tell what worked, and write down the result. Share the chart with the team: when people can see items stuck in a queue, they usually suggest the fixes themselves.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good cycle time?

There is no universal number. It depends on the type of work and your takt time. A useful goal is to be at or below takt time and to see your own median and 85th percentile trend downward.

What is the difference between cycle time and lead time?

Cycle time covers the active span of working on an item. Lead time starts earlier, when the customer asks, and so includes the time the request waited before work began.

Can cycle time be too short?

Yes, if it is reached by skipping checks or pushing problems downstream. Watch quality and rework alongside speed so a shorter cycle does not just move the cost elsewhere.

How many items do I need to measure it reliably?

Start with a few dozen completed items of the same type. That is usually enough to see a median and a rough spread, which you can refine over time.
