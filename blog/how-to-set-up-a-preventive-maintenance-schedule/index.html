How Do You Set Up a Preventive Maintenance Schedule?

A step-by-step guide to preventive maintenance for small businesses: list your assets, rank them by criticality, set intervals, assign owners and track results.

November 11, 2026 · 6 min read

Most small businesses handle maintenance the same way: something breaks, someone fixes it, and work stops in the meantime. A walk-in fridge fails on a Friday night, a compressor dies in the middle of an order, the clinic's steriliser shows an error before the first appointment. A preventive maintenance schedule replaces that pattern with short, planned checks that catch problems while they are still cheap. This guide shows how to build one in an afternoon and keep it running.

What is the difference between preventive and reactive maintenance?

Reactive maintenance means repairing equipment after it fails. Preventive maintenance means servicing it on a planned schedule, before it fails, based on time or usage. Reactive work is not always wrong: for a cheap desk lamp, running it until it breaks is sensible. The problem starts when critical equipment is treated the same way, because an unplanned failure costs far more than the repair itself: lost sales, overtime, rush parts, spoiled stock and unhappy customers.

Step 1: Build an asset list

You cannot schedule maintenance for equipment you have not written down. Walk through the premises and list everything that needs care to keep working. For each asset, record:

  • Name and location. For example, walk-in cooler in the kitchen, or forklift 2 in bay B.
  • Make, model and serial number. You will need them for parts and manuals.
  • Purchase or installation date. Useful for warranty and replacement planning.
  • Who services it. An internal person or an outside contractor.

Include building systems such as heating and air conditioning, water heaters, fire extinguishers and emergency lighting, not just production equipment. A typical small business ends up with 20 to 60 assets.

Step 2: Rank assets by criticality

Not every asset deserves the same attention. Score each one from 1 to 3 on two questions: how badly would a failure hurt (safety, compliance, lost revenue) and how likely is it to fail (age, usage, history). Multiply the two scores. Assets scoring 6 or 9 get full preventive plans first. Assets scoring 1 or 2 can often run to failure, as long as you know where to get a replacement quickly. This keeps the schedule short enough that people actually follow it.

Step 3: Choose the right intervals

Start with the manufacturer's manual. It usually lists daily, weekly, monthly and annual tasks, and following it may be a condition of the warranty. Then decide which kind of trigger fits each task:

  • Time-based. Every week, month or year. Best for equipment that ages whether it runs or not, such as fire extinguishers, or equipment used at a steady rate.
  • Usage-based. Every so many operating hours, cycles or kilometres. Best for vehicles, compressors and machines whose workload changes from month to month.

Adjust from experience. If a filter is always clean at its monthly check, try every six weeks. If a belt keeps wearing out early, shorten the interval. Local rules may also set minimum intervals for items such as fire safety equipment, so check those first.

Step 4: Turn tasks into checklists with owners

“Service the oven” is not a task anyone can complete reliably. Break each job into a short checklist of concrete steps, for example: clean the door seal, compare the temperature with a probe thermometer, inspect the power cable and record the reading. Then give every checklist one named owner. Shared responsibility usually means nobody does it. Note the tools, parts and safety precautions each task needs, so the owner is not stopped halfway through.

Step 5: Schedule it and set reminders

Put every task on a calendar with an actual due date, not just a frequency. Spread the load so the first week of each month is not overloaded, and plan disruptive work for quiet hours. Remind owners a few days ahead and again on the due date. For usage-based tasks, set a simple routine for reading meters, such as every Monday morning, so the next due point is always known.

Step 6: Log every piece of work

Each completed task should leave a record: date, who did it, what was found, parts used and time spent. Log breakdowns too. After a few months this history shows which assets fail repeatedly, whether your intervals are right and when repairing something has started to cost more than replacing it. It is also the evidence an inspector or insurer will ask for.

Which metrics should you track?

  • Planned vs unplanned maintenance ratio. The share of maintenance hours that were scheduled in advance. Many teams aim for at least 80% planned over time.
  • Downtime. Hours that critical assets were unavailable because of failures. This should fall as the schedule matures.
  • Overdue tasks. The number of preventive tasks past their due date. A growing list is the earliest warning that the plan is slipping.

Review these three numbers once a month. Together they are enough to show whether the schedule is working.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Planning too much at once. A schedule with 300 tasks collapses within a month. Start with your critical assets and add the rest later.
  • Vague tasks. “Check the machine” invites a quick glance. Specific steps produce consistent results.
  • No owner. Tasks assigned to the whole team drift into overdue.
  • Not logging. Without records you cannot tell whether intervals are right or prove that work was done.
  • Never revisiting intervals. Your first schedule is an educated guess. Refine it every few months using your logs.

Keeping the schedule alive

Most maintenance plans do not fail on day one but in month three, when reminders stop and nobody checks what is overdue. A spreadsheet and a shared calendar can work for a handful of assets. Frequently asked questions

How often should preventive maintenance be done?

It depends on the asset. Start with the manufacturer's recommended intervals and any legal minimums, then adjust based on what your inspection logs show.

Is preventive maintenance worth it for a very small business?

For critical equipment, usually yes, because one unplanned failure can cost more than a year of checks. For cheap, non-critical items, running them to failure is often fine.

What is a good planned vs unplanned maintenance ratio?

Many teams aim for around 80% planned work or more. Track your own ratio monthly and look for steady improvement rather than a perfect figure.

Do I need special software to run a maintenance schedule?

No. A spreadsheet and a calendar work for a few assets. As the list grows, a tool with recurring tasks, owners and reminders makes overdue work much easier to spot.
