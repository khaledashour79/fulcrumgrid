What Should an Invoice Include to Get Paid on Time?

A clear invoice gets paid faster. Try this checklist: invoice number, PO number, due date, itemization, payment details, late fees, contact and tax details.

October 31, 2026 · 5 min read

Most late payments are not caused by customers who refuse to pay. They are caused by invoices that are unclear, incomplete or hard to process. A missing purchase order number, a vague due date or no bank details can send an invoice to the back of an accounts payable queue for weeks. This checklist covers what a good invoice includes, so that nothing stands between you and your money.

Why do invoice details affect how quickly you get paid?

On the other side of your invoice is usually someone who processes dozens or hundreds of them. They check each one against an order, a contract and an approval workflow. If anything does not match or is missing, the invoice is set aside and waits for a reply. Every question your invoice leaves unanswered adds days to the payment cycle. A complete invoice removes the reasons to stop.

The essential checklist

  • Invoice number. Use a unique, sequential number, such as INV-2026-0142. It lets both sides refer to the same document, and in many countries sequential numbering is a legal requirement.
  • Dates. Show the invoice date, and where relevant the date of supply or delivery. The invoice date usually starts the payment clock.
  • Purchase order (PO) number. Many larger customers will not process an invoice without one. Ask for the PO number when you accept the job, and print it prominently.
  • Payment terms and due date. Do not write only “Net 30”. Add the actual date, for example “Payment due by 30 November 2026”. A date is harder to misread than a rule.
  • Itemization. List each product or service with a clear description, quantity, unit price and line total. Vague lines such as “consulting” invite questions. “Consulting, 12 hours at 90, week of 5 October” does not.
  • Payment instructions. Include account name, IBAN or account number, bank name, SWIFT/BIC if the customer pays internationally, and the reference they should quote. Mention any other methods you accept, such as card or an online payment link.
  • Late fee terms. State clearly what happens after the due date, such as a fixed fee or interest at a stated rate. It should match your contract, and it works best as a calm statement of terms, not a threat.
  • Contact for queries. Name a person, an email address and a phone number. If a customer has a question and cannot find who to ask, the invoice simply waits.
  • Tax details. Show your tax or VAT registration number, the customer’s where required, the tax rate, the tax amount and the total including tax. Subtotal, tax and grand total should be easy to find.
  • Full legal details for both parties. Use the exact company names, addresses and, where relevant, registration numbers that appear on the contract.

Do the rules differ by country?

Yes, and this matters. Required invoice content, numbering rules, tax wording and even the format vary by jurisdiction. Some countries require specific statements, such as a reverse-charge note for cross-border sales or an exemption reference. Others require electronic invoices in a mandated format, or specific language. Interest on late payment may also be regulated, with statutory rates or caps. Treat this article as a general checklist, and confirm the exact requirements for your country and your customer’s with a qualified accountant or the local tax authority.

How should you present and send the invoice?

Keep it clean

Put the total due and the due date where the eye lands first, usually at the top right. Use a plain layout, one currency and a readable font. Clutter slows down the person trying to approve it.

Send it promptly, to the right person

Invoice as soon as the work is delivered or the milestone is reached, not at the end of the month. Ask who actually receives invoices, as it is often a dedicated accounts payable address rather than your usual contact. Sending to the wrong inbox is a common cause of delay.

Confirm receipt

For larger amounts, a short message asking the customer to confirm they have received the invoice and that it contains everything they need costs nothing and prevents a month of silence.

Common mistakes that delay payment

  • Missing or wrong PO number. The single most common reason an invoice is rejected by large customers.
  • Duplicate or non-sequential numbers. They look unprofessional and trigger checks.
  • Unclear payment terms. If the due date is not obvious, the customer will assume the most generous reading.
  • Changing bank details without warning. Customers rightly verify these, which takes time.
  • Bundling disputed items with undisputed ones. One questioned line can hold up the whole invoice.

After you send it

A great invoice does not end the work. Note the due date, send a friendly reminder a few days before it, and follow up promptly the day after if payment has not arrived. Consistent, polite follow-up is the most reliable way to shorten the payment cycle. Frequently asked questions

What is the minimum an invoice must include?

At a minimum: your details, the customer's details, a unique invoice number, the invoice date, a description of the goods or services, amounts, and any tax. Requirements differ by country, so check local rules.

Should I put a late fee on every invoice?

Only if it is agreed in your contract or permitted by local law. Stating terms clearly and consistently is fair to the customer. Many businesses use late fees mainly as a signal and waive them in practice.

What payment terms are most common?

Net 30 is the most common, meaning payment is due 30 days after the invoice date. Net 14 and Net 60 are also used. Shorter terms improve cash flow, but longer ones may be standard in some industries.

When should I send the invoice?

As soon as the goods are delivered, the service is complete or a milestone is reached. Delays in invoicing delay payment by the same amount, because the payment clock starts on the invoice date.
