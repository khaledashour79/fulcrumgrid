What Is a Gemba Walk and How Do You Do One? (Step-by-Step Guide)

A Gemba walk means going to where the work happens to observe, ask and learn. Learn how to prepare, what to ask, how to follow up and the mistakes to avoid.

November 21, 2026 · 6 min read

Most management decisions are made far from the work itself: in meeting rooms, from reports and dashboards, through summaries of summaries. A Gemba walk is a simple habit that closes that gap. Instead of asking how the work is going, you go and see it, with your own eyes, alongside the people who do it.

What does Gemba mean?

Gemba (also written genba) is Japanese for "the real place", the spot where something actually happens. In lean management it means the place where value is created: the factory floor, the warehouse, the kitchen, the service desk. The practice grew out of the Toyota Production System, where managers were expected to understand problems first-hand rather than through reports. A Gemba walk is a planned visit to that place to observe, ask questions and learn.

Why should managers go and see the work?

  • Reports hide detail. A metric tells you that lead time went up. Only the work tells you that people are waiting for approvals or hunting for missing parts.
  • The people closest to the work know the most. They already know where the friction is; a walk gives them a reason and a moment to say so.
  • It builds trust. Regular, respectful visits show that leaders care about how the work is done, not only about the results.
  • Better decisions. Improvements based on what you saw are more likely to fix the real problem than ones based on assumptions.

How do you prepare for a Gemba walk?

  • Pick a purpose or theme. A walk with a focus, such as safety, handoffs between teams or delays in one step, teaches far more than a general look around.
  • Plan the route. Follow the flow of the work from start to finish, so you see how one step affects the next.
  • Invite the team lead. Tell the area's supervisor in advance, explain the purpose and ask them to walk with you. It should never feel like an inspection or an ambush.
  • Keep it short. Thirty to sixty minutes is usually enough.

What should you observe and ask?

Your job is to understand, not to judge. Watch the process as it really runs, then ask open questions. Treat people with respect: they are the experts in their own work. Look for problems in the process, not people to blame. And resist the urge to fix things on the spot. Quick decisions made in the moment often skip root causes and undermine the team lead.

Good questions to ask

  • Can you walk me through what you are doing right now?
  • How do you know whether this step went well?
  • What usually gets in your way?
  • What happens when something goes wrong here?
  • Where do you wait for information, materials or approvals?
  • If you could change one thing about this process, what would it be?

Then listen. Silence is fine; the most useful answer often comes after a pause.

How do you capture what you see?

Take brief notes as you go: what you saw, where, and what people told you, in their own words where possible. Note facts rather than opinions, for example "three orders waiting for a credit check" rather than "the team is slow". Sort your notes into a few groups afterwards: quick fixes, issues to investigate and ideas to test.

How do you follow up and close the loop?

A walk with no follow-up teaches people that speaking up is pointless. Within a day or two, share what you saw with the team lead, agree on a small number of actions, and give each one an owner and a date. Keep those actions in one shared place, such as FulcrumGrid Command Center, where follow-ups can sit alongside the KPIs they are meant to improve. On your next walk, start by reporting back on what changed since last time.

How often should you do a Gemba walk?

Regularity matters more than length. Front-line supervisors might walk daily, department managers weekly and senior leaders every two to four weeks. Put the walks in your calendar so they survive busy periods, which is when they are most useful.

Can you do a Gemba walk in an office, remote or service team?

Yes. The "real place" is wherever the work happens, even if it is digital.

  • Walk a ticket queue. Sit with a support agent and follow a few tickets from arrival to resolution. Watch where they wait, bounce between teams or get reopened.
  • Follow a customer order end to end. Trace one order from the first enquiry through quoting, fulfilment and invoicing, and note every handoff.
  • Shadow remotely. On a video call, ask someone to share their screen and do a real task while you watch, without taking over.

What are the most common Gemba walk mistakes?

  • Turning it into an audit. Checking for compliance and handing out criticism quickly shuts people down.
  • Talking more than listening. If you do most of the talking, you learn very little.
  • Solving problems on the spot. Note the issue and work on it with the team afterwards.
  • Walking without a purpose. A walk with no theme becomes a social visit.
  • No follow-up. Unanswered feedback is worse than no walk at all.
  • Walking only when things go wrong. If you show up only during crises, the walk will feel like a warning sign.

Frequently asked questions

What is the main purpose of a Gemba walk?

To understand how the work really happens by going to where it is done, observing the process and asking the people who do it. It is about learning and spotting improvement opportunities, not inspecting or judging individuals.

How long should a Gemba walk take?

Usually 30 to 60 minutes. A short, focused walk done regularly is more valuable than an occasional long tour of everything.

Is a Gemba walk the same as management by walking around?

They are related, but a Gemba walk is more structured. It has a clear purpose, follows the flow of the work, focuses on the process rather than people and always ends with follow-up actions.

Should you fix problems during a Gemba walk?

Generally no, unless there is an immediate safety risk. Note the problem, understand its cause with the team and agree on actions afterwards, so solutions address the root cause and the team owns them.
