How Do You Handle an Employee Who Is Always Late?

Chronic lateness wears down teams fast. Learn how to find the cause, document it, hold the first conversation and escalate fairly, with legal caveats.

October 23, 2026 · 7 min read

One person who arrives late now and then is normal life. One person who arrives late every week is a management problem, because the rest of the team notices, covers the gap and starts to wonder whether the rules apply to everyone. This guide answers the questions managers ask most about chronic lateness. It is general information, not legal advice.

Why is this person always late?

Start with curiosity, not a verdict. Lateness has many causes, and the right response depends on which one you are facing.

  • Practical: a changed commute, childcare, caring duties or unreliable transport.
  • Health-related: a medical condition, medication, sleep problems or mental health strain.
  • Workplace-related: unclear start times, a schedule change nobody explained, or low engagement.
  • Habitual: the person simply does not treat start times as firm.

You will only learn which applies by asking, and you should ask before you document anything as a disciplinary matter.

How do you document lateness fairly?

Facts beat impressions. “You are always late” invites an argument; “you arrived after 9:10 on seven of the last fifteen working days” does not.

  • Pull actual clock-in or badge data covering at least four to six weeks.
  • Record dates, scheduled start, actual arrival and any reason given.
  • Note the impact in concrete terms, such as a missed stand-up or a colleague covering a desk.
  • Check that colleagues in the same role are held to the same standard, so you are not singling anyone out.

What should the first conversation look like?

Hold it privately, soon after the pattern is clear, and keep it informal but serious.

Open with the pattern, then listen

Share two or three specific examples and ask what is going on. Then stop talking. People often reveal the real cause once they feel heard, and you may learn about something that changes your approach entirely.

Explain the impact and agree a way forward

Say plainly why start times matter in this role. Then agree what will change, by when, and how you will both know it is working.

Should expectations be in writing?

Yes. After the conversation, send a short note that restates what you discussed: the expected start time, the pattern you observed, any support or adjustment agreed, the review date and what happens if things do not improve. A written summary prevents later disagreement about what was said, and it treats the employee fairly because nothing is a surprise.

What are the escalation steps?

Follow your own written policy and local rules first. A typical path looks like this:

  • Informal conversation with a written follow-up.
  • Review after two to four weeks against the agreed expectation, with a brief note either way.
  • Formal warning if lateness continues without a good reason, stating the required change and a timeframe.
  • Final warning and, ultimately, further action if the problem persists.

Acknowledge improvement when it happens. If the behavior changes, say so and close the matter; escalation is only useful if people can also step back down.

How do you balance flexibility and fairness?

Flexibility is not the opposite of fairness. If someone has a genuine reason, such as a caring responsibility, a shifted start time might solve the problem completely. The test is consistency: would you offer the same adjustment to anyone with a comparable need? Explain the reasoning to the team without sharing private details, and make clear that arrangements are based on role requirements, not favoritism. Where a start time truly cannot move, for example a shift handover or customer coverage, say so honestly.

What legal and pay caveats apply?

Rules differ by country and sometimes by contract, so check before you act.

  • Pay deductions: many jurisdictions restrict or forbid docking pay beyond the time actually missed, and some require written consent. Confirm with payroll or counsel first.
  • Health and disability: if a medical condition may be involved, you may have a duty to consider reasonable adjustments before disciplining.
  • Protected reasons: caring duties, religious observance or pregnancy can carry protections in some places.
  • Process: a fair, documented procedure is usually required before dismissal, and probation rules may differ.
  • Consistency: uneven enforcement can create discrimination risk.

When in doubt, speak to an employment lawyer or your local labor authority before issuing a formal warning.

Frequently asked questions

How many late arrivals count as a pattern?

There is no universal number. A useful rule of thumb is a repeated occurrence over several weeks, such as a few times in a month, beyond what your policy allows. Check the data before you call it a pattern.

Can I deduct pay for lateness?

Often only for time actually not worked, and some countries restrict or prohibit deductions or require written consent. Confirm local rules with payroll or an employment lawyer first.

Should I offer flexible hours?

Consider it when the reason is genuine and the role allows it. Make sure you would offer the same to anyone in a similar situation, and write the new arrangement down.

Do I need a written warning first?

Not always for the first conversation, but a written follow-up is wise. Formal warnings should follow your policy and local law, so check the required steps before issuing one.
