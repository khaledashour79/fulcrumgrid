How Do You Handle an Employee Who Is Always Late?

Handle chronic lateness by finding the cause, checking the policy and records, talking early, documenting steps and applying the rules fairly to everyone.

October 17, 2026 · 5 min read

One late arrival is nothing. A pattern of late arrivals is a different matter: colleagues cover, meetings start without people and resentment builds. Managers often avoid the topic until they are frustrated, and then the conversation goes badly. This guide walks through a calm, fair way to handle it. It is general information, not legal advice, and employment rules on lateness, pay deductions and discipline vary by country.

Why is the person late? Find the cause first

Treat lateness as a symptom before you treat it as a behaviour problem. Common causes include unreliable transport, childcare or caring duties, health issues, poor sleep, an unrealistic start time, or a team culture where nobody really starts on time. Some causes are within the person's control and some are not. You cannot choose the right response until you know which one you are dealing with, so ask before you assume.

What should you check before the conversation?

  • The policy. What does your written policy say about start times, grace periods and notifying a manager? If it is vague or unwritten, fix that first.
  • The records. Gather actual dates and minutes late for the last two or three months. "Always late" often turns out to be three times a week or twice a month, and the facts shape the conversation.
  • The context. Has the start time, shift pattern or workload changed recently? Was the person previously reliable? A sudden change usually has a reason.
  • Your own practice. Have you ever mentioned it before, or let it slide? Silence can be read as acceptance.

How do you hold the first conversation?

Hold it early, in private and soon after a recent instance, not in the middle of a busy day or in front of others. Keep it short and factual.

  • Start with the facts. "In the last six weeks you arrived more than ten minutes late on eight days." Avoid labels such as lazy or unreliable.
  • Explain the impact. Describe how it affects the team, customers or deadlines.
  • Ask and listen. "Is something making mornings difficult?" Let the person answer fully before you respond.
  • Agree what happens next. Confirm the expected start time, how to report an unavoidable delay and when you will check in again.
  • Follow up in writing. A brief, friendly note summarising what was agreed keeps everyone clear.

What documented steps and escalation should follow?

If the first conversation does not change things, move step by step and keep a written record at each stage.

  • Informal reminder: the first conversation, with a short note.
  • Review period: agree a timeframe, often four weeks, and check the records together.
  • Formal warning: if lateness continues without a valid reason, follow your disciplinary procedure, state what must improve and by when, and give the person a chance to respond.
  • Further steps: only after a fair process and, where required, local legal advice.

Do not skip steps, and never act on rumour. Pay deductions for lateness are restricted in many countries, so check local rules before touching someone's pay.

Can flexible options solve it?

Sometimes the best fix is changing the arrangement, not the person. If a bus timetable or school run makes 9:00 impossible but 9:30 works, a shifted start with a matching finish costs the business little. Other options include flexible core hours, a fixed later shift, a few remote days or an agreed grace window. Flexibility is not a reward for lateness. It is a practical response when the work allows it, and it should come with a clear, reliable new start time. If the role needs fixed hours, such as a shop opening or a shift handover, say so plainly and explain why.

How do you stay consistent and fair?

  • Apply the same standard to everyone. If one person is warned for ten minutes late, a favourite colleague should not escape the same.
  • Separate lateness from protected reasons. Illness, disability, pregnancy or caring responsibilities may need adjustments, and may be legally protected in your country. Handle those with support, not sanctions.
  • Protect privacy. Keep personal details between the manager and the few people who need them.
  • Acknowledge improvement. If things get better, say so. People who feel noticed keep improving.
  • Check yourself. Are you reliably on time, and is the rule clear to the whole team?

How do you track attendance without turning it into surveillance?

Good tracking is simple and transparent. Record start times and lateness the same way for everyone, tell the team what is recorded and why, and review the data regularly instead of only after a complaint. Look at patterns by person, day and team: lateness clustered on Mondays or in one team often points to a scheduling or management cause. A spreadsheet is enough for a small team. Find out why, check the facts, talk early and kindly, document each step and keep the rules the same for everyone. Most lateness problems are resolved at the first or second conversation when the person feels heard and the expectation is clear.

Frequently asked questions

How late is too late?

It depends on your policy, role and industry. Many employers set a short grace period, such as five minutes, and treat repeated lateness beyond it as an issue. What matters most is that the rule is written, clear and applied equally.

Can I deduct pay for lateness?

In some countries employers can deduct pay for time not worked, in others it is restricted or needs a contract clause. Check local employment law before making any deduction.

What if the employee has a medical or family reason?

Listen first, then consider reasonable adjustments such as a later start or flexible hours. Health and caring situations may carry legal protection, so handle them with support and take local advice if unsure.

When should I move to a formal warning?

When the informal conversation and review period have not improved things, there is no valid reason, and the expectations were clearly explained. Follow your written procedure and document each step.

Should I mention it if the lateness is only a few minutes?

Yes, but proportionately. A light early conversation is easier and kinder than waiting until the pattern has lasted for months.
