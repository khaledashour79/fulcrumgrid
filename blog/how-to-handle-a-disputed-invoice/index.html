How Do You Handle a Customer Who Disputes an Invoice?

When a customer disputes an invoice, speed and calm matter. Learn how to find the real cause, protect the relationship, resolve it fairly and keep cash moving.

October 3, 2026 · 6 min read

Sooner or later, a customer will tell you an invoice is wrong. Maybe the amount looks too high, maybe the goods arrived damaged, maybe nobody on their side ever approved the order. However it arrives, a dispute stops payment, and a badly handled one can cost you the customer as well. The good news is that most disputes are resolved quickly when you respond with a clear process instead of defensiveness. This guide answers the questions finance teams ask most often.

What should I do first when a customer disputes an invoice?

Acknowledge it the same day. A short, polite reply that says you have received the dispute, you are looking into it and you will come back by a specific date does more than any long explanation. Silence is what turns a small query into an escalation. Do not argue the merits in that first message, and do not send a payment reminder at the same time.

How do I find out what the dispute is really about?

Ask for specifics. “The invoice is wrong” is not yet a dispute you can resolve. Ask which line, which amount or which delivery is in question, and what they expected instead. Most disputes fall into a handful of categories:

  • Pricing or quantity errors: the invoice does not match the quote, contract or purchase order.
  • Delivery or quality issues: goods or services were late, incomplete or not as described.
  • Administrative problems: wrong PO number, wrong entity, missing VAT details or a duplicate invoice.
  • Internal confusion on their side: the person who ordered never told accounts payable.
  • Genuine disagreement: they believe they do not owe the amount, or part of it.

Often it is an administrative problem, and those are the easiest to fix.

Should I stop chasing payment while it is disputed?

Pause chasing the disputed amount, but not the undisputed part. If a customer disputes one line on a five-line invoice, ask them to pay the other four on time and agree to handle the disputed line separately. Better still, issue a corrected invoice or a credit note for the contested item so the rest is clean. This keeps cash flowing and shows good faith. Make a note of the date so the dispute does not quietly age for months.

How do I investigate quickly and fairly?

Gather the paper trail before you reply with a position:

  • The signed quote, contract or purchase order.
  • Delivery notes, timesheets or proof of service.
  • Emails or messages where scope or price changed.
  • Your original invoice and any earlier reminders.

Involve the person who owns the relationship, such as the account manager or project lead, because they often know what was promised verbally. Be honest with yourself: if the evidence says you made a mistake, say so early. Admitting an error quickly builds more trust than defending it for two weeks.

How do I resolve it?

Once you know the facts, there are usually three outcomes:

  • You were right. Share the evidence calmly, explain how the amount was calculated and confirm the due date.
  • You were wrong. Apologise briefly, issue a credit note or corrected invoice and confirm the new amount and date.
  • It is partly both. Offer a fair middle ground, such as a partial credit, and agree it in writing.

For larger or older disputes, pick up the phone or arrange a short call. Tone is hard to judge by email, and a ten-minute conversation often settles what ten emails cannot. Afterwards, confirm what was agreed in writing, including the final amount and the payment date.

What should I avoid?

  • Ignoring it and hoping it goes away. It will not, and the invoice only gets older.
  • Automated reminders on a disputed invoice. Nothing irritates a customer more than a stern reminder for something they have already raised with you.
  • Threatening escalation too early. Collections letters and late fees can wait until the dispute is genuinely resolved or clearly in bad faith.
  • Verbal-only agreements. If it is not written down, it will be disputed again.

How do I stop the same disputes recurring?

Treat each dispute as information. Track the reason, and after a few months look for patterns. If many disputes are about missing PO numbers, collect them before invoicing. If they are about scope, tighten your quotes. If delivery is the issue, that is an operations problem, not a finance one. Sending invoices promptly, with clear descriptions and the customer’s own references, prevents a large share of disputes before they start.

A spreadsheet and a shared inbox can handle this for a small number of invoices. As volume grows, it helps to have disputes flagged on the invoice itself, with reminders paused for the contested amount and a visible history of who said what. Handled well, a dispute is not a failure. It is a chance to show a customer that you are organised, fair and easy to work with, and that is often what gets you paid on time the next ten times.

Frequently asked questions

How long should I take to respond to an invoice dispute?

Acknowledge it within one business day, even if you do not yet have an answer. Then give a specific date for your full reply, usually within three to five working days.

Do I have to stop all collection activity during a dispute?

Pause chasing the disputed amount only. Any undisputed part of the invoice is still due, so ask the customer to pay it on time while you resolve the rest.

When should I issue a credit note instead of a new invoice?

When the original invoice has already been sent and booked, a credit note cancels or reduces it and keeps your records clean. You can then issue a corrected invoice for the right amount.

What if the customer disputes an invoice just to delay paying?

Stay polite and ask for the specific issue in writing. Genuine disputes can be explained; delay tactics usually cannot. If nothing concrete comes back, restate the due date and escalate through your normal process.

How can I reduce future invoice disputes?

Invoice promptly, use clear descriptions, include the customer's PO or reference numbers and keep quotes precise. Track the reasons for each dispute so you can fix the cause.
