What Is a Kaizen Event and How Do You Run One Step by Step?

A kaizen event is a short, focused workshop where a cross-functional team improves one process. Learn when to use one, the roles and how to run it step by step.

November 26, 2026 · 6 min read

Most teams know that a process is slow or error-prone long before anyone fixes it, and improvement ideas pile up in meetings without ever being tried. A kaizen event is a way to break that pattern: a small team clears its calendar for a few days, focuses on one process and leaves with a better way of working already in place.

What is a kaizen event?

A kaizen event, also called a kaizen blitz or rapid improvement event, is a short, structured workshop in which a cross-functional team analyses and improves one clearly scoped process. It usually lasts two to five days. The team studies how the work is really done, identifies waste and root causes, tests changes on the spot and documents the new standard before the event ends.

The word kaizen is Japanese and is usually translated as "change for the better" or "continuous improvement". It was popularised in the West by Masaaki Imai's 1986 book on the subject. The intensive event format took shape later, mainly in the 1990s, as companies outside Japan adapted Toyota-style improvement practices. It started in manufacturing, but it works just as well for offices, service teams and software companies.

How is a kaizen event different from everyday kaizen?

Kaizen as a philosophy means everyone makes small improvements every day, often without any special meeting. A kaizen event is the opposite in rhythm: concentrated, time-boxed and aimed at a bigger step change in one area. The two work best together. Events deliver a visible jump in performance, and daily kaizen keeps the gains and builds on them.

When should you run a kaizen event, and when not?

A kaizen event is a good fit when:

  • the problem sits in one process with a clear start and end point;
  • the people who do the work can be freed up for a few days;
  • you can measure the process today, for example cycle time, errors or backlog;
  • the solution is likely to come from changing how work is done, not from buying a new system.

It is a poor fit when the root cause is unknown and needs weeks of data analysis, when the fix depends on a large IT project or budget approval, or when the problem is really a strategy or staffing decision.

Who takes part in a kaizen event?

  • Sponsor. A manager who chooses the problem, removes obstacles and approves changes during the event.
  • Facilitator. Keeps the team on schedule, guides the methods and makes sure every voice is heard. Ideally not the process owner.
  • Process owner. The person responsible for the process day to day, who will own the new standard afterwards.
  • Team. Usually five to eight people: those who do the work, someone from the upstream and downstream steps, and one or two fresh eyes from outside the area.

How do you run a kaizen event step by step?

Before the event

  • Pick and scope the problem. Choose one process and define where it starts and ends. "Improve customer service" is too big; "cut the time from signed contract to first login" is workable.
  • Measure the baseline. Collect current figures for lead time, error rate, rework or whatever matters most. Without a baseline you cannot prove anything changed.
  • Write a charter. One page with the scope, target, team, dates, constraints and what the team may change without further approval.

During the event

  • Map the current state. Draw every step, handoff and wait on a wall or whiteboard.
  • Go and see. Watch the work where it happens and follow a real order or request. The map on the wall and reality rarely match.
  • Find waste and root causes. Look for waiting, rework, duplicate data entry, unnecessary approvals and searching. Ask "why" until you reach causes you can act on.
  • Brainstorm and test countermeasures. Generate ideas, pick the most promising and try them on real work the same day.
  • Standardise. Write the new method as a simple standard work sheet or checklist and train the people involved.

After the event

  • Keep a 30-day follow-up list. Every action not finished during the event gets an owner and a due date.
  • Measure the results. Compare the same metrics against the baseline after two, four and eight weeks.
  • Sustain. The process owner checks the standard regularly and the sponsor reviews progress until the new way is simply how work is done.

What does a kaizen event look like in practice?

A small software company found that new customers waited an average of 12 working days between signing and going live. A four-day event brought together sales, onboarding, support and finance. Walking through three real accounts showed that customer details were typed into three different tools, kickoff calls waited for a manager's approval, and technical setup only began after the first invoice was paid. The team created a single intake form, let onboarding schedule kickoffs directly and started setup in parallel with invoicing. Tested on the next five customers, lead time fell to around six days. Two remaining actions, an automated welcome email and an updated contract template, went on the 30-day list.

What are common kaizen event mistakes?

  • Scope too big. Trying to fix a whole department in a week leads to vague ideas and no finished changes.
  • No baseline. Without before figures, results become opinions and support fades.
  • No follow-through. The energy of the event disappears if open actions have no owner or due date.
  • Ideas without testing. A list of suggestions is not a kaizen event. Changes should be tried on real work before the team leaves the room.

Run well, a kaizen event gives a team a quick, visible win and a repeatable way to tackle the next problem.

Frequently asked questions

How long does a kaizen event last?

Most kaizen events last two to five days. Smaller teams sometimes split the work into shorter sessions, but the preparation and follow-up always take longer than the event itself.

What is the difference between kaizen and a kaizen event?

Kaizen is the ongoing habit of making small improvements every day. A kaizen event is a short, intensive workshop focused on improving one scoped process in a few days.

Who should be on a kaizen event team?

Usually five to eight people: those who do the work, representatives of the steps before and after, the process owner and one or two outsiders who bring a fresh view.

Can kaizen events work outside manufacturing?

Yes. Offices, service firms, healthcare and software teams use them to improve processes such as customer onboarding, invoicing, hiring and order fulfilment.
