How Should You Handle Employment Reference Requests?

A Q&A for small-business owners on employment references: what to disclose, why consent and one point of contact matter, and what to avoid saying.

October 19, 2026 · 6 min read

Sooner or later the call or email arrives: “We are considering one of your former employees. Could you tell us what she was like to work with?” In a small business with no HR department, it is easy to answer on the spot, warmly and candidly, and just as easy to regret it later. This Q&A explains how to handle reference requests calmly and consistently. It is general guidance, not legal advice. Rules differ by country and state, so check local requirements or ask an employment lawyer about anything unusual.

Why do you need a reference policy at all?

Without one, every manager answers differently. One gives a glowing review of a friend, another says nothing about someone who struggled, and a third shares details that were never meant to leave the building. Inconsistency is what creates risk: a former employee who learns that others received warmer references can fairly ask why. A short written policy makes the answer the same every time, and it protects both the business and the person being discussed.

What can you safely disclose?

The safest default is a neutral reference limited to facts you can verify from your records:

  • Dates of employment. The start and end date.
  • Job title. And, if helpful, the main duties in a sentence or two.
  • Eligibility for rehire. Optional. A plain “yes” or “no” can work if you apply it to everyone, but many businesses leave it out because a “no” invites questions.

Pay, reasons for leaving, performance ratings, disciplinary history, health, family circumstances and anything personal should stay out unless the former employee has asked you to share it and the law allows it. When in doubt, say less.

Do you need written consent?

For anything beyond dates and title, yes, get it in writing. A short signed note or an email from the former employee is enough, as long as it says who may ask, what you may share and, ideally, for how long. Some employers collect consent in the exit paperwork so it is already on file. Without consent, confirm only the basics, and in some places even that is best treated carefully, so follow your local rules.

Who should answer reference requests?

Choose a single point of contact, such as the owner, an HR lead or the office manager, and tell the whole team. Everyone else follows one rule: do not answer, take a name, company and phone number, and pass it on. This stops well-meant chats at the coffee machine, informal replies on messaging apps and social-media comments. Before sharing anything, confirm the caller is genuine by calling back through the company's main number instead of the one they gave you.

What should you avoid saying?

  • Opinions and gossip. “Difficult”, “lazy” or “a great culture fit” are judgements, not facts.
  • Speculation. Never guess why someone left or what they might do next.
  • Anything you cannot back up. If it is not in a document, a record or a reviewed appraisal, leave it out.
  • Protected or private details. Health, pregnancy, age, religion, complaints or legal disputes.
  • Off-the-record remarks. There is no such thing. Anything said can be repeated.

The legal risk is usually defamation, meaning a false statement of fact that damages someone's reputation, though a reference given honestly and without malice is often better protected. Inflated praise is risky too: if you hide serious problems and the next employer is harmed, you may face questions. Sticking to verified facts avoids both problems.

How do you give a good reference to someone leaving on good terms?

Being neutral by default does not mean being cold. When a valued employee departs, you can offer a signed letter of reference that is accurate and specific: dates, title, responsibilities and two or three concrete strengths, such as “managed our supplier relationships and cut delivery delays by a third”. Agree on the wording with the employee before they leave, give them copies, and note in the letter whether it speaks for the company or for the writer personally. A letter in hand also reduces the number of calls you receive later.

What should you document?

Keep a simple log for every request: the date, who asked and for which company, whether consent was on file, what was shared and who answered. Store it with the employee's HR records, limit who can see it, and delete it according to your retention rules. If a dispute ever arises, a clear record of a consistent process is your best protection.

A neutral policy, written consent, one voice and a short log: that is all most small businesses need to handle references fairly and calmly.

Frequently asked questions

Are we legally required to give a reference?

In many places no, unless a contract or local law says otherwise. Some countries do require a basic employment certificate. Check local rules, and whichever approach you choose, apply it to everyone.

Can we refuse to give a reference?

Usually yes, and a policy of confirming only dates and job title is common and widely accepted. Just be consistent and avoid singling out one person.

What if a former employee left on bad terms?

Stick to the same neutral facts you would give anyone: dates and title. Do not add warnings or hints. If you are unsure, take advice before replying.

Can a manager give a personal reference?

Only if your policy allows it. If so, they should make clear it is a personal view, not the company's, and stick to what they directly saw. Many small businesses simply route everything through one contact.
