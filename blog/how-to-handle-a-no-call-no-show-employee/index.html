How Do You Handle an Employee Who Doesn't Show Up or Call?

When an employee fails to show up and does not call, stay calm, check on their safety, document everything and follow your policy. Here is a step-by-step guide.

November 10, 2026 · 6 min read

An empty desk with no message is stressful for any manager. Shifts need covering, customers are waiting, and part of you wonders whether the person is simply careless or in real trouble. A no-call, no-show is one of the few HR situations that needs a quick response, but not a rushed judgment. This guide covers what to do in the first hours, how to follow up, and how to handle the conversation fairly. It is general guidance, not legal advice, and the rules differ between countries and contracts.

What counts as a no-call, no-show?

A no-call, no-show means an employee misses a scheduled shift or workday without telling anyone beforehand and without contacting you afterwards in a reasonable time. It is different from calling in late, or from sending a message that an employer simply dislikes. Your own policy defines the details: who must be told, by when and through which channel. If the policy does not say, you will struggle to treat the matter consistently, so fix that first.

What should you do on the first day?

Resist the urge to assume the worst about the person. Accidents, hospital stays, family emergencies and transport failures all happen, and a sudden silence from a reliable employee is often a sign of something serious.

  • Confirm the absence. Check the schedule, leave requests and any shift swaps. Ask colleagues whether they heard anything.
  • Arrange cover. Protect the team and customers first, and keep this separate from any decision about the employee.
  • Make the first contact attempt. Call, then send a short, neutral message asking them to get in touch.
  • Record what you did. Write down times, methods and responses.

How do you reach out without making it worse?

Keep the tone caring and factual. A message such as "We missed you at work today and want to make sure you are okay. Please call me as soon as you can" works far better than a threat. Try more than one channel: phone, text message and email. If the person has an emergency contact on file, you may use it, but share only what is needed, namely that you are trying to reach them, not details about their job or conduct. If you are seriously worried about someone's safety, especially after repeated silence, local services can carry out a welfare check.

When does it become job abandonment?

Many employers treat several consecutive no-call, no-show days as job abandonment, meaning the employee has effectively left the role. Some policies say three days, others differ, and legal systems vary a great deal. In some places an employer must still send a formal written notice or follow a disciplinary process before ending employment. Before you treat anyone as having resigned, do four things.

  1. Check your policy and the employment contract.
  2. Confirm you have tried to contact the person several times and in several ways.
  3. Send a written letter or email setting out the dates missed, asking them to respond by a clear deadline and explaining what happens otherwise.
  4. Check local employment law, or ask an adviser, before ending employment.

What should the conversation cover when they return?

Hold it privately and soon, and begin by listening. Ask what happened and why they did not make contact. Then cover the following:

  • The facts. State the dates missed and the effect on the team.
  • The reason. Genuine emergencies, illness or safety issues call for support and understanding, and you may need to follow different processes for protected leave or medical absence.
  • The expectation. Explain again how and when to report absence, and why it matters.
  • The outcome. Depending on the circumstances and your policy, this might be no action, a documented reminder, a formal warning or a disciplinary process.

Write a short summary afterwards and share it with the employee. If the reason was a personal crisis, ask whether the person needs time off, flexible hours or access to support services.

What mistakes should you avoid?

  • Deciding in anger. Wait until you have the facts. Ending employment on the second day without a process can create legal and reputational risk.
  • Treating people differently. If one person receives a warning and another does not for the same behaviour, you invite complaints of unfairness.
  • Skipping the paper trail. Without dates, messages and notes, you cannot show that you acted reasonably.
  • Ignoring a pattern. A first incident may call for a conversation, while repeated ones need a clear, formal response.
  • Forgetting the rest of the team. Colleagues who cover need to know the situation is being handled, even if you cannot share the details.

How can you prevent it?

Make the reporting rule simple and visible. Explain it during onboarding, repeat it in the handbook, and give managers one number or channel to use. Keep emergency contacts current, and make it easy for staff to ask for time off, so they do not simply disappear. A team that trusts its manager is more likely to call, even on a bad day.

Check on the person first, document carefully and apply your rules the same way every time. A calm, fair response protects both the business and the person who failed to show up.

Frequently asked questions

How many days of no-call, no-show count as job abandonment?

There is no universal number. Many policies use three consecutive working days, but your contract, policy and local law decide. Always try to make contact and send written notice first.

Should I fire someone after one no-call, no-show?

Not automatically. First establish what happened. Depending on your policy and local rules, a first incident may lead to a conversation or warning, while ending employment needs a fair process.

What if the employee says there was an emergency?

Listen and ask for reasonable evidence if your policy allows it. Genuine emergencies and medical situations usually call for support, and some may be protected under local rules.

Can I contact the employee's emergency contact?

Usually yes, when you cannot reach the person and are concerned. Share only what is needed to reach them and avoid details about their job performance or conduct.
