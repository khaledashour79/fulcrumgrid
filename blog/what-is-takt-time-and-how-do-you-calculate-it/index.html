What Is Takt Time and How Do You Calculate It? (With Example)

Takt time is the pace you must work at to meet demand. Learn the formula, see a worked example, and how it differs from cycle time, lead time and throughput.

November 19, 2026 · 6 min read

Every operation has a heartbeat, whether anyone measures it or not. Customers order at a certain rate, and the team has to finish work at least that fast or a backlog builds. Takt time puts a number on that heartbeat. This guide explains what it is, how to calculate it and how small teams can use it every day.

What is takt time?

Takt time is the pace at which you need to complete one unit of work to meet customer demand exactly. The word comes from the German Takt, meaning beat or rhythm, like the beat a conductor sets for an orchestra. It is not a measure of how fast you work; it is a measure of how fast you need to work. Demand sets it, not the team.

How do you calculate takt time?

The formula is simple:

Takt time = net available working time ÷ customer demand in that period

Both figures must cover the same period, usually one shift or one day. Net available time is the time the team can actually work: scheduled hours minus breaks, meetings, planned maintenance and shift handovers.

A worked example

A small warehouse runs one eight-hour shift. Each packer takes a 30-minute lunch, two 10-minute breaks and a 10-minute start-of-shift briefing. Net available time is 480 − 30 − 20 − 10 = 420 minutes. The team needs to ship 280 orders a day.

Takt time = 420 minutes ÷ 280 orders = 1.5 minutes, or one order every 90 seconds.

Now compare that with the work. If packing one order takes a single packer 4 minutes, one person can only finish one order every 4 minutes. Divide the work content by takt time to find how many people you need: 4 ÷ 1.5 = 2.67, so three packers working in parallel. With only two, the backlog grows by around 70 orders every day.

Takt time vs cycle time vs lead time vs throughput

These terms are often used interchangeably, but each answers a different question.

  • Takt time. How often must we finish one unit to keep up with demand? It is a target set by the customer.
  • Cycle time. How long does it actually take to complete one unit? It is what your process does today.
  • Lead time. How long does a customer wait from request to delivery, including time spent in queues?
  • Throughput. How many units are actually finished per period?

In short, takt time is the required pace, while cycle time and throughput tell you whether you are hitting it.

What should you do when cycle time is longer than takt time?

If a step takes longer than takt time, it becomes a bottleneck and work piles up in front of it. You have three broad options, best tried in this order:

  • Remove waste. Walk the process and cut steps that add no value: searching for materials, re-entering data, waiting for approvals, unnecessary movement.
  • Balance the work. Move tasks from the overloaded step to steps with spare time, so every station finishes close to takt.
  • Add capacity. Put an extra person or machine on the constrained step, or run it in parallel, as in the packing example.

The opposite case matters too. If cycle time is far below takt, you may be overstaffed, or tempted to produce work nobody has asked for yet.

Can you use takt time outside manufacturing?

Yes. Any team that handles a steady stream of similar, repeatable requests has a takt time.

  • Warehouses. Orders picked or packed per shift.
  • Kitchens. If a restaurant serves 120 dishes during a two-hour lunch rush, the kitchen needs to plate one dish every minute.
  • Service desks. If a support team has 6 net hours a day and receives 72 tickets, takt time is 5 minutes per ticket.

The work does not need to be identical. It only needs to be similar enough that an average unit means something. Where it is not, group work into types and calculate takt for each.

What are the most common mistakes?

  • Using gross instead of net time. Dividing the full eight-hour shift by demand makes takt look easier than it is. In the example above, 480 minutes gives about 103 seconds instead of 90, hiding a gap of roughly 14 percent.
  • Ignoring demand variation. Demand rarely arrives evenly. A Monday peak or a seasonal rush can halve takt time. Calculate takt for peak periods as well as averages, and plan staffing for both.
  • Treating takt as a speed target for people. Takt describes the rhythm the system needs. Pushing individuals to work faster usually raises errors and rework.
  • Forgetting downtime and quality losses. If some units need rework or equipment is down for an hour a week, real capacity is lower than the plan assumes. Aim for cycle times comfortably below takt, often 85 to 90 percent of it, to leave a buffer.

How do you monitor takt time daily?

Takt time is most useful when it becomes a visible daily rhythm rather than a number in a spreadsheet.

  • Recalculate when demand changes. Update takt each day or week from the actual order forecast.
  • Turn it into hourly targets. A 90-second takt means 40 orders per hour. Post the planned and actual count on a board every hour.
  • Record the reasons for gaps. When an hour falls short, note why in a few words. Patterns in those notes show where to improve.
  • Review the trend weekly. Look at how often you met the plan, how peak days went and the top causes of misses.

A shared dashboard helps here. A tool such as FulcrumGrid Command Center can put demand, output and the gap against plan in one view, so the team reviews the same numbers at its daily huddle instead of rebuilding them by hand.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good takt time?

There is no good or bad takt time, because demand and available time set it. What matters is that the cycle time of each step sits at or slightly below it.

Is takt time the same as cycle time?

No. Takt time is the pace demand requires, while cycle time is how long your process actually takes per unit. Comparing the two shows whether you can keep up.

How often should I recalculate takt time?

Whenever demand or available hours change noticeably. Many small teams recalculate weekly from the forecast and check peak days separately.

What unit should takt time be expressed in?

Use whatever fits the work: seconds per unit on a line, minutes per order in a warehouse or minutes per ticket on a service desk. Use the same unit when comparing it with cycle time.
