What Questions Should You Ask in an Exit Interview?

An exit interview is your last honest conversation with a departing employee. Learn when to hold it, who should run it, which questions to ask and how to act on the answers.

October 4, 2026 · 6 min read

When someone resigns, they take their knowledge with them, but they also take something just as valuable: an honest view of how your company really works. An exit interview is the structured conversation that lets you keep that view. Done well, it shows you why people leave, what they would change and what you should protect. Done badly, it is a form that nobody reads. This guide covers the questions people ask most, from timing to the questions themselves.

What is the purpose of an exit interview?

The goal is learning, not persuasion. You are not trying to win the person back or defend past decisions. You want candid information about the role, the manager, the team and the organization, plus a smooth, respectful handover. A good exit interview also leaves the person with a positive last impression, and that matters because former employees become referrals, customers and sometimes returning colleagues.

When should you hold it, and who should run it?

Hold the conversation in the final week, once the notice period is nearly over and the person is relaxed enough to be frank but still engaged. Avoid the last day, when everyone is distracted. Some companies add a short follow-up survey two or three months later, when people can look back with more distance.

The interviewer should not be the departing person’s direct manager, because that is exactly who employees find hardest to criticize. An HR representative or a trusted leader from another team works best. Give the conversation 30 to 45 minutes in a private room or a quiet video call, and tell the employee beforehand what it is for and that taking part is voluntary.

Which questions should you ask?

Group your questions by theme so the conversation flows and you can compare answers across people later. Pick two or three from each group rather than reading a long script.

The decision to leave

  • What first made you start thinking about leaving?
  • What finally made you decide to go?
  • Was there anything we could have done to change your mind?

The role and the work

  • How well did the job match what you expected when you joined?
  • Did you have the tools, information and time you needed?
  • What part of the role did you enjoy most, and what least?

Manager and leadership

  • Did you receive regular, useful feedback and recognition?
  • How supported did you feel in your goals and your workload?
  • What could your manager do differently to help the next person?

Team and culture

  • How would you describe the culture to a friend?
  • Did you feel included and able to speak up?
  • Were there any conflicts or behaviors we should know about?

Pay, growth & development

  • Did you see a realistic path for your career here?
  • How did your pay and benefits compare with what you could find elsewhere?
  • What training or opportunities would have kept you?

Looking ahead

  • What should we definitely not change?
  • What is the one thing you would fix first?
  • Would you recommend us as an employer, and would you consider coming back?

How do you handle the feedback you hear?

  • Listen, do not defend. If you argue with a comment, people stop sharing. Say thank you and ask a follow-up question.
  • Ask for examples. “The culture is bad” is hard to act on. “Decisions are made in meetings I was never invited to” is specific.
  • Separate facts from feelings. Both are useful, but they lead to different actions.
  • Take notes, then write them up. Record the main points the same day, while they are fresh.
  • Expect some venting. One angry comment is a data point, not a verdict.

What about confidentiality?

Be clear from the start about what happens to the answers. The most common approach is to keep individual comments confidential and share only themes, grouped across several people and stripped of names. If you will pass something on, for example because it describes harassment or a legal risk, say so before it comes up. Promise only what you can keep, because a broken promise destroys trust in the process for everyone still working for you.

How do you act on patterns?

One exit interview is an anecdote. Ten are evidence. Store the answers in a consistent format, with the same core questions each time, and review them every quarter. Look for repeats by team, manager, tenure and role. If three people from the same department mention unclear career paths, that is a finding, not a coincidence.

Then close the loop. Choose one or two issues, name an owner, set a date and tell the company what changed. Nothing makes employees more cynical than feedback that disappears, and nothing builds trust faster than visible follow-through.

Ask the right questions, listen without defending, protect confidentiality and act on what repeats. That is how a routine formality becomes one of your best sources of insight.

Frequently asked questions

Is an exit interview mandatory?

No. In most places it is voluntary, and it should be presented that way. People are far more candid when they choose to take part, so explain the purpose and let them decline.

Should an exit interview be written or spoken?

Both have value. A conversation produces richer detail and lets you ask follow-ups, while a short written survey is easier to compare across people. Many teams use a conversation plus a brief form.

How long should an exit interview take?

Plan for 30 to 45 minutes. That is long enough to go beyond polite answers and short enough that the person does not feel held back from handing over their work.

What if the employee will not say anything useful?

Do not push. Ask open questions such as what you should keep and what you should fix, accept short answers, and consider a follow-up survey a few months later when feelings have settled.
