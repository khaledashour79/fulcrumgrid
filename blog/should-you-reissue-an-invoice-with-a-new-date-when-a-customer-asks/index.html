Should You Reissue an Invoice With a New Date When Asked?

A customer asks for a re-dated invoice. Learn why it resets terms and aging, the audit risks, safer alternatives, when reissuing is right and what to reply.

November 27, 2026 · 6 min read

“Could you send us a new invoice with today’s date?” It sounds like a small favour, and saying yes feels like good customer service. But re-dating an invoice quietly hands your customer a fresh set of payment terms, makes your receivables look healthier than they are and can leave a messy trail for your accountant or auditor. In most cases the right answer is a polite no, paired with something that genuinely helps the customer pay.

Why do customers ask for a re-dated invoice?

  • The original went missing. The invoice was lost in an inbox or never reached accounts payable, and the customer assumes a new one is needed.
  • They missed an approval window. Some companies only approve invoices within a set number of days of the invoice date, and an older invoice gets flagged or parked.
  • Month-end or year-end cutoffs. The customer wants the cost to fall in a different period or budget, or their books for the month are already closed.
  • Purchase order problems. The PO was raised after the invoice, expired, or ran out of budget, so their system rejects an invoice dated before it.
  • Buying time. A new date usually means a new 30- or 60-day term. For a customer short on cash, that is an easy request to make.

What actually happens when you change the invoice date?

Payment terms run from the invoice date. Move the date forward by five weeks and the due date moves with it, so an invoice that was already overdue suddenly becomes current. That has three knock-on effects:

  • Your aging report stops telling the truth. Money that has been outstanding for 70 days appears as 10 days old, which hides slow payers and weakens your follow-up.
  • DSO looks better than it is. Days sales outstanding falls on paper while your cash position stays the same, so decisions about credit limits and cash planning are based on flattering numbers.
  • Late fees and interest lose their footing. If you later want to charge interest or escalate, the customer can point to a due date that has not yet passed.

Is it a problem for audit and tax?

It can be. Once an invoice is issued, it is part of your accounting record and, in many countries, a tax document. Editing the date on an issued invoice, or issuing a second invoice for the same sale, can create a gap or duplicate in your numbering, shift revenue or VAT into the wrong period and make it look as though records were altered after the fact. Rules vary by country, so check with your accountant, but the general principle is consistent: you do not change an issued invoice; you correct it with a credit note and, if needed, a new invoice. That way both documents and the reason for the change stay visible.

What can you offer instead?

  • Resend the original as a copy. Send the same invoice number and date, clearly marked “Copy” or “Reprint”, with a payment link. This solves lost invoices without creating a new document.
  • Send a statement of account. A statement lists every open invoice with its original date and due date, which often satisfies an AP team that needs to see the full picture.
  • Keep the original due date, but agree a payment date. If the customer genuinely needs a little time, agree a short promise-to-pay date in writing, for example within seven days, and record it. The invoice stays overdue in your books, which is accurate.
  • Help with the approval problem directly. Offer a short confirmation letter, add the missing PO reference to a cover email, or upload the copy to their supplier portal.

When is reissuing legitimate?

Reissuing is the right move when the original invoice is genuinely wrong: the amount or quantity is incorrect, the PO number is missing or wrong, the invoice is addressed to the wrong legal entity, or tax details need correcting. In those cases, issue a credit note that cancels the original in full, then a new invoice with the correct details. Note the original invoice number on both. Whether the new invoice carries today’s date depends on your local rules, but the payment terms you agree should reflect when the customer first had what they needed to pay, not reset automatically.

How should you reply to the customer?

Be helpful, specific and firm about the date. For example:

“Thanks for getting in touch. We are not able to change the date on an issued invoice, but I have attached a copy of invoice 2087 for $6,250, dated 3 October, together with a statement of your account and a link to pay online. The original due date was 2 November. If you need a PO reference added or a portal upload, tell me and I will sort it today. Could you confirm a payment date? If 4 December works for you, I will note it on our side.”

If the customer points to a real error, switch to the credit note route and tell them exactly what you will send and when.

The bottom line

A request for a new invoice date is usually a request for more time or a workaround for a process problem. Solve the process problem, agree a firm payment date if needed and keep the original date intact. Your aging report stays honest, your records stay clean and your customer still gets what they need to pay.

Frequently asked questions

Should I change the date on an invoice if the customer asks?

Usually not. Changing the date resets the payment terms and makes the debt look newer than it is. Resend the original as a copy and agree a payment date instead.

Does reissuing an invoice restart the payment terms?

In practice, yes. Terms run from the invoice date, so a new date gives the customer a new due date. That is why reissuing should be reserved for genuine errors.

How do I correct an invoice that has already been issued?

Issue a credit note that cancels the original invoice, then a new invoice with the correct details, referencing the original number on both. Check local tax rules with your accountant.

What is the difference between a copy and a new invoice?

A copy is the same document with the same number and date, marked “Copy” or “Reprint”. A new invoice is a separate document with its own number, which can cause duplicate payments or record gaps.
