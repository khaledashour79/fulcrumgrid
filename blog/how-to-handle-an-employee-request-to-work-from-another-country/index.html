How Should You Handle an Employee's Request to Work From Another Country?

Working from abroad can create tax, immigration, social security and data risks. Here is a practical framework for answering the request fairly and safely.

November 2, 2026 · 6 min read

An employee asks to spend three months working from their parents’ home abroad, or to relocate permanently while keeping the job. It sounds like a simple scheduling favor, but it can change which country’s tax, employment and immigration rules apply to you and to them. The goal is not to say no by reflex or yes by default. It is to make an informed, documented decision. This guide is general information, not legal or tax advice.

Why is this more than a normal remote work request?

Because the law follows the person’s physical location, not the company’s address. A few weeks of laptop work in another country can be harmless, or it can trigger obligations in that country that nobody planned for. The risk depends on the country, the length of stay, what the employee does and how senior they are.

What tax risks should you check?

Two questions matter. First, the employee’s personal tax residence: many countries tax people who stay beyond a threshold, often around 183 days, and some have lower thresholds or other tests. Payroll withholding may need to be set up in the new country. Second, corporate exposure. If the employee signs contracts, manages a team or works as your regular presence there, the company may be treated as having a permanent establishment, which can make part of your profits taxable locally and bring registration and filing duties. Tax treaties can help but do not cover every case.

Does the employee have the right to work there?

Being allowed to visit is not the same as being allowed to work. Many tourist visas and visa-free stays do not permit remote work for a foreign employer, and penalties can include fines, deportation and future entry bans for the employee. Some countries offer digital nomad or remote work visas with income requirements and fixed durations. Ask the employee to confirm their status in writing, and never encourage them to “just stay on a tourist visa.”

What about social security and benefits?

Social security contributions usually follow where the work is done. Bilateral or regional agreements may allow the employee to stay in the home system for a limited period, often with a certificate, but without such an agreement you could owe contributions in two countries. Also check health insurance, pension accrual and occupational accident cover, since your current policies may not apply abroad.

Which employment law points apply?

Some mandatory local rules, such as minimum notice, working time, leave, and termination protections, can apply to employees working in a country even if their contract is governed by another law. Check that the contract names a place of work, that any change is agreed in writing, and that you are not unintentionally creating new rights. A local employment lawyer can say quickly whether this is a concern.

What data and security risks come with it?

Privacy and export rules can restrict where personal data is accessed or stored, and some industries, clients or contracts require data to stay in specific regions. Public networks, shared housing and border device inspections raise the risk. Require a company-managed device, a VPN, multi-factor authentication and encrypted storage, and check client contracts before approving access from abroad.

A decision framework you can reuse

  • 1. Clarify the request. Which country, which dates, why, and whether it is temporary or permanent.
  • 2. Assess the role. Does it involve signing deals, sales, managing local staff or regulated data? Those raise the risk.
  • 3. Check the legal basics. Work authorization, tax residence, social security and any permanent establishment concern.
  • 4. Estimate the cost. Advisers, payroll setup, insurance and extra tools against the value of keeping the person.
  • 5. Decide and document. Approve, approve with conditions, or decline, giving reasons that apply equally to similar requests.
  • 6. Review the outcome. Confirm the arrangement ended or was formalized as agreed.

Should you start with a time-limited trial?

For short stays, a written trial is often the safest route. Set a clear end date, well under the local tax residence threshold, name the exact country and keep the same hours and duties. Require the employee to confirm their right to work, state that the company can end the arrangement earlier, and record that it creates no right to a longer stay. Any extension needs a new review.

When should you get professional advice?

Before approving anything beyond a short, low-risk stay, speak to a tax adviser and an employment or immigration lawyer in the destination country. The cost is usually small compared with back taxes, penalties or a forced exit. For permanent relocation, you may need to hire through a local entity or an employer-of-record service instead.

What should your policy say?

  • Approval. Working abroad needs prior written approval from a named person.
  • Limits. Maximum days per year, list of approved countries and excluded roles.
  • Employee duties. Confirm work authorization, follow security rules and report changes of plan.
  • Costs. Who pays for advice, extra taxes, insurance and equipment.
  • Right to withdraw. Ending the arrangement on notice if risks change.

Frequently asked questions

Can an employee work from abroad on a tourist visa?

Usually not. Many tourist visas and visa-free stays do not allow remote work for a foreign employer, and breaches can lead to fines or entry bans. Ask for written confirmation of their status and take local advice.

How many days abroad are safe?

There is no universal number. Many countries use a threshold around 183 days for tax residence, but some use lower limits or other tests, and work authorization and social security rules differ. Check the specific country before agreeing.

What is a permanent establishment?

It is a taxable presence of your company in another country, which can arise if an employee works there regularly, signs contracts or acts as your representative. It can trigger local corporate tax and filing duties, so ask a tax adviser.

Can we just say no?

Usually yes, if you apply the same criteria to similar requests and do not discriminate. Explain the legal and security reasons, and consider a short approved trial or an alternative such as extra leave.

Do we need a policy before the first request?

It helps a lot. A short policy covering approval, limits, employee duties and costs makes decisions faster and fairer, and gives you something to point to when you have to decline.
