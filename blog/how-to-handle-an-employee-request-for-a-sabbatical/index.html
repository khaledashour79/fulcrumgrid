How Should You Handle an Employee's Request for a Sabbatical?

A small business guide to sabbatical requests: paid or unpaid, how to decide, what to put in writing, covering the work and welcoming the employee back.

November 16, 2026 · 6 min read

A valued employee asks for three months off to travel, study, write a book or simply recharge. For a small business, the first reaction is often panic: who will do the work? But a sabbatical request is usually a sign of something good. The person wants a break, not an exit, and they are asking you rather than resigning. Handled well, a sabbatical can keep a strong employee for years. Handled badly, it can lose them, or leave a gap you never planned for.

Important: unlike most types of leave, sabbaticals are rarely a legal right, but the law still matters. Rules on benefits, pension contributions, health cover, job protection and continuity of employment differ between countries and regions, so check the position where you operate before you agree terms.

What is a sabbatical, and how is it different from a leave of absence?

A sabbatical is an extended, planned break from work, typically one to twelve months, taken for personal growth, study, travel, volunteering or rest. A general leave of absence usually covers a need: illness, a new child, caring for a relative. The key differences are:

  • Purpose. A sabbatical is chosen, not required by circumstances.
  • Planning. It is normally requested months in advance, which gives you time to prepare.
  • Legal status. It is usually discretionary, granted under your own policy rather than a statutory entitlement.

Should a sabbatical be paid or unpaid?

Most small businesses offer unpaid sabbaticals, and that is perfectly reasonable. Some offer partial pay, or full pay after a long service milestone, such as four weeks paid after seven years. Another option is a self-funded sabbatical, where the employee takes a slightly reduced salary for a period beforehand and receives it as income during the break. Whatever you choose, decide in advance what happens to benefits, holiday accrual and pension contributions, because these are often where disputes start.

How do you decide whether to approve it?

Weigh three things honestly:

  • Tenure and track record. Sabbaticals are usually reserved for employees with a few years of service and a solid record.
  • Business impact. Can the role be covered for the period requested? Is the timing compatible with your busy season or a major project?
  • Retention value. What would it cost to replace this person if they left instead? Recruiting and training a replacement often costs far more than a few months of cover.

If the answer is not a clear yes, consider a counter-offer: a shorter break, different dates or a phased start, rather than a flat refusal.

What questions should you ask?

  • How long do you want to be away, and how flexible are the dates?
  • What do you plan to do, and will it involve paid work elsewhere?
  • Do you intend to return to the same role?
  • Would you be willing to be contacted in a genuine emergency?
  • What would help you prepare a good handover?

Ask with curiosity, not suspicion. You are not judging how they spend their time, only planning around it.

How do you plan coverage and the handover?

Start early. List the employee's recurring tasks, key clients, passwords and deadlines, and decide who covers each one. Options include sharing the work among colleagues, hiring a temporary replacement or freelancer, or pausing lower-priority work. Ask the employee to document processes in the weeks before they leave, and arrange a few days of overlap with whoever covers. Thank the people who take on extra work, and recognise it, because a sabbatical should not feel like a punishment for the rest of the team.

What should the written agreement include?

Put everything in writing and have both sides sign it. Cover at least:

  • Dates. Start date, return date and any notice needed to change them.
  • Pay and benefits. What is paid, what is paused and what continues, including health cover and pension.
  • Return role. Whether they return to the same job or an equivalent one, at the same pay.
  • Early return. Whether they may come back early and how much notice they must give.
  • Other work. Whether working for another employer during the break is allowed.
  • Non-return. What happens if they decide not to come back, such as repaying any paid sabbatical salary or the notice period they must give.

How do you stay in touch and manage re-entry?

Agree a light contact plan before they go, perhaps a short update once a month and a direct line for emergencies only. Respect the break: a sabbatical full of work messages is not a sabbatical. Two to four weeks before the return date, confirm that they are still coming back and schedule a welcome meeting. On their first day, explain what changed, update their access and tools, and give them a lighter first week. Ask what they learned. Many people return with fresh ideas and renewed energy.

Should you turn it into a policy?

Yes. Once one employee has taken a sabbatical, others will ask. A short written policy keeps decisions fair and consistent, and protects you from accusations of favouritism. Set out who is eligible, how long the break can be, whether it is paid, how much notice is required and how requests are decided. Publish it, apply it to everyone, and review it once a year.

Frequently asked questions

Is a sabbatical a legal right?

In most places, no. Sabbaticals are usually granted at the employer's discretion under a company policy, although a few countries have specific schemes. Local rules on benefits and job protection still apply, so check where you operate.

How long should a sabbatical be?

Most small business sabbaticals last between one and six months. Longer breaks are possible but harder to cover. Set a maximum in your policy so every request starts from the same baseline.

Can I refuse a sabbatical request?

Usually yes, if it is discretionary, but base the decision on business reasons and your policy, never on personal or protected characteristics. Explain your reasons in writing and offer alternatives where you can.

What if the employee does not come back?

Your written agreement should cover this. It typically sets the notice the employee must give and whether any paid sabbatical salary must be repaid. Without an agreement, your options are far more limited.
