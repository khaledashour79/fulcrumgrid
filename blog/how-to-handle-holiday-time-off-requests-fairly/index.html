How Do You Handle Holiday Time-Off Requests Fairly?

Set request deadlines, pick a fair allocation method, protect minimum cover, respect different holidays and keep records so next year's rotation stays fair.

November 20, 2026 · 6 min read

Every year the same thing happens: a handful of dates around the end-of-year holidays, a school break or a major festival, and almost the whole team asks for them at once. Someone has to stay, and how you decide who will shape morale for months. The good news is that most of the conflict comes from unclear rules, not from the dates themselves. Here is a practical way to handle it.

When should you publish the rules?

Earlier than you think. Two to three months before a peak period, tell everyone which dates are in high demand, how many people can be off on each day, the deadline for requests and how you will decide if there are too many. Requests submitted before the deadline are treated equally; anything after it is handled on whatever capacity is left. A clear deadline stops the quiet race to ask first and gives people time to plan travel.

Which allocation method is fairest?

There is no perfect method, but the trade-offs are clear. Pick one, write it down and apply it consistently.

  • First come, first served. Simple and transparent, but it rewards whoever is quickest and can lock in the same people every year. It works best with a fixed opening date, so nobody books twelve months ahead.
  • Rotation. Whoever had the most popular days last year goes to the back of the queue this year. It is usually seen as the fairest over time, but it only works if you keep records.
  • Seniority. Rewards loyalty and is easy to explain, but newer staff may never get a peak holiday, which can push good people out.
  • Lottery. Neutral and quick, but it can feel arbitrary, and the same person can lose twice in a row. Combining it with rotation fixes that.

Many small businesses use a hybrid: rotation as the main rule, with a lottery to break ties.

How many people do you really need at work?

Set minimum staffing per role, not just per headcount. Three people on shift do not help if nobody present can approve a refund, open the till or run payroll. For each peak day, list the skills that must be covered and how many people each one needs. Expected demand matters too: a shop may need more staff before a holiday, an office far fewer. If the numbers are tight, cross-train someone now, so more people can cover more roles next year.

Should you allow swaps?

Yes, with simple conditions. Let employees trade days among themselves as long as the required skills stay covered and a manager approves the change. Swaps often solve the problem better than any rule, because colleagues know whose dates matter most to whom. Record every swap so it counts in next year's rotation.

How do you respect different religious and cultural holidays?

Not everyone celebrates the same holidays. Your team might include people marking Christmas, Eid, Diwali, Lunar New Year, Passover or Hanukkah, or none at all. Ask early which dates matter most to each person rather than assuming. Someone who works over Christmas may be happy to, if they can take their own festival off in return. Treat requests for religious observance as seriously as any other, avoid scheduling key events on major festivals where you can, and never ask people to justify their faith. In many places, refusing reasonable religious accommodation can also raise discrimination concerns, so check local law.

Is it fair to prioritise parents?

It is tempting, especially around school holidays, but automatic priority for parents tends to breed resentment. People without children have partners, elderly relatives, friends and plans of their own. A better approach is to let everyone name their single most important date and give it equal weight, whatever the reason. Parents can still secure school-holiday dates through rotation and swaps, without a rule that suggests one person's life matters more than another's.

How should you communicate decisions and denials?

Announce all decisions at the same time, soon after the deadline, so nobody hears second-hand. When you have to say no, do it personally, explain which rule applied and offer something: an alternative date, a half day, priority next year or help finding a swap. Keep the tone factual and kind. A refusal that is explained and consistent is accepted far more easily than one that looks like favouritism.

Why keep a record for next year?

Fairness is judged over several years, not one. Keep a simple record of who asked for which dates, who got them, who worked and who swapped. Next year, that history is what makes a rotation credible. Also note any legal rules that apply, such as minimum leave entitlements, carrying over unused days or extra pay for working public holidays; these vary, so check local law. A quick checklist

  • Publish peak dates, limits and the request deadline two to three months ahead.
  • Choose one allocation method and write it down.
  • Set minimum cover by role and skill, not just headcount.
  • Allow swaps that a manager approves.
  • Ask each person which holidays matter most to them.
  • Announce all decisions together and explain every refusal.
  • Record requests, approvals and swaps for next year.

Frequently asked questions

What is the fairest way to allocate holiday time off?

A rotation based on who had the popular dates last year is usually seen as fairest over time, often with a lottery to break ties. Whatever you choose, publish it early and apply it consistently.

Can an employer refuse a holiday time-off request?

In many places employers can refuse or move leave for genuine business reasons, but rules on notice, entitlements and religious accommodation vary. Check local law and explain any refusal clearly.

Should parents get priority during school holidays?

Automatic priority tends to cause resentment. Let everyone name their most important date and treat it equally, then use rotation and swaps so parents get school-holiday dates over time.

How early should employees request holiday time off?

Set a deadline two to three months before each peak period and treat every request submitted before it equally. Requests that arrive later are handled on the capacity that remains.
