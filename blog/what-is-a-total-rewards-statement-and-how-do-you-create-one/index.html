What Is a Total Rewards Statement and How Do You Create One?

A total rewards statement shows an employee the full value of pay, benefits and perks. Learn what to include and how a small business can build one in a day.

November 4, 2026 · 6 min read

Most employees know their salary and little else about what they receive. A total rewards statement fixes that by putting every part of someone’s compensation on a single page. This guide answers the questions small business owners ask most: what it is, what goes in it, and how to build one without a dedicated HR team.

What is a total rewards statement?

A total rewards statement is a personalized document that shows the full value of what an employee gets for working with you. It covers not just base pay but also benefits, paid time off, bonuses, employer contributions and development support. It is sometimes called a total compensation statement. Its purpose is to turn costs that employees never see into a number they can understand.

Why does a small business need one?

Small businesses rarely match large employers on salary, but they often offer flexibility, learning and real benefits that go unnoticed. A statement helps you in four ways:

  • Retention: people who see the real value of their package are less likely to compare it with a salary figure alone.
  • Hiring: you can show candidates the whole offer, not only the headline pay.
  • Fairer conversations: raises and reviews start from complete numbers on both sides.
  • Your own clarity: you see what each person really costs and which benefits are worth keeping.

What should a total rewards statement include?

Cash compensation

Annual base salary (or hourly wage multiplied by expected hours), plus bonuses, commissions and overtime paid in the period.

Benefits the employer pays for

The employer share of health insurance, retirement contributions or matching, life and disability cover, and any other plan you fund. Show the employer portion separately from what the employee pays.

Time off and flexibility

Paid vacation days, public holidays, sick leave and parental leave, plus flexible hours or remote work if you offer them. These can be shown as days, with an estimated value as a note.

Growth and extras

Training budget, courses, equipment, phone or internet allowances, meals, gym memberships and similar perks.

How do you create one, step by step?

  • Step 1: List everything you offer. Walk through payroll, insurance invoices and your employee handbook, and write down each item, even small ones.
  • Step 2: Collect the figures per person. Pull annual amounts from payroll records and benefit statements, and ask providers for the employer share if it is unclear.
  • Step 3: Convert to one period. Use annual amounts throughout so the page adds up without mixing monthly and yearly numbers.
  • Step 4: Handle time off carefully. Paid leave is already inside base pay, so show it as information (for example, 25 paid days, worth roughly annual pay divided by working days, times 25) rather than adding it to the total again.
  • Step 5: Add it up. Show cash, employer-paid benefits and extras as separate blocks with one clear total.
  • Step 6: Keep it to one page. Use plain labels, a simple table and a short note explaining each line.
  • Step 7: Check and share. Have your accountant or payroll provider review the numbers, then hand the statement over in a short one-to-one conversation, not just by email.

What does a simple example look like?

Imagine an employee with a base salary of $42,000, a $2,000 bonus, $3,600 of employer-paid health insurance, a $1,260 retirement match and an $800 training budget. The total rewards figure is $49,660, about 18% above the salary alone. Beneath it you might add a note: “Includes 25 paid vacation days (about $4,000 of your base pay).” The employee now sees roughly $7,660 of value they might otherwise have overlooked.

What mistakes should you avoid?

  • Double counting. Do not add paid time off on top of a salary that already covers it.
  • Presenting estimates as promises. Label bonuses and benefit costs as current figures that may change, and state that the statement is not a contract.
  • Overloading the page. Ten clear lines beat forty confusing ones.
  • Letting it go stale. Refresh it every year and whenever pay or benefits change.
  • Ignoring local rules. Tax treatment and disclosure requirements vary, so confirm details with a qualified adviser. This article is general information, not legal or tax advice.

Do you need software for this?

Not at first. With a handful of employees, a spreadsheet and an hour per person is enough. It becomes tedious when figures live in several places and need rebuilding every year. In that case a tool such as FulcrumGrid HR Suite, which keeps employee records, pay details and time-off balances together, can save you from hunting for the numbers. Use it only if it fits your size, since the statement itself works fine on paper.

What should you do next?

Start with one employee, including yourself if you are on payroll. Build the page, check it with your accountant, and see what questions it raises. Once the format works, repeat it for the team and schedule a yearly refresh alongside your regular reviews.

Frequently asked questions

Is a total rewards statement the same as a payslip?

No. A payslip shows what was paid in one pay period, while a total rewards statement shows the full annual value of pay, benefits, time off and perks, including what the employer pays on top.

How often should I give employees a total rewards statement?

Once a year is a good rhythm, ideally before review season. Also update it when someone joins or when pay or benefits change in a meaningful way.

Should I include employer payroll taxes?

It is optional. Some companies include them to show true cost, others leave them out to keep the page simple. Whichever you choose, say so clearly and ask your accountant.

Is a total rewards statement legally required?

In most places it is not, but pay transparency and disclosure rules differ by country and region. Check local requirements and have your adviser confirm what applies to you.

Can I make one without HR software?

Yes. A spreadsheet or a one-page document works well for a small team. Software becomes useful when the number of employees or data sources makes yearly updates slow.
