How Do You Collect Small Overdue Invoices Without Losing Money?

Small overdue balances can cost more to chase than they are worth. Learn how to price your collection effort, set a threshold and automate the follow-up.

November 14, 2026 · 6 min read

A $40 invoice that is 60 days late is easy to ignore. Twenty of them are not. Small overdue balances quietly add up, and the usual collection routine of personal emails and phone calls can cost more than the money you recover. The answer is not to stop collecting. It is to treat small balances as a separate, low-touch process with clear rules.

Why do small overdue balances pile up?

Most small balances are not the result of a customer refusing to pay. They come from friction:

  • Short-pays. A customer pays $1,195 on a $1,200 invoice because of rounding, a bank fee or an outdated price list.
  • Leftover fees. Late fees, card surcharges or shipping charges added after the main invoice was paid.
  • Tiny invoices. One-off add-ons, small reorders or monthly usage charges billed on their own.
  • Partial credits. A credit note that did not fully cover a disputed line, leaving a remainder nobody owns.

Each one is minor, so nobody prioritises it. Over a year, they can become a meaningful slice of your receivables and a mess in your aging report.

How do you work out your cost to collect per invoice?

Before setting any policy, estimate what one collection attempt actually costs. A simple version:

  • Staff time. Minutes spent per follow-up multiplied by a loaded hourly rate. Ten minutes at $36 an hour is $6 per touch.
  • Number of touches. If a typical overdue invoice needs three reminders and one call, that is four touches.
  • Payment costs. Card or transfer fees on the eventual payment.
  • Tools and postage. Any per-letter or per-message charges.

In this example, four manual touches cost around $24 before fees. Chasing a $15 balance by hand loses money, and chasing a $30 balance barely breaks even. That number is your starting point for a threshold.

How do you set a small-balance policy?

Write down a short policy so decisions are consistent rather than made invoice by invoice:

  • Set a threshold. Balances below a set amount, for example two to three times your cost per manual touch, go into the low-touch track.
  • Define the track. Small balances get automated reminders only. No phone calls, no escalations, unless the customer has several small balances that add up.
  • Set a time limit. Decide how long a small balance stays open before it is bundled, written off or reviewed.
  • Name an owner. One person reviews the small-balance list once a month.

Review the threshold once or twice a year. As automation lowers your cost per touch, you can afford to pursue smaller amounts.

What does a low-touch collection sequence look like?

For small balances, automation does the work. A practical sequence:

  • Day 1 overdue: a friendly email with the amount, the invoice number and a one-click pay link.
  • Day 10: a short text message reminder with the same link.
  • Day 25: a final email offering to add the balance to the next invoice or charge the card on file.

Keep messages short and make paying easier than ignoring. A pay link removes the effort of logging in or finding bank details, and an autopay option stops the same balance from reappearing. Segmenting customers by balance size helps here: a tool such as FulcrumGrid Collection can run reminder sequences automatically and put small balances on a lighter track than large ones, so your team spends its time on the invoices that matter most.

Should you bundle small balances or write them off?

For customers you still work with, bundling is often the cheapest fix. Add the leftover amount to the next invoice as a clearly labelled line, or send a monthly statement that lists every open item. The customer pays everything in one go, and you avoid a separate collection effort.

Writing off makes sense when the customer is no longer active, the balance is below your threshold and reminders have gone unanswered, or the amount comes from a genuine misunderstanding you would rather absorb. Keep chasing when the customer is active, the amount is near or above your threshold, or a pattern of short-pays suggests a process issue on their side.

On the accounting side, written-off amounts are usually recorded as bad debt or a small-balance adjustment, and in many places bad debts can be deductible if certain conditions are met. Rules vary by country and by accounting method, so agree a consistent approach with your accountant and document each write-off.

How do you stop small balances from happening?

Prevention is cheaper than any collection process:

  • Set a minimum invoice amount. Combine small charges into a monthly invoice instead of billing each one separately.
  • Take deposits. For project work, collect part of the fee upfront so the final invoice is smaller and less likely to be short-paid.
  • Keep a card on file. With the customer's consent, charge small amounts and leftover fees automatically.
  • Fix the root cause. If short-pays keep appearing, check price lists, rounding and bank fee terms with the customer.

Handled this way, small overdue invoices stop being a nagging distraction. You know what each chase costs, you have a rule for every balance, and most of the work happens automatically.

Frequently asked questions

What counts as a small overdue balance?

It depends on your costs. A useful rule is any balance below two to three times what one manual follow-up costs your team, often somewhere between $20 and $100.

Is it worth chasing a very small invoice?

Usually yes, but only with automated reminders and pay links. Manual calls and emails for a few dollars often cost more than you recover.

Can I add an old small balance to a new invoice?

Yes, as long as it is clearly labelled with the original invoice number and date. Many businesses also send a monthly statement listing all open items.

When should I write off a small balance?

When the customer is inactive, the balance is below your threshold and reminders have gone unanswered. Record it consistently and check the tax treatment with your accountant.
