How Do You Run a Workplace Investigation in a Small Business?

A fair investigation protects your people and your business. Learn the steps, from first complaint to outcome, and when to bring in outside help.

November 7, 2026 · 6 min read

Sooner or later, most employers receive a complaint: about bullying, harassment, theft, a safety breach or a manager’s behaviour. In a small business there is no HR department to take over, so the owner or office manager has to act. This guide answers the questions people ask most. It is general guidance, not legal advice, and the rules differ by country and region.

When do you need a formal investigation?

Not every grievance needs one. A minor disagreement between colleagues can often be settled with a conversation. Start a formal investigation when an allegation is serious, involves possible discrimination, harassment, safety or dishonesty, or when an informal fix has failed. If you are unsure, it is usually safer to look into it properly than to ignore it.

Who should investigate?

Choose someone impartial: not involved in the events, not a close friend or direct line manager of either person, and able to keep things confidential. In a very small team that may be difficult. Consider a manager from another area, a trusted advisor or an outside investigator. If the owner is the subject of the complaint, an outside person is almost always the right call.

What are the steps?

1. Acknowledge the complaint and act quickly

Thank the person for raising it, explain what happens next and give a realistic timeline. Delay is one of the most common reasons a small problem becomes a big one. Ask for the complaint in writing, or write it down together and have them confirm it.

2. Protect people while you look into it

Consider interim measures: separating the people involved, changing reporting lines or, for serious allegations, a short paid suspension. Treat these as neutral steps, not a verdict, and say so. Remind everyone that retaliation against the person who complained is not acceptable.

3. Define the scope

Write down exactly what is alleged, which policies might be involved and what you need to find out. A clear scope keeps the investigation fair and stops it from drifting into unrelated issues.

4. Gather evidence

  • Documents. Emails, messages, schedules, time records and earlier warnings.
  • Physical or digital records. Access logs, CCTV or system data, where you are allowed to use them under privacy rules.
  • Witnesses. People who saw or heard something relevant.

Collect and store evidence securely, and do it before interviews so that you can put specific facts to people.

5. Interview fairly

Interview the person who complained, the person accused and the witnesses, in that order. Use open questions, such as “What happened next?”, and avoid leading ones. Take notes, let the person read and sign them, and allow the accused to respond to every allegation. Check whether your local rules or policies give employees the right to bring a companion.

6. Weigh the evidence

Most workplace investigations use a “balance of probabilities” test: is it more likely than not that this happened? You are not a court, so you do not need certainty. Separate facts from opinion, note where accounts conflict and explain which version you found more credible and why.

7. Decide and communicate

Write a short report with the allegation, the evidence, your findings and the reasoning. Then decide on an outcome that is proportionate: no action, training, a coaching conversation, a formal warning or, in the most serious cases, dismissal. Tell both people the outcome in person, as far as confidentiality allows, and follow with a letter.

8. Follow up

Check in after a few weeks with the person who complained. Is the behaviour over? Do they feel safe? Look for a lesson too: a missing policy, a training gap or a manager who needs support.

What records should you keep?

Keep the complaint, your plan, evidence, interview notes, the report, the decision and any letters. Store them in a restricted-access place, separate from the general personnel file, and keep them only as long as your local rules require.

When should you call a lawyer?

Get advice from an employment lawyer or a qualified HR professional early if the case involves:

  • Serious allegations such as sexual harassment, discrimination, violence or fraud.
  • Possible dismissal or legal action by either side.
  • Whistleblowing or complaints about senior leaders or the owner.
  • Possible criminal conduct, where the police may need to be involved.

Common mistakes

  • Deciding before investigating. A conclusion in advance makes the process unfair.
  • Promising total confidentiality. You can promise to share only on a need-to-know basis, not to keep everything secret.
  • Skipping the accused’s side. Everyone deserves a chance to respond.
  • Poor notes. If it is not written down, it is hard to prove later.
  • Ignoring the aftermath. The work is not done when the report is.

Frequently asked questions

Do I have to investigate every complaint?

Not every complaint needs a formal process, but you should always take it seriously and respond. Minor issues may be resolved informally. Allegations of harassment, discrimination, safety risks or dishonesty usually call for a proper investigation.

Can I suspend an employee during an investigation?

Sometimes, for example when there is a risk to people or evidence. It should be brief, usually paid, and described as neutral rather than a punishment. Rules vary, so check your local requirements first.

How long should an investigation take?

As quickly as is fair. Many small cases take one to three weeks, while complex ones take longer. Tell everyone the expected timeline and update them if it changes.

Should the accused see the evidence?

Generally they should know the allegations and have a fair chance to respond to the key evidence. What you can share may be limited by privacy or by witness safety, so seek advice in sensitive cases.

Is this guide legal advice?

No. It is general information. Employment law differs between countries and regions, so consult a qualified lawyer or HR professional for serious or high-risk cases.
