How to Handle an Employee Leave of Absence Request

A practical guide for small businesses: types of leave, what to document, how to approve or deny fairly, how to stay in touch and how to manage the return to work.

October 27, 2026 · 6 min read

Sooner or later, every small business gets the message: an employee needs time away. It might be a new baby, surgery, a sick parent, a bereavement or a long-planned sabbatical. In a team of ten, one absence is felt immediately, and mishandling the request can cost you trust, money and sometimes a legal claim. This guide walks through a calm, fair process from the first conversation to the first day back.

Important: leave rules differ widely between countries and, often, between states or regions. Entitlements, pay, notice periods, job protection and eligibility are set by local law, so check the rules where you operate or ask a qualified adviser before making decisions.

What types of leave exist?

Names and rules vary, but most employees ask for one of these:

  • Medical or sick leave. For the employee's own illness, injury or recovery, from a few days to many months.
  • Parental leave. Maternity, paternity, adoption or shared parental leave around the arrival of a child.
  • Family and caregiver leave. Time to care for a seriously ill spouse, child or parent.
  • Bereavement leave. Short, paid or unpaid time after the death of a close relative.
  • Statutory or civic leave. Jury service, military or reserve duty, public duties.
  • Personal or unpaid leave. Study, travel, a sabbatical or other personal reasons, usually at the employer's discretion.

Some of these are legal rights you must grant. Others are discretionary and depend on your own policy. Knowing which is which is the first thing to settle.

Step 1: Receive the request properly

Ask for requests in writing, even if the first mention was a chat in the corridor. A short email is enough. It should state the type of leave, the proposed start date and the expected length. Acknowledge it within a day or two and say when they can expect a decision. Treat the conversation as private, and share details only with the people who truly need them.

Step 2: Check eligibility and your policy

Before answering, check three things: whether the law gives the employee a right to this leave, whether your written policy or their contract adds anything, and whether the employee meets conditions such as length of service. If you have no written policy, now is the time to write one so future cases are handled the same way.

Step 3: Ask for the right documentation

Request only what you need and no more. Typically that means:

  • A medical certificate or a note from a healthcare provider, confirming the need for leave and the expected duration, not the diagnosis itself.
  • For parental leave, an expected due date or adoption confirmation.
  • For family care, a simple statement of the relationship and the need.
  • For bereavement, a brief description is usually enough.

Store these records securely, separate from general personnel files, with access limited to HR or the owner. Health information is sensitive and often has special legal protection.

Step 4: Approve or deny fairly

Fairness is mostly consistency. Apply the same criteria to everyone, whatever their role, seniority or how much you like them. Where leave is a legal entitlement, you generally cannot refuse it, though you may be able to discuss timing. Where it is discretionary, weigh the business impact honestly, but base the decision on workload and policy, never on the reason being personal, a health condition, pregnancy, family status or any protected characteristic.

If you must deny or modify a request, do it in writing, explain the reason, offer an alternative where possible (a shorter period, a later start, partly remote work) and keep a record. A written, reasoned decision protects both sides.

Step 5: Plan the cover

Once approved, agree a handover plan: which tasks are urgent, who covers them and what can wait. Options include redistributing work, a temporary hire, a freelancer or adjusted deadlines. Tell clients and the team what they need to know, without revealing private reasons. Be careful not to overload colleagues, because resentment spreads quickly in a small team.

Step 6: Keep in touch, respectfully

Agree before the leave begins how and how often you will contact the employee. For a long absence, a short check-in every few weeks may be welcome, but they should never feel pressured to work or answer business messages. Share important company news so they do not feel forgotten. Check what your local law says about contact during protected leave, as some places restrict it.

Step 7: Manage the return to work

A few weeks before the end date, confirm the return date and arrange a short meeting. Cover what changed while they were away, their workload and, where needed, a phased or part-time return. After medical leave, ask whether any adjustments would help, such as flexible hours or a changed workstation, and consider whether you are required to provide them. Make the first week gentle, then check in again after a month. In many countries, employees returning from protected leave have a right to the same or an equivalent job, so confirm your obligations.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Deciding by gut feeling. Without a policy, similar cases end up with different outcomes.
  • Asking for a diagnosis. You usually need to know the duration, not the details.
  • Gossip. Keep the reason confidential.
  • Silence. Neither disappearing nor over-contacting helps the employee.
  • Poor records. Dates, approvals and balances should be written down.

Handled well, a leave of absence is a short gap and often builds loyalty. Employees remember how they were treated when life got hard.

Keeping track

The practical difficulty is often admin: who is off when, what balance remains, which documents were received. A shared spreadsheet works for a while.
