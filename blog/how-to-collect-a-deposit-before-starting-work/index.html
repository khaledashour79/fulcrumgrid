How to Collect a Deposit Before Starting Work (and How Much to Ask For)

Why a deposit protects your cash flow, how much to ask for, what to put in the contract, how milestone billing works and what to do if the client refuses.

October 27, 2026 · 6 min read

Starting work before any money has changed hands is one of the most common reasons small businesses and freelancers end up chasing payments. The work is delivered, the costs are already spent, and the invoice sits unpaid. A deposit changes that dynamic from day one. This guide explains why deposits matter, how much to ask for, how to word them in your contract and what to do when a client pushes back.

Why collect a deposit?

  • It covers your early costs. Materials, software licences, subcontractors and your own time are spent before the first invoice is normally paid.
  • It filters out unserious clients. Someone who pays a deposit has made a decision. Someone who refuses might not have paid the final invoice on time either.
  • It protects your cash flow. A project paid 60 or 90 days after delivery can choke a small business, even with an honest client.
  • It reserves your time. You turn down other work to hold a slot for this client, and the deposit makes the commitment mutual.
  • It reduces disputes. A client who has already paid something is more invested, approves faster and disappears less.

How much should the deposit be?

There is no universal figure, but most service businesses land between 25% and 50% of the project value.

  • 25% fits low-risk work, repeat clients and projects with few upfront costs, such as consulting or short design jobs.
  • 30 to 40% is a common middle ground for creative, marketing and software projects.
  • 50% fits work with high upfront costs, custom production, new clients with no track record, or projects whose cancellation would leave you with unsellable work.
  • 100% upfront is reasonable for small jobs, digital products or subscriptions, where splitting the payment costs more than it saves.

A useful rule: the deposit should cover at least your direct costs plus the value of the time you reserve. If materials are 30% of the budget, a 10% deposit forces you to finance the client's project.

Deposit, retainer and advance: what's the difference?

A deposit is a partial payment of the final price that secures the start of work. A retainer is a recurring payment that reserves your availability for ongoing work. An advance is usually money paid before you incur costs, settled later. Use the term that matches your agreement, and make clear whether the amount is refundable.

What should the contract say?

A deposit only protects you if it is in writing. Include these points in your proposal, contract or order form:

  • Amount and due date. For example: “A deposit of 40% of the project fee, €2,000, is due on signing.”
  • When work starts. “Work begins once the deposit is received in cleared funds.” This sentence is what makes the deposit enforceable in practice.
  • How it is applied. “The deposit will be deducted from the final invoice.”
  • Refund terms. State whether it is refundable, partly refundable or non-refundable, and in what circumstances. A common formula: refundable before work begins, non-refundable afterward to the extent of the work done.
  • Cancellation and delays. What happens if the client cancels or if their delays push the schedule back by more than a set number of weeks.
  • Payment methods and late fees. Where to pay and the interest or charges that apply on late payment, where local law allows.

Have a local professional review the final wording. Rules on refundable deposits and penalty clauses vary between countries and between business and consumer clients.

Milestone billing: what comes after the deposit

On any project longer than a few weeks, split the remaining balance into milestones tied to visible progress, not just dates. A typical structure for a 10,000 project:

  • 30% on signing (the deposit)
  • 30% at the first delivery, such as the approved draft or prototype
  • 30% at final delivery, before you hand over files or go live
  • 10% on acceptance, after a short review period

Good milestones are concrete and verifiable: “approval of the design mockups” works, “when it is 50% done” does not. Each milestone gets its own invoice, its due date and a clause stating that the next phase begins after payment. Tie the last payment to delivery, not to the client's calendar.

What if the client refuses to pay a deposit?

Find out why

Ask calmly. Often there is an internal approval process, a cash-flow concern or a bad experience with another supplier, and each case has a different answer.

Offer alternatives, not discounts

  • Lower the deposit to 20 or 25%, but shorten the intervals between milestones.
  • Split the deposit into two payments before work begins.
  • Start with a small, paid first phase, such as a discovery phase, and treat it as a test.
  • Accept an approved purchase order, with short payment terms, from an established company.
  • Offer an escrow account on large projects, so both sides can see the funds secured.

Know your limit

If a new client refuses any form of advance payment and wants all the risk on you, that tells you a lot about how the relationship will go. Turning down the work is a legitimate business decision. With long-standing clients who have a clean payment history, a smaller deposit or none can be fine, but make it a conscious exception.

Common mistakes

  • Starting before the money arrives. Once you have begun, you lose your bargaining power.
  • Agreeing only verbally. If it is not in the signed document, it is a request, not a condition.
  • Vague refund terms. Ambiguity causes arguments at the worst moment.
  • Not issuing a proper invoice. Send a numbered deposit invoice with a due date, and a receipt once it is paid.
  • Forgetting to deduct it. Make sure the final invoice shows the deposit already received.

Frequently asked questions

How much deposit should I ask for?

Most service businesses ask for 25 to 50% of the project value. Use the higher end with new clients, custom work and high upfront costs, and the lower end with repeat clients and low-risk work.

Is the deposit refundable?

It depends on what your contract says. A common approach is refundable before work begins and non-refundable afterward, to the extent of the work done. Put it in writing and check local rules.

What is the difference between a deposit and a retainer?

A deposit is a partial payment toward the price of a specific project. A retainer is a recurring payment that reserves your availability for ongoing work.

Should I start before the deposit arrives?

No. State in the contract that work begins when the deposit is received. Starting earlier weakens your position and exposes you to the very risk the deposit was meant to cover.

What if my client says they never pay deposits?

Ask why and offer alternatives: a smaller deposit, a short paid first phase, shorter milestones or a purchase order. If they refuse any advance payment, consider turning down the work.
