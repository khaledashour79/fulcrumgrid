How Do You Run an Annual Salary Review in a Small Business?

A step-by-step guide to the annual salary review: set a merit budget, check market data, use compa-ratio and a merit matrix, calibrate and explain the outcome.

November 23, 2026 · 6 min read

In many small businesses, pay rises happen when someone asks, when someone threatens to leave or when the owner remembers. The result is pay that nobody can quite explain. An annual salary review replaces that with one predictable moment each year when every salary is looked at with the same budget, the same data and the same rules. Here is how to run one without a large HR team.

What is an annual salary review?

It is a once-a-year process in which you review every employee’s base pay, decide on any increases within a set budget and communicate the outcome on a fixed date. It is separate from promotions, which follow a change in role, and from bonuses, which reward results for a period. Keeping the three apart makes each decision easier to explain.

Step 1: How do you set the budget?

Start with what the business can afford, not with individual names. Most companies express the budget as a merit pool: a percentage of total base payroll set aside for increases. If your annual payroll is 800,000 and you approve a 3.5 percent pool, you have 28,000 to distribute across the team.

To choose the percentage, look at three things: your profit and cash forecast for next year, published salary increase surveys for your sector and region, and local inflation. Consider holding back about half a percentage point for market corrections and retention cases.

Step 2: Where do you get market data?

You do not need an expensive consultancy. Combine two or three sources: free or low-cost salary surveys from industry associations, government wage statistics, recruiter reports and the salaries in job adverts for similar roles. Match on the job content and level, not just the title, and use the same data every year so trends are comparable.

Step 3: How do salary bands and compa-ratio help?

A salary band sets the minimum, midpoint and maximum for each role or level. The midpoint is roughly what the market pays a fully competent person in that job. Compa-ratio shows where someone sits in the band:

Compa-ratio = current salary ÷ band midpoint

Someone earning 45,000 in a band with a 50,000 midpoint has a compa-ratio of 0.90. Below about 0.90 usually means a person is new to the role or underpaid; above about 1.10 means they are already well paid for the job. If you do not have bands yet, build simple ones before the review, because without them every decision becomes a negotiation.

Step 4: How should performance affect pay?

Performance should clearly influence increases, but a formula should not make the decision on its own. Ratings are imperfect, and a rigid link pushes managers to inflate them. Use performance to set a guideline range, then let managers adjust for context: a strong performer at the top of the band, a recent promotion, a skill the market is short of or a pay gap you have just discovered.

A simple merit matrix

A merit matrix combines performance with position in the band. People who perform well and sit low in their band get the largest increases, so pay moves towards the market faster. Here is an example for a 3.5 percent pool:

  • Exceeds expectations: 6 percent if below 0.90, 4.5 percent if between 0.90 and 1.10, 3 percent if above 1.10.
  • Meets expectations: 4.5 percent if below 0.90, 3 percent if between 0.90 and 1.10, 1.5 percent if above 1.10.
  • Not yet meeting expectations: usually no increase, with a clear plan and a check-in date instead.

Run your team through the matrix and adjust the percentages until the total fits the pool.

Step 5: What does a realistic timeline look like?

For a team of 10 to 100 people, allow about eight weeks:

  • Weeks 1–2: set the budget, refresh market data and update bands.
  • Weeks 3–4: finish performance reviews so ratings are ready.
  • Week 5: managers propose increases using the matrix.
  • Week 6: calibration meeting and final approval.
  • Weeks 7–8: prepare letters, hold conversations and update payroll before the effective date.

Step 6: Why do managers need to calibrate?

Some managers rate generously and others harshly. In a calibration meeting, managers and the owner or HR lead review all proposals side by side, compare people in similar roles and challenge outliers. Check that increases are consistent across teams, part-time staff and people on leave, look for unexplained gaps between groups doing similar work, and record the reason for every decision that falls outside the guideline.

Step 7: How do you communicate the outcome?

Every employee should hear the result from their manager in a short one-to-one conversation, followed by written confirmation. A good conversation covers:

  • The decision: the new salary, the percentage and the effective date.
  • The reasons: how performance and position in the band shaped it.
  • The path forward: what would lead to a higher increase or a promotion next time.

Be honest when the budget is tight, and never blame another manager or the process. If someone receives no increase, explain why and what needs to change.

Step 8: What should you document?

Keep a record of the budget, the market data used, the bands, the matrix, each person’s old and new salary, the rating and the reason for any exception. Good records make next year’s review faster and help you answer questions consistently. Rules on pay equity, transparency and record keeping vary by country, so check the requirements that apply to you; this article is general guidance, not legal advice.

Frequently asked questions

What is a typical merit increase budget?

It depends on your sector, region, inflation and finances. Check recent salary increase surveys for your market each year rather than relying on a fixed rule, and set the pool your business can sustain.

Should salary reviews happen at the same time as performance reviews?

Finish performance reviews a few weeks before pay decisions. Many teams discuss them in separate meetings so the feedback conversation is not dominated by money.

What if an employee is at the top of their salary band?

Consider a smaller increase or a one-off lump sum instead of a base pay rise, and talk about the growth or promotion that would open a higher band.

Do we have to give everyone an increase?

Not necessarily. Increases should reflect budget, performance and market position. Whatever you decide, explain the reasons clearly and check any rules that apply where you operate.
