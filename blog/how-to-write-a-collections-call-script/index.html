How to Write a Collections Call Script That Gets Invoices Paid

A good collections call script is polite, short and ends with a payment date. Learn what to include, a template to adapt, and how to answer common objections.

October 8, 2026 · 6 min read

Most overdue invoices are not unpaid because the customer refuses to pay. They are unpaid because nobody told the right person, clearly, what is owed and by when. A good collections call script fixes that. It does not turn your team into robots; it gives them a reliable structure so every call is polite, short and ends with a commitment. This guide shows what to put in a script, gives a simple template, and covers the objections you will hear most.

Why do collections calls often fail?

Calls go wrong for predictable reasons: the caller is nervous and apologises for ringing, the invoice details are not at hand, the conversation drifts into complaints, and it ends with “we will look into it” and no date. A script prevents all four by deciding in advance what you will say, what you will ask and how you will close.

What should a collections call script include?

  • Preparation checklist. Invoice number, amount, due date, days overdue, payment history, any open disputes and the contact’s role. Never call without them.
  • Opening. Your name, your company and the reason for the call, in one sentence.
  • Invoice facts. State the number, amount and due date plainly, without accusation.
  • A direct question. Ask for the status, such as whether the invoice was received and approved.
  • The ask. A specific payment date, not “soon”.
  • Objection responses. Short, prepared answers to the five or six reasons you hear most.
  • Close and recap. Repeat the date, amount and payment method, and say you will confirm by email.

A simple script you can adapt

1. Open with purpose

“Hello Ms Lee, this is Sam from Northwind Supplies. I am calling about invoice 1042 for $3,200, which was due on September 15. Do you have a minute?”

2. Ask before you assume

“Did the invoice reach you, and has it been approved for payment?” This one question tells you the cause: a missing invoice, an internal approval, a dispute or a cash problem. Then listen, and do not rush to fill the silence.

3. Make the ask

“Can you confirm the payment will be made by this Friday?” If the answer is vague, offer two options: “Would Thursday or Friday work better?” People commit more readily to a choice than to an open question.

4. Recap and confirm

“So that is $3,200 by bank transfer on Friday, October 9. I will send a short email now with the invoice attached so you have it.” Send that email the same day.

How do you handle common objections?

  • “I never received it.” Resend it during the call, confirm the correct address, and agree a payment date counted from the moment it arrives.
  • “It is in approval.” Ask who approves it, where it is stuck and when a decision is due. Offer to speak to the approver directly.
  • “We have a problem with the order.” Log the dispute, ask what exactly is wrong and agree a date to resolve it. Ask whether the undisputed part can be paid now.
  • “Cash is tight.” Stay calm and propose a short payment plan, for example two instalments, rather than waiting indefinitely.
  • “Call the accounts department.” Ask for a name and a direct number or email, and note the date and time you called.

What tone works best?

Be friendly, firm and brief. Assume the invoice is a simple oversight until the customer tells you otherwise, because most are. Avoid blame words such as “you failed” and never threaten something you will not do. Use the customer’s name, speak slowly and aim for calls of three to five minutes. If the customer is upset, acknowledge it first (“I understand this is frustrating”) and then return to the date.

What should you do after the call?

Record the outcome the same day: who you spoke to, what was promised, the agreed date and the next step. Send the confirmation email. If the promised date passes without payment, call again the next morning and refer to the earlier promise. It also helps to set the sequence in advance, for example a reminder before the due date, a call three days after it and escalation at 30 days, so follow-up does not depend on anyone’s memory.

How do you improve the script over time?

Track how many calls end with a payment date and how many promised dates are kept. If many customers say they never received the invoice, fix your invoicing, not the script. Review call notes monthly and update the objection answers with what you actually hear.

A script is a starting point, not a speech. Prepare, ask one clear question, agree one clear date and confirm it in writing.

Frequently asked questions

How many times should I call about an overdue invoice?

Send a reminder before the due date, make the first call within a few days after it, then follow up roughly weekly, each time referring to the last promise. If there is no progress after three or four attempts, escalate to a senior contact.

Should I call or send an email first?

For routine invoices, a friendly email before and just after the due date is enough. Once an invoice is overdue or the amount is large, a call is faster and more personal. Always follow the call with a confirmation email.

What if the customer keeps promising but never pays?

Keep a written record of each promise, ask for a payment in part or in instalments, and escalate to someone with authority to approve payment. Consider pausing new orders until the account is current.

How long should a collections call last?

Three to five minutes is usually enough: open, ask the status, agree a date and recap. Longer calls tend to drift into complaints, so book a separate conversation for any real dispute.
