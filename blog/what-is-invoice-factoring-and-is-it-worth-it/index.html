What Is Invoice Factoring, and Is It Worth the Cost?

Factoring advances 80-90% of an unpaid invoice for a fee; learn the costs, recourse vs non-recourse, the alternatives and when it is really worth it.

October 25, 2026 · 6 min read

Waiting 45 or 60 days for a customer to pay is hard on any small business, especially when payroll and suppliers will not wait. Invoice factoring promises cash now instead of later. This guide answers the questions owners ask most: what factoring is, what it costs, how recourse differs from non-recourse, what the alternatives are, and when it is worth it.

What is invoice factoring?

Invoice factoring is a financing arrangement in which you sell your unpaid invoices to a third party, called a factor, at a discount. The factor pays you most of the invoice value right away, then collects from your customer and pays you the rest, minus its fees. It is not a traditional loan. The decision rests mainly on how creditworthy your customers are, not on your own balance sheet, which is why it is popular with young or fast-growing businesses.

How does invoice factoring work, step by step?

  • You invoice as usual and submit the invoice to the factor.
  • The factor advances cash, typically within one to two business days.
  • The customer pays the factor, often to a payment address or account the factor controls.
  • You receive the reserve, meaning the held-back part of the invoice, minus the factor’s fee.

In some arrangements your customer knows a factor is involved (notification factoring). In others the factor stays in the background and you keep collecting yourself (confidential or non-notification factoring), which usually costs more.

How much does invoice factoring cost?

Two numbers matter: the advance rate and the factoring fee.

  • Advance rate: the share of the invoice paid upfront, commonly 80% to 90%. The remainder is held as a reserve until your customer pays.
  • Factoring fee (discount rate): often about 1% to 3% of the invoice value for every 30 days it stays open, or a flat rate for a set period. Rates vary by industry, volume and customer credit.
  • Extra charges: application or setup fees, minimum monthly volumes, bank transfer fees, and charges for late-paying invoices. Always ask for the full fee schedule.

Here is an example. You factor a $10,000 invoice at an 85% advance and a fee of 3% for the period until your customer pays. You receive $8,500 upfront. When the customer pays, you get the $1,500 reserve minus the $300 fee, so $1,200. Your total is $9,700. That looks like 3%, but you got most of the money about six weeks early, so the annualized cost is far higher, often well above 20%. Compare factoring on that basis, not on the headline rate.

What is the difference between recourse and non-recourse factoring?

The difference is who carries the loss if your customer never pays.

  • Recourse factoring: if the customer does not pay within an agreed period, you must buy back the invoice or replace it. Fees are lower because you keep the credit risk.
  • Non-recourse factoring: the factor absorbs the loss, but usually only for specific reasons such as customer insolvency. Fees are higher, and disputes, quality complaints or late payment generally remain your problem.

“Non-recourse” is not the same as “no risk”. Read the contract definition of a covered loss before you rely on it.

How does factoring compare with the alternatives?

A business line of credit

A line of credit lets you draw cash when needed and pay interest only on what you use. It is often cheaper than factoring, but approval depends on your own credit and financials, and limits may be modest for newer businesses.

An early-payment discount

Offering terms such as 2% off if paid within 10 days costs you a known amount and gets money in faster without a third party. Annualized, 2/10 net 30 is expensive too, but you decide which customers get it.

Better collections

Often the cheapest fix is simply getting paid on time. Clear terms, a pay link on every invoice, and automatic reminders before and after the due date can cut your average days-to-pay by a week or more. When is invoice factoring worth it?

  • You are growing faster than your cash. Big orders from reliable customers can pay for the fee many times over.
  • You cannot yet qualify for a line of credit or loan. Factoring may be the only quick option.
  • Your customers pay slowly but safely. Long terms with solid payers suit factoring well.
  • Your margins are healthy. If a 2% to 4% fee wipes out your profit on a job, factoring will not fix the problem.

When should you avoid it?

Be cautious if your margins are thin, if you only need a short bridge that a line of credit would cover, or if the contract locks you into minimum volumes or long terms. Also check how the factor will treat your customers: its collection style reflects on your business.

What should you do next?

Work out your real cost per invoice, compare at least two or three offers, and test whether faster collections alone would close the gap. If factoring still makes sense, start with a single customer or a short contract rather than your whole receivables book. This article is general information, not financial advice.

Frequently asked questions

What is a typical advance rate for invoice factoring?

Most factors advance 80% to 90% of the invoice value upfront and hold the rest as a reserve, which is released to you, minus fees, once your customer pays.

How much does invoice factoring cost?

Fees commonly run about 1% to 3% of the invoice per 30 days, plus possible setup and other charges. Annualized, the cost is often well above 20%, so compare offers on that basis.

Is recourse or non-recourse factoring better?

Recourse is cheaper but you carry the loss if the customer does not pay. Non-recourse costs more and usually covers only specific events such as insolvency, so read the contract closely.

Is invoice factoring the same as a loan?

No. You sell your invoices rather than borrow against your own credit, though the effect on cash flow is similar. Approval depends mostly on your customers' credit.

What are cheaper alternatives to invoice factoring?

A business line of credit, an early-payment discount for customers and faster collections through pay links and reminders are often cheaper when you qualify or your customers cooperate.
