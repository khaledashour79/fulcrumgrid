What Is Retainage (Retention) and How Do You Get It Released?

Retainage is money withheld until a project is finished; learn how it works, when you can claim it and the steps that get retention released and paid faster.

November 1, 2026 · 6 min read

You finish the job, the client is happy, and yet a slice of your money is still sitting in someone else's account months later. That slice is retainage, and for contractors and subcontractors it can be the difference between a healthy month and a cash crunch. This guide explains what retainage is, when it becomes payable and how to get it released without damaging the relationship.

What is retainage?

Retainage, also called retention, is a percentage of each progress payment that a client or main contractor holds back until the work is complete. It is common in construction, engineering and fit-out projects. The rate is usually 5% to 10% and is written into the contract. Think of it as a security deposit: it gives the client leverage to make sure defects are fixed and the paperwork is finished before the final money leaves.

How much money are we really talking about?

More than most people notice while the job is running. On a contract worth 400,000, a 10% retention is 40,000. If your margin is 8%, the retained amount can be larger than your profit on the whole project. That is why retainage deserves its own line in your cash-flow forecast and your receivables report, not a footnote.

When does retainage become payable?

The contract decides, so read the clause before you promise your accountant a date. The usual triggers are:

  • Substantial or practical completion. Some contracts release half the retention here and keep the rest for the defects period.
  • End of the defects liability period. Typically 6 to 24 months after completion, after which the remaining retention is due.
  • Final acceptance or final certificate. The client or their engineer formally signs off the work.
  • Milestone or phase completion. Larger projects sometimes allow release phase by phase.

Many jurisdictions also have rules on how long a client may hold retention after completion, and some require it to be held in a separate account. Rules vary widely, so check local law or ask a lawyer if the amount is large.

What do you need to have ready before you ask?

Most delayed releases are not disputes at all; they are incomplete closeout packages. Before you send the request, gather:

  • The completion certificate or signed acceptance that shows the work is finished.
  • A cleared punch list, meaning every snag item is fixed and signed off.
  • Lien or payment waivers from you and your own suppliers and subcontractors, where required.
  • Closeout documents: as-built drawings, warranties, test reports and operation manuals.
  • Insurance and bond documents, including a warranty bond if the contract allows one in place of cash retention.
  • A clear retainage invoice that references the contract, the certificates and the exact amount held to date.

How do you request release step by step?

  1. Confirm the trigger. Note the exact clause and the date the condition was met.
  2. Send a formal request in writing, addressed to the person named in the contract, with the invoice and supporting documents attached.
  3. Ask for a confirmation date. A polite line such as “please confirm the expected payment date by Friday” turns a vague promise into something you can track.
  4. Follow up on a schedule. A reminder before the due date, one on the due date and one a week later is reasonable and professional.
  5. Escalate with a paper trail. If nothing moves, go to the project sponsor or finance lead, then to a formal notice under the contract.

Keep every message in writing. If the matter ever becomes a dispute, a clean timeline of requests and replies is your strongest evidence.

What if the client refuses or delays?

First find out why. Common reasons are an open defect, a missing document, a disagreement about completion dates, or simply an invoice stuck in someone's approval queue. Fix what you can and ask for partial release of the undisputed part. Withholding the whole amount over a small issue is often not allowed, so point to the contract wording calmly. If the delay continues, a formal notice, mediation or, as a last resort, legal action may follow. Many contracts and local laws also add interest on late retention, so record the due date precisely.

How can you reduce the retainage problem next time?

  • Negotiate the terms up front: a lower rate, a cap once 50% of the work is done, or release at substantial completion.
  • Offer a retention bond or bank guarantee instead of cash, if your finances allow it.
  • Close out as you go. Collect warranties and test reports during the project, not in the last week.
  • Flow terms down carefully. If you hold retention from your own subcontractors, make sure the release dates line up with yours.

The takeaway is simple: know your trigger, finish your closeout package early, request in writing and follow up on a schedule. Retainage is money you have already earned; treat it like any other invoice.

Frequently asked questions

Is retainage the same as retention?

Yes. Retainage is the term mostly used in the United States; retention is common in the UK, Europe and the Middle East. Both mean money withheld from payments until agreed conditions are met.

How much retainage is normal?

Most contracts hold 5% to 10% of each payment. Some reduce the rate or stop withholding once the project is about half complete, so check what your contract says.

How long can a client hold retainage?

It depends on your contract and local law. Often it is released at completion or after a defects period of 6 to 24 months. Some places set legal time limits, so verify the rules where the project is.

Can I get retainage released early?

Sometimes. You can ask for release at substantial completion, replace cash with a retention bond or guarantee, or request payment of the undisputed portion while a small issue is resolved.
