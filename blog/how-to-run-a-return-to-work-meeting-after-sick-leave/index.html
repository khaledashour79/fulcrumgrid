How Do You Run a Return-to-Work Meeting After Sick Leave?

A return-to-work meeting welcomes people back, checks they are ready and spots patterns early. Learn when to hold one, what to ask and what to avoid.

November 17, 2026 · 6 min read

Coming back after sick leave can feel awkward for everyone. The employee may worry about what people think, and the manager may not know what they are allowed to ask. A short, well-run return-to-work meeting removes most of that awkwardness. It shows the person they were missed, checks they are ready to work and gives you an early, fair way to spot problems. This guide explains how to run one step by step.

What is a return-to-work meeting?

It is a brief, private conversation between an employee and their manager, held when the employee comes back after an absence due to illness. For a single day off it may take five minutes. After a longer absence it may take half an hour and include a plan for the weeks ahead. It is a welfare conversation, not a disciplinary hearing, and it should never feel like one.

Why does it matter?

  • Welfare. It is the moment to check that the person is well enough to work and to offer support if they are not fully recovered.
  • Spotting patterns. A quick, consistent record after every absence makes it easier to notice when short absences are adding up, or when the same cause keeps coming back.
  • Reducing repeat absence. Many absences have a cause at work, such as workload, a difficult shift or a physical strain. Asking about it gives you the chance to fix it before it happens again.
  • Fairness. When everyone gets the same conversation, nobody feels singled out.

When should you hold one?

The simplest rule is after every sickness absence, however short. Consistency is what keeps the meeting from feeling like an accusation. Some organisations only hold a full meeting once an absence passes a threshold, such as three or more days, and use a quick check-in for shorter ones. Either approach works if you write it into your policy and apply it to everyone.

Timing matters. Aim for the first day back, ideally in the first few hours. Waiting a week makes the conversation feel like a formality or, worse, like something is being saved up. If the manager is away, a deputy or HR can hold it.

Who should run it?

Usually the employee's direct line manager, because they know the work and can make practical changes. HR should step in when the manager is part of the problem, when the absence is long or complex, or when the employee asks for someone else. Whoever runs it needs a little training: how to open the conversation, what not to ask and when to refer the matter on.

What is a good agenda?

  • Welcome them back. Find a private space and start by saying you are glad they are back.
  • Check how they are. Ask whether they feel well enough to do their normal work.
  • Confirm the dates and paperwork. Agree the dates of absence and whether any certificate or self-declaration is needed under your policy.
  • Ask about work factors. Is anything at work causing or worsening the problem?
  • Discuss support or adjustments. Agree anything that would help, and for how long.
  • Catch them up. Share what changed while they were away and any urgent priorities.
  • Agree next steps. Summarise what was agreed and when you will check in again.

Which questions should you ask?

  • How are you feeling now? Are you ready to do your usual tasks?
  • Is there anything about your work that contributed to the absence or could slow your recovery?
  • Is there anything we can change, for a while or permanently, that would help?
  • Do you expect any follow-up appointments we should plan around?
  • Is there anything else you would like me to know?

Which questions should you avoid?

The employee decides how much medical detail to share. Do not press for a diagnosis, ask about medication or ask about family medical history. Avoid questions that touch on pregnancy, disability, age, religion or other protected characteristics unless the employee raises them and they are relevant to support. Never imply doubt with questions like "Were you really ill?" Rules on health data, medical certificates and discrimination differ between countries, so check local employment and privacy law, or ask an adviser, before you finalise your script.

How do you handle adjustments and a phased return?

After a longer illness, an operation or a mental health absence, going straight back to full duties may not be realistic. A phased return might mean shorter hours for two to four weeks, lighter duties, fewer customer-facing shifts or more breaks. Base it on what the employee says and, where available, on advice from their doctor or an occupational health service. Write down what is changing, how long it lasts and when you will review it, so the arrangement does not drift on indefinitely.

How should you document it?

Keep a short, factual note: the date, the absence dates, the general reason in the employee's own words, any agreed support and the review date. Share the note with the employee so they can correct it. Store it confidentially with restricted access, separate from general team files, and keep any medical detail to the minimum needed. Keep it only as long as your retention policy and local law allow.

What if short-term absences keep recurring?

Your notes are what make a pattern visible. If absences pass a trigger point in your policy, hold a separate, planned review meeting rather than turning a routine return-to-work chat into a formal one. Tell the employee in advance what it is about. Look for an underlying cause, such as an ongoing health condition, caring duties or a problem at work, and consider a medical referral. Only if there is no improvement and no valid reason should you move to your formal attendance procedure.

Frequently asked questions

Is a return-to-work meeting a disciplinary meeting?

No. It is a welfare conversation to welcome the employee back and check they are ready to work. If absence later becomes a concern, that is handled in a separate, planned meeting under your attendance policy.

How long should a return-to-work meeting take?

For a short absence, five to ten minutes is usually enough. After a long absence or a complex illness, allow up to half an hour so you can agree any phased return or adjustments.

Can I ask an employee what illness they had?

You can ask in general terms how they are and whether work played a part, but they do not have to share a diagnosis. Rules on health data vary by country, so check local law before asking for medical detail.

Do I need a meeting after a single sick day?

Many employers hold a brief check-in after every absence because consistency makes it fair and helps spot patterns. Others set a threshold. Choose one rule, write it in your policy and apply it to everyone.
