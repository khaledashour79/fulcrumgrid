What Is First Pass Yield and How Do You Calculate It?

First pass yield shows how much work is done right the first time. Learn the formula, a worked example, rolled throughput yield and how to improve it.

November 23, 2026 · 6 min read

Most teams know their output. Far fewer know how much of that output was right the first time. First pass yield fills that gap. It tells you what share of work goes through a process without being fixed, redone or sent back, and it is one of the most revealing numbers an operations team can track. This guide covers the formula, a worked example, the multi-step version and how to use it outside the factory.

What is first pass yield?

First pass yield (FPY) is the percentage of units that complete a process step correctly the first time, with no rework, repair or return. A unit that fails inspection, gets fixed and then passes still counts as a failure for first pass yield. That is the point: the metric shows how well the process works when nobody steps in to rescue it.

It is also called first time yield or, in service work, first time right.

How do you calculate first pass yield?

First pass yield = units passed first time without rework ÷ units entering the process × 100

Count units at the start of the step, not just the ones that finished. Anything scrapped, reworked or returned for correction comes off the top line.

A worked example

A workshop starts 500 assemblies in a week. At final inspection, 440 pass with no issues. Another 40 fail, are repaired and then pass. The last 20 cannot be saved and are scrapped.

First pass yield = 440 ÷ 500 = 88 percent.

That means 60 assemblies, or 12 percent, consumed extra time, materials or inspection effort.

How is first pass yield different from final yield?

Final yield, sometimes called process yield, counts every good unit at the end, including the ones that were repaired. In the example, 480 of 500 assemblies eventually passed, so final yield is 96 percent.

The 8-point gap between 96 and 88 percent is the hidden factory: the people, time and space spent fixing work that should have been right first time. Final yield tells you what you delivered. First pass yield tells you what it cost to deliver it. A team that reports only final yield can look excellent while quietly spending a day a week on corrections.

What is rolled throughput yield?

Most work passes through several steps. Rolled throughput yield (RTY) is the probability that a unit gets through every step without any rework. You calculate it by multiplying the first pass yield of each step:

Rolled throughput yield = FPY of step 1 × FPY of step 2 × … × FPY of the last step

Take an order fulfilment process with three steps: picking at 97 percent, packing at 99 percent and invoicing at 95 percent. Rolled throughput yield is 0.97 × 0.99 × 0.95 = 0.912, or about 91 percent. The simple average of the three steps is 97 percent, which hides the fact that almost one order in eleven needs fixing somewhere along the way. The more steps a process has, the faster small losses compound, which is why rolled throughput yield is the better headline figure for end-to-end processes.

Can you use first pass yield outside manufacturing?

Yes. Any process with a clear definition of “done correctly” has a first pass yield.

  • Orders shipped right first time. Of 1,200 orders shipped in a month, 54 needed a reshipment, a correction or a replacement. First pass yield is 1,146 ÷ 1,200 = 95.5 percent.
  • Tickets resolved without reopening. A support team closed 400 tickets and 36 were reopened within seven days. First pass yield is 364 ÷ 400 = 91 percent.
  • Invoices issued without correction. Out of 250 invoices, 15 had to be credited and reissued. First pass yield is 235 ÷ 250 = 94 percent.

The key is to agree in advance what counts as a failure and over what window. A ticket reopened after 90 days is probably a new issue; one reopened the next morning is not.

How do you improve first pass yield?

Our guide to measuring and reducing rework goes deeper into root causes. In short, the fastest gains usually come from a few habits:

  • Measure by step, not just end to end. Step-level yields show exactly where defects enter.
  • Rank failure reasons. Log a short reason for every failure. A handful of causes usually explain most of the losses.
  • Fix the input, not the output. Unclear instructions, missing information and poor handoffs cause more errors than careless people do.
  • Build checks into the step. A checklist, a required field or a simple mistake-proofing device catches errors before they move downstream.
  • Do not hide fixes. If people quietly correct work without logging it, first pass yield looks better than it is and the cause never gets addressed.

How do you track first pass yield weekly?

  • Pick one or two processes. Start where errors are most costly or most visible to customers.
  • Set a target. Base it on your current baseline, for example moving from 88 to 92 percent over a quarter.
  • Record units in and first-time passes. Keep the counting rules fixed so that weeks stay comparable.
  • Review the trend and the top reasons. A weekly look at the line and the top three failure causes is enough to drive steady improvement.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good first pass yield?

It depends on the process and its complexity. Many repeatable service processes aim for 95 percent or more, while complex assemblies may start lower. The trend against your own baseline matters most.

Is first pass yield the same as first time right?

They describe the same idea. First time right is the term more often used in services and logistics, while first pass yield is more common in manufacturing.

Why is rolled throughput yield lower than first pass yield?

Because it multiplies the first pass yields of every step. Even when each step performs well on its own, small losses compound across the whole process.

Do reworked units count as good in first pass yield?

No. Any unit that needed correction, repair or return counts as a failure for first pass yield, even if it was eventually delivered. It does count as good in final yield.
