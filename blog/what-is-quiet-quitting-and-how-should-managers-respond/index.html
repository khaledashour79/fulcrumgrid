What Is Quiet Quitting and How Should Managers Respond?

Quiet quitting means doing only the job as written. Learn what drives it, how to tell it from burnout, and how managers can respond with clarity and care.

October 20, 2026 · 6 min read

Few workplace terms have sparked as much debate as quiet quitting. Despite the name, nobody actually quits: an employee simply stops going above and beyond and does the job as written, no more. For some managers that sounds alarming. For others it sounds like a healthy boundary. This guide explains what quiet quitting is, what usually causes it, how to tell it apart from burnout and low performance, and how to respond in a way that rebuilds engagement instead of damaging trust.

What is quiet quitting?

Quiet quitting is a reduction in discretionary effort. The person still does the tasks in their job description and meets the basic requirements, but they stop doing unpaid extras: staying late, taking on stretch work, answering messages at night or volunteering for projects. Strictly speaking, that is simply working to contract. What worries managers is the change: someone who used to contribute ideas and energy now does the minimum.

What causes it?

Quiet quitting is rarely about laziness. It is usually a signal that something in the working relationship has stopped adding up. The most common causes are:

  • Unclear expectations: people do not know what good looks like, so they protect themselves by doing only what is clearly asked.
  • Unsustainable workload: when extra effort becomes the default, people eventually withdraw it.
  • Lack of recognition: going the extra mile without acknowledgment teaches people to stop.
  • No growth path: if effort does not lead to development, pay or progression, the incentive disappears.
  • Weak relationship with the manager: people rarely disengage from a manager they trust and feel heard by.

How is it different from burnout or low performance?

The three can look similar from a distance, but they call for very different responses.

Quiet quitting

Output meets the standard, but initiative and enthusiasm are gone. The person is typically calm and consistent, often with a clear boundary around their time.

Burnout

Exhaustion is the main feature. Quality and reliability may slip, the person seems drained or cynical, and you may see more sick days or visible stress. Burnout needs relief and support, not motivation techniques.

Low performance

Work falls below the agreed standard. This is a capability, clarity or conduct issue, and it is addressed through a performance process with specific, documented expectations.

You cannot tell which one you are facing by guessing. You find out by looking at objective evidence, such as goals and output, and then by asking.

How to start the conversation

Choose a private, unhurried one-to-one and open with curiosity rather than accusation. Instead of "You seem checked out", try describing what you have observed and asking for their view.

  • Share a specific, neutral observation: "I've noticed you've stepped back from the planning meetings."
  • Ask open questions: "How are you finding the workload? What would make your role more rewarding?"
  • Listen without defending. Take notes, and do not argue with their experience.
  • Agree on one or two concrete next steps and a date to follow up.

Practical steps managers can take

  • Clarify expectations: write down goals, priorities and what "good" looks like for the role.
  • Rebalance workload: check real hours and task load, and remove or defer something when you add something.
  • Recognize specifically: thank people for particular contributions, soon after they happen.
  • Hold regular one-to-ones: short, frequent check-ins catch disengagement much earlier than annual reviews.
  • Offer growth: discuss development goals, training and realistic next roles.
  • Respect boundaries: make working hours and response times explicit, and model them yourself.
  • Look at the team: if several people are disengaging, the cause is probably structural, not personal.

What not to do

  • Do not punish boundaries. Penalizing someone for working their contracted hours drives out your best people and the rest of the team notices.
  • Do not label or gossip. "Quiet quitter" is a judgment, not a diagnosis. Keep conversations factual and private.
  • Do not rely on surveillance. Monitoring activity tends to deepen distrust and does not restore motivation.
  • Do not skip the conversation. Waiting and hoping lets a small problem harden into a resignation.
  • Do not confuse it with a performance case. If the work meets the standard, a warning is the wrong tool.

Treat quiet quitting as information. It tells you where expectations, workload or recognition are out of balance, and fixing those is usually good for the whole team, not just one person. Keeping goals, one-to-one notes and follow-ups in one place also makes it far easier to spot patterns early.

Frequently asked questions

What is quiet quitting?

Quiet quitting is when an employee stops doing unpaid extra effort and sticks strictly to the duties of their role. They still do the job, but no longer go above and beyond.

Is quiet quitting the same as burnout?

No. Quiet quitting is a deliberate pull-back of extra effort, while burnout is exhaustion that often hurts quality and wellbeing. Burnout needs support and relief; quiet quitting needs a conversation about expectations and engagement.

How should a manager bring it up?

Hold a private one-to-one, describe a specific observation without judgment, ask open questions about workload and motivation, listen, and agree on concrete next steps with a follow-up date.

Should managers discipline quiet quitters?

Not if the work meets the agreed standard. Working your contracted role is not misconduct. If output falls below the standard, address that through a clear performance process instead.

How can companies prevent quiet quitting?

Set clear expectations, keep workloads sustainable, recognize good work specifically, hold regular one-to-ones and offer real growth opportunities.
