What Is Pay Compression and How Do You Fix It on a Budget?

Pay compression happens when new hires earn close to, or more than, experienced staff. Learn how to spot it, fix it on a budget and explain it to your team.

November 13, 2026 · 6 min read

You hire a new team member at the going market rate, and a few weeks later your most experienced person in the same role finds out the newcomer earns almost as much as they do. Sometimes more. Nobody planned it, yet it feels unfair, and it often triggers a resignation. This is pay compression. Here is what causes it, how to find it in your own payroll and how to fix it without blowing the budget.

What is pay compression?

Pay compression is a narrowing of the pay gap between people who should be paid differently: new hires and long-serving staff, junior and senior employees, or team members and their managers. When the gap closes completely and a newer or less senior person earns more, it is called pay inversion.

A small difference is not a problem in itself. Compression becomes a problem when the gap no longer reflects real differences in experience, skill or responsibility.

Why does it happen?

  • Market rates move faster than raises. To attract candidates you pay today's rate, while current staff have received modest annual increases based on yesterday's rate.
  • Raises are a flat percentage. A 3% raise every year rarely keeps pace with a hiring market that jumps 8% in a tight year.
  • Counteroffers and one-off deals. Pay agreed under pressure during hiring or retention creates outliers nobody revisits.
  • Minimum wage increases. When the floor rises, entry-level pay moves up but the levels above it often stay put.
  • No salary structure. Without bands, every offer is a fresh negotiation, so pay drifts.

How do you spot pay compression?

You do not need specialist software for a first check. Export a simple list with each person's role, start date, level and base pay, then work through these steps.

1. Compare pay with tenure inside each role

Sort employees in the same role by start date. In a healthy structure pay tends to rise with experience and performance. If the most recent hires sit at or near the top of the list, you likely have compression.

2. Run a simple ratio check

Divide each person's pay by the pay of the newest hire in the same role. A seasoned employee at a ratio of 1.00 to 1.05 is earning almost exactly what a newcomer earns. Many small businesses treat anything below roughly 1.10 for someone with several years of strong performance as a flag worth reviewing. A ratio below 1.00 is inversion.

3. Check the manager gap

Compare each manager's pay with the highest-paid person who reports to them. If a supervisor earns barely more than their team, the extra responsibility is not being rewarded and few people will want the job.

4. Look for the human signals

Questions about how pay is decided, sudden disengagement among long-serving staff or exit interviews that mention fairness are often the first visible sign.

How do you fix it on a budget?

Most small businesses cannot correct everything at once. The goal is a credible plan that closes the worst gaps first.

  • Prioritize by risk. Start with inversions, then strong performers in hard-to-fill roles with the lowest ratios. These are the people most likely to leave and most costly to replace.
  • Phase the increases. Spread adjustments over two or three pay cycles, for example half now and half in six months. Tell people the timeline so the wait feels like a commitment, not a delay.
  • Separate adjustments from merit raises. A market or equity adjustment fixes the base; the normal performance raise still applies on top. Mixing them makes the correction look like a reward and the reward look like nothing.
  • Slow down new-hire offers. Before matching an aggressive candidate ask, check what it does to current staff. Sometimes a sign-on bonus solves the hiring problem without creating a permanent gap.
  • Build simple salary bands. Give each role a minimum, midpoint and maximum, and place people within the band based on experience and performance. Bands are the long-term cure, because every future offer and raise has a reference point.
  • Use non-salary levers carefully. Extra leave, training budgets or a title change can help while you phase pay, but they do not replace closing a real gap.

How do you talk to employees about it?

Silence is the most common mistake. If people have already noticed the gap, saying nothing reads as indifference. Keep the conversation honest and specific:

  • Acknowledge the issue plainly: the market moved and your pay structure did not keep up.
  • Explain what you reviewed and how adjustments are being decided, without sharing other people's salaries.
  • Give the person their own numbers: the new figure, the timeline and how future raises will work.
  • Commit to a regular review, for example once a year against market data, so it does not happen again.

If you cannot adjust someone yet, say so and give a date for the next review. A clear "not now, here is when" builds more trust than a vague promise.

How do you prevent it from coming back?

Review pay against the market at least once a year, check every new offer against the current team before you send it, and keep bands up to date. Compression is rarely caused by one bad decision; it builds quietly over many small ones, so a short annual review catches it early.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between pay compression and pay inversion?

Compression is when the pay gap between experienced and newer or junior staff becomes too small. Inversion is when it reverses and the newer or less senior person earns more.

Is pay compression illegal?

Not in itself. It becomes a legal risk if the gaps line up with a protected characteristic such as gender or age, so review your data with that in mind.

How much should an experienced employee earn above a new hire?

There is no fixed rule. Many small businesses aim for a clear, explainable difference, often at least 10% for several years of strong performance in the same role.

Should I lower a new hire's offer to avoid compression?

Not if it means losing good candidates. It is usually better to pay the market rate and raise current staff, or use a one-off sign-on bonus.

How often should I check for pay compression?

At least once a year, and every time you make an offer above the current pay of people already in that role.
