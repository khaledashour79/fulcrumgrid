How Do You Run a Quarterly Business Review for a Small Business?

How to run a quarterly business review for a small business: its purpose, a simple agenda, the data to bring, how to decide and assign actions, and how to follow through.

October 12, 2026 · 6 min read

A quarterly business review (QBR) is a structured meeting, held every three months, where you step back from daily firefighting and ask three questions: what happened, why, and what should change next quarter. For a small business it does not need slides or a boardroom. It needs a fixed date, a short list of numbers, and the discipline to leave with decisions rather than opinions.

What is the purpose of a quarterly review?

Monthly check-ins catch problems; a quarterly review spots patterns. Three months is long enough for a trend to show and short enough to correct course before a bad habit becomes a bad year. A good review does four things:

  • Compares results with plan: revenue, margin, and cash against the targets you set last quarter.
  • Explains the gap: not just that sales fell, but which customers, products, or channels drove it.
  • Sets priorities: a handful of goals for the next 90 days, not twenty.
  • Assigns ownership: every action has one named person and a date.

A simple agenda for 90 minutes

Keep the meeting to one to two hours and send the agenda ahead of time. A workable order:

  • Last quarter's commitments (10 minutes): what you said you would do, and what actually got done.
  • Results against targets (20 minutes): walk through the key numbers without debating causes yet.
  • What worked and what did not (25 minutes): discuss causes, customers, and surprises.
  • Risks and opportunities (15 minutes): cash pressure, key-customer dependence, capacity, new openings.
  • Next quarter's priorities (15 minutes): choose, and cut, goals.
  • Actions and owners (5 minutes): read the list back before everyone leaves.

Invite only the people who own a result: for a small firm that may be you, a partner, and the heads of sales, operations, and finance.

What data should you bring?

Resist the urge to bring everything. Pick numbers that drive decisions, show the previous quarter and the same quarter last year where you can, and note where each figure came from.

  • Revenue and gross margin: by product, service line, or customer segment.
  • Cash and receivables: cash balance, cash flow, and an ageing view of unpaid invoices.
  • Customers: new, retained, and lost, plus your top five by revenue and how dependent you are on them.
  • Pipeline and conversion: leads, quotes, and win rate.
  • Costs and headcount: the biggest expense lines and any capacity strain.
  • Customer feedback: complaints, reviews, and repeat-order patterns.

Send the figures a day or two ahead. Time spent reading numbers aloud is time not spent deciding.

How do you turn findings into decisions?

The review fails if it ends with 'we should look into that'. For each significant finding, run it through three short questions.

Is it a problem, a symptom, or noise?

One slow month may be noise. A margin that has slipped three quarters in a row is a problem. Ask why before you propose a fix.

What is the smallest change that would help?

Raising prices on one service, dropping an unprofitable client, or adding a deposit requirement is often better than a company-wide overhaul.

Who owns it, and by when?

Write each action as a verb, an owner, and a date, for example 'Review pricing on the three lowest-margin services, Dana, by November 15.' Limit yourselves to three to five priorities; more than that and none of them will be finished.

How do you follow through?

Most of the value of a QBR is created after the meeting ends.

  • Circulate a one-page summary within 24 hours: results, decisions, actions, owners, and dates.
  • Check in monthly: a fifteen-minute review of progress against the list, not a second QBR.
  • Track outcomes, not activity: note whether the action moved the number it was meant to move.
  • Open the next review with the list: unfinished items get finished, re-scoped, or dropped on purpose.
  • Keep a running record: a simple document of each quarter's numbers and decisions makes patterns visible over time.

Frequently asked questions

What is a quarterly business review?

It is a structured meeting held every three months to compare results with plan, understand why they differ, set priorities for the next quarter, and assign owners to the resulting actions.

How long should a quarterly business review take?

For most small businesses, between 90 minutes and two hours is enough, provided the numbers are shared in advance so the meeting is spent on discussion and decisions.

Who should attend a quarterly review?

Only the people who own a result or will carry out an action: typically the owner or managing director and the leads for sales, operations, and finance.

How is a quarterly review different from a monthly meeting?

A monthly meeting tracks progress and fixes short-term issues. A quarterly review looks at trends, questions strategy and priorities, and resets goals for the next 90 days.

What if we do not complete the actions we set?

Treat it as information. Review why at the start of the next QBR, then finish, re-scope, or deliberately drop each item, and set fewer actions next time if you took on too many.
