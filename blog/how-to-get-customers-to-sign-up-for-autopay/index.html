How Do You Get More Customers to Sign Up for Autopay?

Customers rarely switch to autopay on their own. Learn why they hesitate and seven practical, low-pressure ways to raise sign-ups and get paid on time.

November 10, 2026 · 6 min read

Getting paid on time is far easier when customers do not have to remember anything. Autopay (automatic or recurring payment, sometimes set up as a direct debit) turns payment from “someone must act” into “it simply happens”. The catch is that most customers will not switch it on by themselves. This guide explains why they hesitate and the practical steps that raise autopay sign-ups without pressuring anyone.

Why is autopay worth the effort?

A manually paid invoice depends on a chain of small actions: someone opens the email, finds the payment details, gets approval and clicks pay. Every link in that chain is a chance to forget. Autopay removes the chain. Payments arrive on the due date, you send fewer reminders, and your cash forecast becomes more reliable. Customers gain too: no late fees, no missed deadlines and less admin.

Why do customers hesitate to sign up?

  • Loss of control. They worry about being charged the wrong amount, or at a bad moment.
  • Trust. They are unsure who can see or use their card or bank details.
  • Cash flow. They like to choose when money leaves their account.
  • Friction. The set-up form is long or asks for details they do not have at hand.
  • Inertia. They simply never got round to it.

Each tactic below answers one of these concerns.

Seven practical ways to raise autopay sign-ups

1. Ask at the right moment

The best moments are onboarding, when you agree terms, and right after a successful payment, when paying feels easy. Asking in the middle of a dispute or an overdue chase feels like pressure and gets refused.

2. Make autopay the default, not the only option

Pre-select autopay in the proposal or checkout, but give customers a clear way to choose manual payment. Defaults raise uptake noticeably, and a visible alternative keeps the offer honest.

3. Explain the benefit in their words

“Never miss a due date” works better than “this improves our collections”. Be specific: “We will charge the invoice total on the due date and email you a receipt.”

4. Offer a small incentive

A few extra days on payment terms, a small discount or a waived card fee can tip the decision. Compare the cost with what chasing late invoices costs you today; a modest incentive often pays for itself.

5. Remove friction

Use one short form, one link and a mobile-friendly page, and accept the payment methods your customers already use. Every extra field loses sign-ups. Even better, let customers enrol directly from the invoice they are already looking at.

6. Build in safeguards

  • A notice a few days before each charge.
  • A clear receipt after every payment.
  • An easy way to pause or cancel.
  • An approval step or cap for amounts above an agreed limit.

Safeguards can sound like they reduce sign-ups, but they remove the biggest fear, which is losing control. They usually increase enrolment.

7. Follow up with customers who have not enrolled

Add a short line to invoice emails and to your payment reminders, such as “Tired of tracking due dates? Set up autopay in a minute.” Ask again after a few on-time payments, once trust has been built.

What should the autopay message say?

Keep it short and plain: what will be charged, when, and how to stop. For example: “With autopay, we charge your invoice total on the due date. You will get a reminder three days before and a receipt afterwards, and you can pause or cancel at any time.” Avoid legal jargon and artificial urgency.

How do you measure whether it is working?

  • Enrolment rate: the share of active customers on autopay.
  • On-time rate: autopay customers compared with manual payers.
  • Days to pay: average time from invoice to cash for each group.
  • Failed payment rate: mostly expired cards and insufficient funds.

A failed autopay still needs follow-up. Set up an automatic retry, tell the customer straight away and offer a simple way to update their details.

The short version

Ask at a natural moment, make autopay the easy default, explain the benefit plainly, protect customers with notices and an easy exit, and measure the result. Done respectfully, autopay is good for both sides of the invoice.

Frequently asked questions

Why will customers not sign up for autopay?

Most worry about losing control over when and how much they are charged, are unsure who can access their payment details, or find the set-up form too long. Clear safeguards and a simple form address most of these.

When is the best time to ask a customer to enrol in autopay?

During onboarding, when you agree payment terms, or right after a successful payment. Avoid asking in the middle of a dispute or an overdue chase, because it feels like pressure.

Should autopay be opt-in or the default?

Pre-selecting autopay while keeping a clear manual option raises uptake and stays fair. Forcing it with no alternative tends to cost goodwill and sales.

Do incentives for autopay really work?

Often, yes. A small discount, a few extra days on terms or a waived card fee can tip the decision. Compare the cost with what chasing late invoices costs you today.

What happens when an autopay payment fails?

Retry automatically, notify the customer immediately and give them an easy way to update their card or bank details. A failed autopay should enter your normal reminder sequence.
