How Should You Handle Short Payments and Partial Payments?

Customers often pay less than the invoice total. Learn to tell short pays from partials, apply the cash correctly, and collect the remaining balance.

October 26, 2026 · 6 min read

Sooner or later a payment lands that does not match the invoice. The amount is lower than what you billed, the remittance note says nothing, and the invoice now sits half-open in your ledger. How you handle that moment decides whether the balance gets collected in days or quietly ages for months.

What is the difference between a short payment and a partial payment?

A short payment is an unplanned underpayment: the customer pays less than the invoice total, usually without agreeing it with you first. A partial payment is deliberate: you and the customer agreed to settle in instalments, or the customer told you in advance that they are paying part now and the rest later. The cash may look identical in the bank, but the right response is different. A partial payment on an agreed plan needs tracking. A short payment needs an explanation.

Why do customers short-pay?

Most short payments are not hostile. Common causes include:

  • A dispute: damaged goods, a wrong quantity, or a service the customer feels was not delivered in full.
  • Deductions: an early-payment discount taken after the window closed, a freight charge, or a rebate the customer believes it is owed.
  • Taxes and fees: withholding tax, or bank and transfer charges that were taken out of the amount sent.
  • Currency and rounding: exchange-rate differences that leave a few cents or dollars outstanding.
  • Plain error: a keying mistake, or paying an old version of the invoice.
  • Cash flow: the customer simply cannot pay the full amount right now and hopes you will not notice.

What should you do when the money arrives?

  • 1. Apply the cash straight away. Post the payment to the invoice it belongs to, even though it does not clear it. Leaving it as unapplied cash makes the customer look delinquent on the full amount and leads to embarrassing reminders.
  • 2. Find the reason. Check the remittance advice first. If it is silent, contact the customer quickly and ask a neutral question: what does this payment cover?
  • 3. Decide how to treat the gap. If the customer is right, issue a credit note and close the invoice. If you are right, ask for the rest. If it is a genuine partial payment, agree the dates of the remaining instalments.
  • 4. Put it in writing. Record the reason, who agreed what, and the new due date on the account. A verbal promise that nobody wrote down is not a promise.
  • 5. Follow up the balance on a date. The open amount should have its own reminder schedule, not be lost inside the original invoice history.

How should you respond to a disputed shortfall?

Treat the dispute and the undisputed part separately. If a customer says one line item is wrong, they still owe the rest, so ask for payment of the undisputed amount and work the disputed line on its own track. Bring in the sales or delivery team early, because they usually hold the proof that settles it.

Should you ever write off a small shortfall?

Sometimes, yes. Chasing a two-dollar bank-fee difference can cost more in staff time than the amount itself. Many finance teams set a small tolerance threshold, for example under a fixed amount or under one percent of the invoice, below which the difference is written off automatically and logged. Keep two safeguards: the threshold should be approved by someone senior, and repeated shortfalls from the same customer should be reviewed, because a pattern of small deductions can add up to a real loss or signal a problem with your pricing or delivery.

What mistakes make short payments worse?

  • Ignoring them until month-end. The customer has forgotten the context and the trail has gone cold.
  • Marking the invoice as paid. This hides the open balance and breaks your reporting.
  • Accusing the customer. Most underpayments are errors or deductions. Start with a question, not a demand.
  • Accepting deductions silently. If you never push back, customers learn that taking a discount they are not entitled to costs them nothing.
  • Not tracking the cause. Without a reason code, you cannot see that most of your shortfalls come from the same pricing or shipping problem.

Frequently asked questions

What is a short payment on an invoice?

A short payment is when a customer pays less than the invoice total, usually without prior agreement. The cause may be a dispute, a deduction, bank fees, or a simple mistake.

Should I mark an invoice as paid after a partial payment?

No. Apply the payment to the invoice but leave it open for the remaining balance. Marking it paid hides what you are still owed and breaks your aging and cash reports.

Can I write off a small shortfall?

Yes, if you set a clear tolerance threshold approved by a senior finance person and log each write-off. Review repeated shortfalls from the same customer rather than writing them all off.

How do I find out why a customer paid less?

Start with the remittance advice, then ask the customer a neutral question about what the payment covers. Involve sales or delivery if a dispute about the goods or service is involved.
