What Is a Lockbox and Does a Small Business Need One?

A lockbox is a bank service that collects and deposits your customers' paper checks for you. Here is how it works, what it costs and when it is worth it.

November 4, 2026 · 6 min read

If your bank or a vendor has ever pitched you a “lockbox”, you may have wondered whether it is a clever cash-flow tool or something built for much bigger companies. This explainer answers the most common questions about lockboxes, remittance processing and whether a small business needs one.

What is a lockbox?

A lockbox is a payment-collection service, usually run by a bank. Customers mail their checks and remittance slips to a special post office box instead of to your office. The bank (or a processor) empties the box several times a day, opens the envelopes, scans the checks and payment details, deposits the funds into your account and sends you the data you need to apply each payment to the right invoice. The process of handling those payments and the information that comes with them is called remittance processing.

How does a lockbox work, step by step?

  • You change the remit-to address on your invoices to the lockbox address.
  • Customers mail payments there, usually a check plus a remittance stub or invoice copy.
  • The bank collects the mail and opens it, often several times a day.
  • Checks are scanned and deposited directly into your business account.
  • You receive a file or portal report with check images, amounts, payer names and invoice numbers.
  • You match payments to invoices in your accounting or invoicing system.

Wholesale versus retail lockbox

A wholesale lockbox handles a modest number of larger business payments that often need careful invoice matching. A retail lockbox handles high volumes of small, standardized payments, such as utility bills or subscriptions, using scannable coupons. Most small businesses that consider a lockbox are looking at the wholesale kind.

What are the benefits of a lockbox?

  • Faster deposits. Checks are processed the day they arrive, with no waiting for someone to visit the bank.
  • Less handling and fraud exposure. Fewer people touch paper checks inside your office.
  • Cleaner data. Digital images and payment details make reconciliation easier and create an audit trail.
  • Continuity. Payments keep flowing when staff are away or the office is closed.

What does a lockbox cost?

Pricing varies by bank, but it usually combines a monthly service fee, a per-item fee for each check processed and sometimes a setup charge or extra fees for exceptions and data files. Many providers expect a minimum volume, so a business receiving only a handful of checks a month may find the fixed cost hard to justify. Always ask for a full fee schedule and compare it with the staff time you would save.

Does a small business need a lockbox?

Probably not, unless paper checks are a big part of your income. A lockbox tends to make sense when:

  • you receive dozens or hundreds of checks every month;
  • your customers are slow to move away from paper, such as some government, healthcare or older commercial clients;
  • you have no one reliable to process mail and deposits each day;
  • your team spends hours matching payments to invoices by hand.

It is probably overkill when most of your customers already pay by bank transfer or card, when you only get a few checks a month, or when a mobile deposit app takes care of them in minutes.

What are the alternatives for a small business?

  • Mobile or remote check deposit. Photograph the check and deposit it from your phone or desk scanner, usually for little or no cost.
  • Online payment links. A pay-now link on each invoice lets customers pay by bank transfer or card, so the check never gets written.
  • Dedicated bank details or virtual accounts. Clear payment references make electronic payments easy to match.
  • A shared mailbox policy. Pick a set day and person for opening mail, depositing checks and recording payments.

For most small businesses, the cheapest improvement is not faster handling of checks but fewer checks to handle.

What should you check before signing up?

  • The full fee schedule, including minimums and exception fees.
  • How quickly funds are credited and how fast data reaches you.
  • Whether the data file or portal fits your accounting software.
  • How the provider handles damaged, post-dated or mismatched checks.
  • How long you can access check images and how to cancel.

What should you do next?

Count how many checks you received last quarter and how long each one took from mailbox to matched invoice. If the number is small, start with mobile deposit and pay links. If it is large and growing, ask two or three banks for lockbox quotes. Either way, try to reduce paper over time. This article is general information, not financial advice.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a lockbox and a P.O. box?

A P.O. box is a mailbox you or your staff empty yourself. A bank lockbox is emptied by the bank or a processor, who deposits the checks and sends you the payment data.

How much does a bank lockbox cost?

Typically a monthly fee plus a per-check fee, and sometimes setup or exception charges. Prices vary by bank, so request a full fee schedule and compare it with your volume.

Is a lockbox the same as remittance processing?

Not exactly. Remittance processing is the work of handling payments and their data. A lockbox is one service that does that work for you, centered on a mailing address.

Can I use mobile check deposit instead of a lockbox?

Yes, if you get only a modest number of checks. Mobile deposit is usually free or cheap, though it still needs someone to handle the checks and match them to invoices.

How can I reduce the number of checks I receive?

Put a pay link and bank details on each invoice, list checks last, and offer autopay or a small early-payment discount for repeat clients.
