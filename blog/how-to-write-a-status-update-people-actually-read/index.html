How Do You Write a Weekly Status Update People Read?

A status update only works if people read it. Learn to lead with the answer, report RAG status honestly, and use a short outcome, progress, risks and asks format.

November 3, 2026 · 6 min read

Most weekly status updates are written to prove effort, not to help a reader decide anything. They run long, bury the one important sentence in paragraph six, and get skimmed or ignored. The fix is not better prose. It is a better structure and a stricter idea of who the reader is and what they need to do next.

Lead with the answer

Your reader is busy and has several of these to get through. Give them the conclusion in the first two lines: is the project on track, and does anything need them? Everything below is evidence for that answer, not a replacement for it.

A strong opening looks like this: "Amber: launch is still 14 November, but we need a decision on the vendor contract by Thursday." A weak one reads: "This week the team continued working on several workstreams." The first tells the reader what to feel and what to do. The second tells them nothing.

Be honest with RAG status

Red, amber and green only help if they mean the same thing every week. Agree on definitions and write them at the top of your template:

  • Green: on track for scope, date and budget, with no help needed.
  • Amber: at risk, but you have a plan and expect to recover without outside help.
  • Red: off track, or you cannot recover without a decision, money, people or a change of scope.

The most common failure is the "watermelon" report: green on the outside, red on the inside. People do it to avoid worrying anyone, but it only delays the bad news until it is harder and more expensive to fix. A team that turns amber early and asks for help is doing its job. A team that goes from green to red in one week has been hiding something.

Use a four-part structure

A reliable skeleton keeps every update short and lets readers find what they need in seconds. Use the same four headings every week.

1. Outcome

One or two sentences: overall status, the headline, and the next milestone with its date. This is the part that gets read by everyone.

2. Progress

Three to five bullets of what was finished since last time, written as results rather than activity. "Completed user testing with 12 customers" beats "worked on user testing". Mention what is planned next week in one line.

3. Risks

List only the risks that could move the date, the budget or the quality. For each one, give the impact, the likelihood, who owns it and what you are doing about it. Three real risks are more useful than ten vague ones.

4. Asks

This is the section most updates forget, and it is why they get read without anything happening. Name the decision or help you need, who needs to provide it and by when. If you have no asks, say so. A clear "No asks this week" is also useful information.

Put numbers on it

Numbers turn opinion into evidence. Instead of "good progress on onboarding", write "onboarding completion rose from 58% to 71%, against a target of 75%". Pick three to five measures that matter for the project, track the same ones every week, and show the direction of change as well as the value. A small trend line, or simply "up 4 points on last week", tells the reader more than a long paragraph.

Always give a number its context: the target, the previous period, or the budget. A figure on its own, such as "we spent 40,000", forces the reader to do the comparison.

Pick a cadence and keep it

Weekly is the default for active projects, and the day matters less than the consistency. Send it at the same time, ideally before the meeting where it will be discussed, so people arrive informed and the meeting can be used for decisions. For slower work, a fortnightly update is fine. For a crisis, move to daily short notes until it settles. When updates arrive late or skip a week, readers stop expecting them and stop reading.

Use a template

A template removes the blank-page problem and makes updates comparable over time. Keep it to one page and include these fields:

  • Project name, author and date
  • Status (RAG) with a one-line reason
  • Outcome and next milestone
  • Progress, risks and asks
  • Key numbers with targets

Store it where the team already works, and write each update by copying last week's and editing it. That makes it easy to see what changed.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Burying the lead. The status and the ask belong at the top, not at the end.
  • Listing activity instead of results. Readers care what changed, not how busy you were.
  • Jargon and acronyms. Write for someone who has not been in your meetings.
  • Hiding bad news. Surprises damage trust far more than honest amber.
  • Vague asks. "We could use some support" gets nothing; "Approve two extra contractors by Friday" gets action.
  • Changing the format every week. Readers learn where to look only if it stays the same.

How long should it be?

Aim for something readable in two minutes: roughly 150 to 300 words, or one screen. If the reader needs more detail, link to it instead of pasting it in. Detail is for the people who want it, and the summary is for everyone else. When in doubt, cut. If a sentence does not change what the reader thinks or does, it does not belong in the update.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a weekly status update be?

Aim for 150 to 300 words, which is about two minutes of reading or one screen. Link to detailed documents rather than pasting them in, so the summary stays short.

What is the difference between amber and red?

Amber means the project is at risk but you have a plan to recover on your own. Red means it is off track and you cannot recover without a decision, extra resources or a change of scope.

Should I send a status update if nothing changed?

Yes, keep it short. A brief 'No change, still green, no asks' confirms that things are fine and keeps the habit that makes readers trust the update.

Who should receive the update?

Send it to the people who make decisions or depend on the work, and keep the list small. Anyone who only wants occasional visibility can read it in a shared location instead.
