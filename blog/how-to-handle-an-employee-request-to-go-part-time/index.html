How Should You Handle an Employee Request to Go Part-Time?

An employee wants to go part-time? Learn how to run the first talk, assess business impact, weigh options and put pro-rated pay and leave changes in writing.

November 22, 2026 · 6 min read

A valued team member asks for a quiet word and says they would like to go part-time. For a small business this can feel unsettling: you rely on that person, and every hour counts. Yet a part-time request handled well often keeps an experienced employee you would otherwise lose. This Q&A walks through the practical steps, from the first conversation to the signed contract change. It is general information, not legal advice, and employment rules vary by country, so check the details locally before you decide.

Why do employees ask to go part-time?

The reasons are usually personal and often temporary:

  • Caregiving. A new baby, a child starting school or an ageing parent who needs support.
  • Study. A degree, a professional qualification or retraining alongside work.
  • Health. Managing a long-term condition or returning gradually after illness.
  • Phased retirement. Older employees who want to ease out of full-time work rather than stop overnight.

Knowing the reason helps you find a workable solution, but you do not need every private detail. Focus on what they need, not on justifying why.

How should I handle the first conversation?

Listen first and avoid answering on the spot. Thank the employee for raising it early and ask practical questions: how many hours or days they have in mind, which days, from what date, and whether they see it as permanent or for a set period. Ask them to put the request in writing, even a short email, so you both start from the same version. Then agree a date by which you will reply. A clear timeline, usually two to four weeks, shows you are taking the request seriously and stops it from drifting.

How do I assess the impact on the business?

Look at the role, not the person. Work through three questions:

  • Coverage. Which hours or days would be left uncovered, and do they matter? A gap on a quiet Friday afternoon is not the same as a gap during your busiest shift.
  • Workload redistribution. Which tasks could move to colleagues, which could be dropped or simplified, and would anyone end up overloaded? Talk to the team before assuming they can absorb more.
  • Client needs. Do customers count on reaching this person at certain times? Could a handover routine or a shared inbox fill the gap?

Write down what you find. If you later say no, you will need a genuine business reason; if you say yes, you will already know what has to change.

What options can I offer besides a simple yes or no?

  • Reduced hours. Shorter days or fewer days each week, on a fixed pattern everyone knows.
  • Compressed schedule. If the real need is a free day rather than fewer hours, the employee could work their full hours over fewer, longer days. That keeps them full-time, so treat it as a different arrangement.
  • Job share. Two part-time people split one role, with a clear handover between them.
  • Trial period. Agree the new pattern for, say, three months, with a review date. It lowers the risk for both sides and lets you decide on evidence rather than guesswork.

What actually changes when someone goes part-time?

  • Pay. Salary is normally pro-rated to the new hours. Check how bonuses, allowances and overtime thresholds are affected.
  • Paid time off. Leave entitlement is usually pro-rated too. Settle what was accrued at the full-time rate and keep it separate from the new accrual.
  • Benefits. Some benefits, such as health cover or pension contributions, may have minimum-hours eligibility rules. Tell the employee clearly what will change before they commit.
  • Contract amendment. Record the new hours, days, pay, leave and start date in a written amendment that you both sign. For a trial, state the review date and what happens afterwards.

How do I stay fair and consistent?

Treat similar requests in a similar way. If you approve reduced hours for one person, be ready to explain why you might decline a comparable request from someone else. Base the decision on the needs of the role, not on the reason given or on assumptions about commitment. Part-time staff should keep the same access to training, promotion and interesting work. Keep a short record of each request: what was asked, what you considered, what you decided and why.

How do I say no well?

Sometimes the answer has to be no, at least for now. Say it in person, then confirm it in writing. Give the specific business reason, such as a coverage gap you cannot fill, rather than a vague "it will not work". Explain what you looked at and offer any alternative you can, such as a later start date, a trial in a quieter season or a different pattern. Tell the employee when they can ask again. A considered no keeps trust; a dismissive one often ends in a resignation.

Is there a legal right to request part-time work?

In some countries, yes. Several give employees a statutory right to request reduced hours, often after a qualifying period, and require employers to respond within a set time and refuse only for recognised business reasons. Many also prohibit treating part-time staff less favourably than full-time colleagues. Check what applies where you operate before you reply.

How do I keep track once it is agreed?

The short version

Listen, ask for the request in writing, assess coverage and workload, consider a trial, put every change in a signed amendment and handle similar requests consistently. Done well, a part-time request can keep good people in your business.

Frequently asked questions

Do I have to agree to a part-time request?

Not automatically, but in some countries employees have a statutory right to request reduced hours and you must consider it properly and refuse only for genuine business reasons. Check the rules where you operate.

How is paid time off calculated after a switch to part-time?

Leave is usually pro-rated to the new hours. Settle what was already accrued at the full-time rate separately, then apply the new accrual rate from the change date.

Is a trial period a good idea?

Often, yes. A trial of around three months with a fixed review date lets both sides test the new pattern and decide on evidence. Put the trial terms in writing.

Does the employment contract need to be changed?

Yes. Record the new hours, days, pay, leave and start date in a written amendment signed by both sides, and keep it on file with the original request.
