How Should You Handle an Employee Request for an Internal Transfer?

Learn why employees ask to transfer internally, how to run a fair process, handle reluctant managers, say not yet well and settle pay, title and paperwork.

November 25, 2026 · 6 min read

Sooner or later a good employee will ask to move to another team, location or role. Yet a transfer request usually means something valuable: a person who wants to stay and is telling you how. Handled well, it keeps talent in the business; handled badly, it often ends in a resignation.

Why do employees ask for an internal transfer?

The reason shapes the right response, so ask first. Common drivers are:

  • Growth. They have learned what the current role can teach and want new skills or a clearer path upward.
  • Friction with their manager. A difficult working relationship is one of the most common unspoken reasons. A transfer can be the healthiest fix, but the signal is worth looking into.
  • Burnout. Heavy workload or repetitive work can make a fresh team look like the only way out.
  • Relocation or personal circumstances. A house move, a partner’s new job or caring duties may make another site or a remote-friendly team the practical option.
  • Better skills fit. Some people are simply better suited to work they have seen another team doing.

Why is saying yes often better than losing them?

An employee who cannot move inside the company will often move outside it. Replacing someone can cost a large share of their annual salary in recruitment, onboarding and lost productivity, and a new hire arrives without knowledge of your customers, systems and culture. A transfer keeps that knowledge, fills a role with someone whose track record you already know and shows the rest of the team that careers can grow internally.

What does a fair internal transfer process look like?

Even a small business benefits from a simple written routine, so decisions do not depend on who asks.

  • 1. A written request. Ask for a short note naming the team or role, the reason and the preferred timing.
  • 2. Clear eligibility rules. Many companies expect a minimum time in the current role, often six to twelve months, and satisfactory performance with no active formal warning. Publish the rules and apply them to everyone.
  • 3. Conversations with both managers. The current manager explains the business impact and timing; the receiving manager confirms there is a real need and a budget for the role.
  • 4. A skills and gap assessment. Compare the person’s skills with the new role’s requirements. Note gaps and whether training or shadowing can close them.
  • 5. Timing and a backfill plan. Agree a start date that gives the current team time to recruit, redistribute work or cover the gap. Four to eight weeks is common.
  • 6. A proper handover. Ask the employee to document open work, contacts and recurring tasks before moving, and to stay reachable for a few weeks afterwards.

What if the current manager does not want to let them go?

Reluctance is natural: nobody wants to lose a strong performer mid-quarter. But blocking a move to keep a good employee, often called talent hoarding, usually backfires. The person feels stuck, motivation drops and they may leave altogether. Separate a legitimate concern about timing from an objection to the move itself. Timing can be negotiated with a later start date or a phased handover; a permanent veto should need the agreement of a senior manager or HR. It also helps to recognise managers whose people move on to bigger roles, so developing talent counts in their favour.

How do you say no or not yet?

Sometimes the answer has to be no: the role is not open, the skills gap is too wide or the person does not yet meet the eligibility rules. Say so promptly, in person, with the real reason, then turn the refusal into a plan. Agree on the specific skills or results needed, the support you will provide and a date to review the request again. A “not yet” with a clear path forward keeps the employee engaged; a vague “maybe later” invites them to look elsewhere.

What happens to pay, title and probation on transfer?

  • Pay. Set pay according to the range for the new role, not the old salary. A sideways move usually keeps pay the same; a move into a higher-graded role may come with an increase. A move to a lower band should be discussed openly and agreed in writing.
  • Title. Give the title that matches the new role, consistent with colleagues doing the same job.
  • Probation or trial period. Some businesses use a short trial in the new role, with an agreed option to return if it does not work out. Check what your contracts and local law allow, and keep continuous service and accrued leave unaffected.

How should you document an internal transfer?

Record the request, the decision and the reasons behind it, the agreed start date, and the new title, manager, pay and any trial terms. Issue a written confirmation or contract amendment signed by both sides. Update the employee record, org chart, system access and payroll on the effective date. Good records keep future decisions consistent and protect you if one is questioned, so keep the request, approvals and role change with the employee’s file, not scattered across emails.

Treat internal transfers as a normal part of keeping good people, not as a favour or a threat. A clear process and a written record let you say yes more often and explain no when you must.

Frequently asked questions

How long should an employee be in a role before requesting a transfer?

Many companies set a minimum of six to twelve months, so the person has delivered in the current role and the team has seen a return on their training. Whatever period you choose, publish it and apply it to everyone.

Can a manager block an internal transfer?

A manager can reasonably ask to delay a move to allow a proper handover or backfill, but a permanent block should need approval from a senior manager or HR. Routine blocking tends to push good employees to leave.

Does pay change when an employee transfers internally?

Pay should follow the pay range of the new role. Sideways moves usually keep the same salary, higher-graded roles may bring a rise, and any reduction should be discussed openly and agreed in writing.

Should an internal transfer include a trial period?

A short trial with an agreed option to return can reduce risk for both sides, as long as your contracts and local law allow it. Length of service and accrued leave should carry over unchanged.
