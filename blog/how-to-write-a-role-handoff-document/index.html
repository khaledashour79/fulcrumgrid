How Do You Write a Role Handoff Document When Someone Leaves?

Learn how to write a role handoff document when someone leaves or changes jobs: what to include, how to structure it and the mistakes that lose knowledge.

November 1, 2026 · 6 min read

When someone leaves or moves to a new role, most of what they know walks out with them. Not the big things, which are usually written down somewhere, but the small ones: which supplier needs a phone call rather than an email, why a report runs on the third and not the first, what to do when the export fails. A good handoff document captures that knowledge while the person is still around to explain it. This guide answers the questions teams ask most.

What is a role handoff document?

A role handoff document is a written guide that lets a successor, or the rest of the team, take over a role without having to rediscover everything. It covers what the role is responsible for, what is in progress, who matters, where things live and what tends to go wrong. It is not a job description and it is not a farewell email. It is a working manual for the next person.

When should you start writing it?

As early as possible. Two weeks is the realistic minimum for most roles, and a month is better for anything involving customers, money or systems only one person understands. Starting early matters because the best material comes from questions, and questions need time. Treat the first draft as a conversation: the leaver writes what they remember, the successor or manager reads it and asks what is unclear.

What should it include?

Keep the structure the same for every role so documents are easy to compare and easy to find. A solid handoff covers these areas:

  • Role summary: the purpose of the role, who it reports to and the three or four outcomes it is judged on.
  • Recurring tasks: daily, weekly, monthly and yearly duties, each with its schedule, steps and where the output goes.
  • Open work: every project, request and promise in flight, with its status, next step and deadline.
  • People: internal stakeholders, customers, vendors and partners, with what each one expects and how they like to be contacted.
  • Systems and access: the tools, folders and accounts the role uses, who owns each login and what must be transferred. Never paste passwords into the document; point to your password manager instead.
  • Known problems: recurring failures, workarounds, risks and decisions that were deliberately postponed.
  • Hidden know-how: the unwritten rules, such as who approves exceptions and which deadlines are really flexible.

How do you write it step by step?

1. Brain-dump first, edit later

Start by listing everything you do in a typical month, using your calendar, sent mail and task list as prompts. Do not worry about order yet. People forget their invisible duties, such as the monthly reminder they send, until they see the evidence.

2. Turn the list into procedures

For each recurring task, write the steps as someone who has never done it would need them. Add a screenshot or a link to the tool where it helps. If a step takes judgment, explain how you decide, not just what you do.

3. Make open work explicit

For each item in progress, state the current status, the next action, the deadline and who is waiting. Mark what is at risk. This section is the one the team will read first, so keep it current right up to the last day.

4. Introduce people, not just names

A contact list is useful, but a short note beats it: “Maria in finance approves anything over 5,000, answers fastest on chat, and dislikes surprises at month end.” Where possible, arrange a short introduction call before the leaver goes.

5. Test it with the successor

Ask the person taking over to walk through one recurring task using only the document. Every place they get stuck is a gap. Fix it while the author can still answer.

What mistakes make handoffs fail?

  • Writing it on the last day. Rushed documents list tasks but skip reasoning and context.
  • Documenting only what is easy. The tedious, rare and risky tasks are exactly what a successor needs most.
  • Having no owner. Someone, usually the manager, should be responsible for reviewing and storing the document.
  • Leaving it in a personal folder. Store it where the team can find it, and tell them where.
  • Treating it as finished. The first month after the transition will reveal gaps, so schedule a check-in at two to four weeks.

How do you keep handoffs from becoming a crisis?

The best handoff is the one you maintain continuously. If every role keeps a living document of its recurring tasks and open work, a departure becomes a review rather than an excavation. Some teams keep this in a shared wiki; others track the work itself in a tool. If you already run operations in FulcrumGrid Command Center, you can keep owners, open projects and recurring tasks in one dashboard, so reassigning a role means changing the owner rather than rebuilding the picture. A shared document with a clear template works well too; consistency matters more than the tool.

Start this week: choose one role, block an hour, and write the role summary and the list of open work. Even that half page will be worth more than nothing, and it makes the full handoff far easier later.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a role handoff document be?

Long enough to cover the areas above, short enough to be read. Most roles fit in three to eight pages. Use links for detail, and put the open work and the most urgent warnings at the top.

Who is responsible for writing it?

The person leaving writes the first draft, but the manager owns the outcome. The manager reviews it, assigns the successor and makes sure time is set aside, because handoff work rarely fits around a full workload.

What if the person leaves with little notice?

Schedule a short interview as soon as you can and record or take notes. Then rebuild from their calendar, sent mail, shared folders and task lists, and ask their colleagues and contacts what they relied on the person for.

Should a handoff document include passwords?

No. List which systems the role uses and who owns each account, then transfer access through your password manager or an admin. Passwords in documents are a security risk and go stale quickly.

Do you need a handoff document when someone just changes teams?

Yes. The person is still reachable, which helps, but their old duties still need an owner. A lighter version covering recurring tasks, open work and key contacts is usually enough.
