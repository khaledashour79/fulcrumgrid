How Should You Respond When Employees Compare Salaries?

Your staff found out they are paid differently. Learn how to respond calmly, check whether the gap is justified, and then explain or correct it fairly.

November 11, 2026 · 6 min read

Sooner or later it happens: two employees compare payslips, and one of them comes to you upset. In a small business this moment feels personal, because you probably set both salaries yourself. How you respond in the next few days matters more than the gap itself. This guide covers what to say, how to check whether the difference is fair, and how to stop the same surprise from happening again.

Can you stop employees from discussing pay?

In most cases, no, and you should not try. In many countries employees have a legal right to discuss their own pay with colleagues, and rules that forbid it can be unlawful. In the United States, for example, most private-sector employees are protected when they talk about wages. In the European Union, the Pay Transparency Directive, which member states had until June 2026 to bring into national law, strengthens the right to pay information and rules out clauses that stop staff from disclosing their pay. Details differ by country, and not every country moves at the same speed, so check your local law or ask an employment adviser before you put anything about pay confidentiality into a contract or handbook.

What should you say in the first conversation?

Your first job is to listen, not to defend the number. The employee usually wants to know two things: whether they are valued and whether the process is fair.

  • Thank them for raising it. Coming to you directly is far better than resentment spreading quietly.
  • Do not discuss the colleague’s pay. Talk about their own salary and how pay is set in general. The other person’s details stay private.
  • Do not promise a raise on the spot. Say you will review it properly and give a date, ideally within one or two weeks.
  • Avoid blaming anyone. Comments such as “they negotiated harder” or “you never asked” only make it worse.

A simple script works: “Thank you for telling me. I want to check this properly against how we set pay for your role, and I will come back to you by Friday next week with what I find.”

How do you check whether the gap is justified?

Pull the facts for both roles and compare them honestly. A difference is usually justified when it rests on factors you would apply to anyone:

  • Role and responsibilities. Are they really doing the same job, or does one person manage people, a budget or key clients?
  • Level. Is one person more senior in your structure?
  • Relevant experience and skills. Years in the field, qualifications or specialist skills the role actually uses.
  • Performance. Documented results over time, not a general impression.
  • Market timing at hire. Someone hired when candidates were scarce may have needed a higher offer. That can explain a gap at the start, but it should narrow over time rather than last forever.

Then ask the harder question: would the gap still look fair if it were written down and shown to both people? If the honest answer depends on who negotiated harder, who asked for a raise or who you happen to know better, the gap is probably not justified. Also check whether it lines up with gender, age or another protected characteristic. If it does, take it seriously and consider getting professional advice.

How do you explain a justified gap?

Be specific about the criteria, not about the colleague. Explain what drives pay for their role, where they sit today and what would move them up: a skill to build, a responsibility to take on or a performance level to reach by the next review. Put that path in writing and agree a date to check progress. People accept a difference much more easily when they can see how to close it themselves.

What if the gap is not justified?

Correct it. Leaving an unfair gap in place because you are worried about cost only delays the problem, and the employee is likely to leave or tell others.

  • Tell the employee clearly. Admit the gap was not right and say what you will do about it.
  • Adjust pay with a date. Correct it at once if you can. If the budget cannot absorb it, phase it in over two or three pay periods and put the schedule in writing.
  • Look for the same pattern elsewhere. One unfair gap often means others. Review everyone in similar roles, not just the person who spoke up.
  • Plan the budget. Set aside a small pay-equity amount in each annual budget, so corrections do not have to wait for a crisis.

How do you prevent the next surprise?

  • Use salary bands. Give each role a minimum and a maximum, and place everyone inside the band for their role.
  • Document every pay decision. Record the salary, the date and the reason for each hire, raise and promotion. A short note is enough.
  • Apply the same criteria to everyone. Decide what moves pay, such as level, skills and performance, and use those factors every time.
  • Review once a year. Compare people in the same roles side by side and fix drift before anyone else notices it.

Common mistakes

  • Reacting angrily or asking who told them. It damages trust and, in many places, may also break the law.
  • Granting an instant raise to end the conversation. It rewards complaining rather than fixing the system, and others will notice.
  • Fixing one salary and ignoring the rest. The next comparison will uncover the same problem.

Frequently asked questions

Is it illegal to tell employees not to discuss their salaries?

In many countries it can be. The United States and the European Union, among others, protect employees who discuss pay in many situations. Rules vary, so check local law before adding any confidentiality clause.

Should I tell an employee what their colleague earns?

No. Keep other people’s pay private. Explain how pay is set for their role, where they currently sit and what would move them higher.

What if I cannot afford to fix an unfair gap right away?

Tell the employee it will be corrected, agree a written schedule over a few pay periods and set aside a pay-equity budget so future corrections are planned.

Is negotiation a valid reason for paying someone more?

Not on its own. It can explain a starting offer, but if two people do the same job at the same level, the gap should close over time through consistent criteria.
