What Is a SIPOC Diagram and How Do You Make One?

A SIPOC diagram maps suppliers, inputs, process, outputs and customers on one page. Learn when to use one and how to build it in under an hour.

November 13, 2026 · 6 min read

Most process problems start with a fuzzy picture. People argue about a step that is broken, but nobody agrees where the process begins, what goes into it or who depends on what comes out. A SIPOC diagram fixes that in one page. It is one of the simplest tools in process improvement, and it suits small teams well because it takes an hour, not a consultant.

What is a SIPOC diagram?

SIPOC stands for Suppliers, Inputs, Process, Outputs and Customers. It is a five-column table that describes a process at a high level:

  • Suppliers: the people, teams or companies that provide what the process needs.
  • Inputs: the materials, information or requests that enter the process.
  • Process: the five to seven main steps that turn inputs into outputs.
  • Outputs: what the process produces, such as a shipped order, a signed contract or a paid invoice.
  • Customers: whoever receives the outputs, inside or outside the business.

A SIPOC is not a detailed flowchart. It deliberately leaves out decisions, loops and exceptions. Its job is to agree on the boundaries and the big picture before anyone dives into detail.

When should a small team use one?

A SIPOC is worth the hour in a few common situations:

  • Before fixing a recurring problem. If orders keep shipping late, map the process first so the team agrees what is in scope.
  • When onboarding someone into a process. One page shows a new hire where their work comes from and where it goes.
  • Before writing an SOP. The SIPOC gives you the outline; the SOP fills in the steps.
  • When handoffs between teams cause friction. Listing suppliers and customers makes hidden dependencies visible.

If a process is tiny, owned by one person and working fine, you do not need one.

How to build a SIPOC in under an hour

Gather two to four people who do the work, plus anyone who receives the output. Use a whiteboard or a shared document with five columns. Then work in this order, which is not the order of the letters.

Step 1: Name the process and set its boundaries

Give it a short name and define a clear start trigger and end point, for example "starts when a customer places an order, ends when the order is delivered". Arguments about scope belong here, not later.

Step 2: List five to seven process steps

Start in the middle column. Write each step as a verb and a noun: "receive order", "check stock", "pick items", "pack and label", "ship", "confirm delivery". If you have more than seven, you are going too deep.

Step 3: Identify the outputs

What does the process produce? Include the main output and the important by-products, such as an invoice or a tracking notification.

Step 4: Identify the customers

For each output, name who receives it. Internal customers count: finance receives the invoice, the warehouse lead receives the stock update.

Step 5: Identify the inputs

Ask what each step needs to start: an order form, stock data, packaging, a courier booking.

Step 6: Identify the suppliers

Finally, name who provides each input. Suppliers can be customers, vendors, systems or other teams.

A worked example: customer order fulfilment

  • Suppliers: customer, online store, packaging vendor, courier.
  • Inputs: order details, payment confirmation, stock levels, boxes and labels, pickup slot.
  • Process: receive order, check stock, pick items, pack and label, hand to courier, confirm delivery.
  • Outputs: delivered order, tracking email, invoice, updated stock count.
  • Customers: end customer, finance team, warehouse lead.

Even this short map often surfaces something useful. A team might notice that stock levels come from a spreadsheet updated only on Fridays, which explains why "check stock" keeps failing midweek.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Going too detailed. Twenty steps is a flowchart, not a SIPOC. Keep it to one page.
  • Skipping the boundaries. Without a clear start and end, the map grows until it describes the whole company.
  • Mapping the ideal, not the reality. Describe how the work actually happens today.
  • Leaving out internal customers. Many delays come from outputs nobody realised another team depended on.
  • Filing it away. A SIPOC is a starting point. If nothing happens next, the hour was wasted.

What to do after the SIPOC

Use the map to decide what to measure and what to document. Pick one or two metrics for the outputs customers care about most, such as on-time delivery rate or order cycle time. Then turn the process column into a short SOP, and draw a detailed flowchart only for the step that causes the most trouble. Revisit the SIPOC when the process changes, for example when you add a new supplier or system.

Done well, a SIPOC gives your team a shared language for a process in less time than a typical meeting about the problem it describes.

Frequently asked questions

What does SIPOC stand for?

Suppliers, Inputs, Process, Outputs and Customers. Together the five columns describe a process at a high level on a single page.

What is the difference between a SIPOC and a process map?

A SIPOC is a high-level summary with five to seven steps and no decisions or loops. A process map or flowchart shows the detailed sequence, decisions and exceptions.

Why do you start a SIPOC with the process column?

The steps are what the team knows best, so starting there quickly sets the scope. Outputs, customers, inputs and suppliers are then easier to identify step by step.

How long should it take to build a SIPOC?

For most small-team processes, 30 to 60 minutes with the people who do the work is enough. If it takes much longer, the scope is probably too broad.
