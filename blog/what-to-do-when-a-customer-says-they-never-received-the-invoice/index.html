What If a Customer Says They Never Received Your Invoice?

A customer says the invoice never arrived. Learn why it happens, how to respond the same day, how to spot a stall, what to say and how to stop it recurring.

November 25, 2026 · 6 min read

“We never received the invoice.” Every accounts receivable team hears it, usually just after a payment falls due. Sometimes it is true: the invoice went to the wrong inbox or was rejected by an automated system nobody told you about. Sometimes it is a polite way of buying time. Either way, how you respond in the next few hours decides whether you get paid this week or next month.

Why do customers say they never received the invoice?

  • Wrong contact or email address. The invoice went to the buyer who placed the order rather than to accounts payable, or to someone who has since left the company.
  • Spam filters and attachment rules. Emails from billing systems, carrying PDF attachments and payment links, are exactly what corporate filters are built to quarantine.
  • Missing purchase order number. Many accounts payable teams automatically reject or park any invoice without a valid PO number, and they rarely tell the supplier.
  • A portal upload was required. Larger customers often accept invoices only through a supplier portal. An emailed invoice may never enter their system at all.
  • A genuine stalling tactic. A customer short on cash may claim the invoice is missing because the excuse costs nothing and resets the conversation.

What should you do the same day?

Take the claim at face value and remove every reason not to pay. Respond within hours, not days.

  • Resend immediately. Send the invoice as a PDF attachment with a direct payment link, so the customer can pay from the email itself. Keep the original invoice number and date; do not issue a new invoice.
  • Confirm the right recipient. Ask who should receive invoices, which address to use, and whether a PO number, portal upload or specific format is needed.
  • Ask for confirmation of receipt. Request a short reply confirming the invoice has arrived and is approved for payment. This turns a vague claim into a clear yes or no.
  • Restate the due date. Repeat the original due date and, where it is justified, offer a fair new payment date.

Should the due date restart when you resend the invoice?

Not automatically. Payment terms normally run from the invoice date, and your records show when it was sent. If the customer genuinely could not have received it, for example because you used an outdated address, a fair compromise is a short grace period of seven to ten days from the resend rather than a full new 30-day term. If the invoice was sent correctly and the customer simply did not process it, keep the original due date and say so politely. Resetting the clock every time an invoice is “lost” teaches customers that the claim works.

How can you tell a real problem from a stall?

Look for these signs:

  • Is it the first time? A one-off missing invoice from a reliable payer is almost always a process issue.
  • Do they engage with details? A customer who gives you the correct accounts payable address or PO number wants to pay.
  • Does your tracking disagree? If delivery or open tracking shows the email was opened several times, the invoice was not lost.
  • Do new excuses follow? When the resend is met with a sudden dispute over the amount or an absent approver, cash-flow trouble is the likelier explanation.

When the pattern points to a stall, move the account into your normal escalation process: firmer reminders, a call to a more senior contact and, if needed, a review of credit terms.

What should you say to the customer?

Keep it short, friendly and specific. For example:

“Thanks for letting us know. I have attached invoice 1042 for $4,800, dated 20 October, together with a link to pay online. Could you reply to confirm it has reached the right person and is approved for payment? The original due date was 19 November, and we would appreciate payment by 2 December. If you need a PO number or a portal upload, just tell me and I will sort it out today.”

If you hear nothing within three working days, pick up the phone. A five-minute call to accounts payable often resolves what takes a week by email.

Should you waive late fees?

If the fault was yours or the delivery failure is genuine, waive the fees and say so; it costs little and builds goodwill. If the invoice was delivered correctly, you are entitled to apply your terms, but a one-time waiver in exchange for payment by a firm date is often the faster route to cash.

How do you prevent “invoice not received” claims?

  • Send to a shared accounts payable mailbox. Ask for a team address rather than an individual, and copy the buyer where useful.
  • Use delivery and open tracking. Knowing when an invoice was delivered and opened gives you facts instead of an argument.
  • Send regular statements of account. A monthly statement listing every open invoice surfaces missing ones before they become overdue.
  • Confirm invoicing instructions at onboarding. Record the billing contact, PO requirements, portal details and required format when the account is opened, and recheck them every year.
  • Upload to the portal when required. If a customer uses a supplier portal, make the upload part of your standard invoicing step, not an afterthought.

A missing invoice is rarely a reason for conflict. Resend quickly, confirm the details, hold a fair line on the due date and fix the root cause, and the claim will become rare.

Frequently asked questions

Should I issue a new invoice if the customer says they never received it?

No. Resend the original invoice with the same number and date. A new invoice risks duplicate payment and blurs the record of when payment was first due.

Does the due date reset if the invoice was not received?

Not automatically. Terms usually run from the invoice date. If the customer genuinely never received it, offer a short grace period from the resend rather than a full new payment term.

How can I prove an invoice was delivered?

Keep sent records, use email delivery and open tracking, save portal upload confirmations and send statements of account that list the invoice. Together they show when it reached the customer.

What if a customer keeps saying invoices are missing?

Treat it as a warning sign. Confirm invoicing details in writing, call accounts payable directly, rely on tracking evidence and, if the pattern continues, escalate and review the customer's credit terms.
