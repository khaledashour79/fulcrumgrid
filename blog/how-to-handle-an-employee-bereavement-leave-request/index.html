How Should You Handle an Employee's Bereavement Leave Request?

What to say when an employee loses someone, how much bereavement leave to offer, pay, cover, documentation and writing a simple, fair bereavement policy.

November 18, 2026 · 6 min read

When an employee tells you that someone close to them has died, the way you respond in the next few minutes will stay with them for years. Many small businesses have no written policy, so managers improvise at the worst moment. This guide covers what to say, what to offer and how to put a simple policy in place.

What should you say first?

Start with the person, not the process. A short, sincere response is enough: "I am so sorry for your loss. Please take the time you need, and we will sort out the details later." If they want to talk, listen. If they want to leave straight away, let them go and follow up by message or phone the next day.

Avoid phrases that, however well meant, can hurt:

  • "I know how you feel." Every loss is different.
  • "They are in a better place" or other religious or philosophical comments, unless you know the person shares that view.
  • "When do you think you will be back?" in the first conversation.
  • Any mention of deadlines, rotas or busy periods before you have expressed sympathy.

Ask only one practical question: how they would like to be contacted over the coming days, and whether they want colleagues to be told.

How much bereavement leave is typical?

Many employers offer somewhere between three and five working days for the death of a close family member, and a day or so for other relatives or friends, often with discretion to extend. These are common practices, not rules.

Legal requirements vary widely by country. Some places set a statutory minimum, some protect the right to time off without guaranteeing pay, and some leave it entirely to the employment contract or a collective agreement. Special rules may also apply to the loss of a child or a pregnancy. Before you set a policy, check the current law where your staff work, or ask an employment adviser.

Should bereavement leave be paid?

Where the law does not require pay, the decision is yours. Paying for a set number of days costs relatively little for a small team and is long remembered. If you cannot afford to pay for extended time, say so clearly and offer alternatives: unpaid leave, using annual leave or a short period of reduced hours. What matters most is that the rules are clear and applied the same way to everyone.

Who counts as close family?

Most policies name a spouse or partner, children, parents, siblings and grandparents, and often in-laws and step-relatives. Families do not always fit a list, though. Someone may have been raised by an aunt, or may be grieving a close friend. Build in discretion so a manager can say yes when the relationship clearly mattered, instead of checking a family tree.

What about travel and extra time?

Funerals and mourning customs differ across cultures and faiths. Some require burial within a day or two; others involve several days of rites or travel abroad. Allow extra days for long-distance travel, and be flexible about when leave is taken: some people need time immediately, others for a funeral weeks later or to settle a relative's affairs.

How do you cover their work?

Make it clear that the employee does not need to hand anything over. Ask only about anything truly urgent, such as a client meeting tomorrow, then take it from there. Reassign tasks, push back non-essential deadlines and tell affected clients only that a colleague is unavailable. Share personal details with the team only as far as the employee has agreed.

Should you ask for proof?

In most cases, no. Asking a grieving employee for a death certificate or a funeral notice can feel like an accusation, and very few people abuse this kind of trust. If your policy does require some evidence, for example for extended paid leave, explain this in the policy in advance, ask gently and later rather than sooner, and accept simple evidence such as an order of service or an online notice.

How can you support their return?

Grief does not end when leave does. Before they come back, ask briefly how they would like their return handled. For the first weeks, consider a lighter workload or flexible hours. Remember key dates, such as the anniversary, and point them to any employee assistance programme or local bereavement support. If grief starts to affect their health or work over a longer period, treat it as a welfare matter, not a performance issue.

How do you write a simple bereavement policy?

One page is enough. It should cover:

  • How many days are offered, and for which relationships.
  • Whether those days are paid, and what happens if more time is needed.
  • Extra time for travel and for cultural or religious observances.
  • How to request leave and whom to tell, including by phone or message.
  • What evidence, if any, is needed, and when.
  • Manager discretion to go beyond the policy.

Check it against local law and share it with every employee.

Frequently asked questions

How many days of bereavement leave should a small business offer?

Many employers offer three to five working days for a close family member and around one day for others, with discretion to extend. Check local law first, as some countries set their own minimums.

Is bereavement leave legally required?

It depends on where your employees work. Some countries set a statutory right, paid or unpaid, while others leave it to contracts or collective agreements. Check current local law or ask an adviser.

Can I ask an employee for a death certificate?

It is best not to as a matter of routine. If your policy requires evidence, say so in advance, ask gently and later, and accept simple proof such as a funeral notice or order of service.

What if an employee needs more time than the policy allows?

Use discretion. Offer extra unpaid leave, annual leave, reduced hours or a phased return, and point them to any available support. Grief does not follow a fixed timetable.
