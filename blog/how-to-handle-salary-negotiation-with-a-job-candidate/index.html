How Do You Handle Salary Negotiation With a Job Candidate?

Handle salary negotiation calmly: set your range, make a reasoned offer, know what is flexible, and put the final deal in writing without losing the candidate.

October 26, 2026 · 6 min read

Salary negotiation makes many small-business owners uncomfortable. You want the best person, but you also have a payroll to protect. The good news is that a negotiation does not have to be a standoff. Handled well, it is simply the first honest conversation with a future employee, and it sets the tone for the whole working relationship.

Should you expect a candidate to negotiate?

Yes. Most candidates expect some room, and many of the strongest ones will ask. A request for more money is not rude and it is not a red flag; it usually means the person is engaged and values their work. Treat it as information about what matters to them, not as a challenge to your authority.

Prepare before you make an offer

Most negotiation trouble starts before the candidate says a word. Decide these things first:

  • Your budget ceiling. Know the highest base salary the role can sustain, including payroll taxes, benefits and equipment, not just the headline figure.
  • Your target and your floor. Pick the number you hope to settle at, and the number above which you would rather keep searching.
  • Market data. Check current pay for similar roles in your region and at your company size. Job postings, industry surveys and public salary ranges all help.
  • Internal fairness. Look at what current employees in similar roles earn. An offer far above them creates resentment, and in some places pay-equity rules apply.

Be transparent about the range early

Share a salary range in the job posting or in the first screening call. It saves everyone time, filters out mismatched expectations, and is legally required in a growing number of places, so check the rules where you hire. If a candidate's expectations sit far outside your range, it is kinder to find out in the first call than in the final round.

How to run the conversation

1. Make a confident, reasoned first offer

Open with a number that is fair and that you can defend. Explain how you got there: the scope of the role, the market, and what the rest of the package includes. A well-explained offer is easier to accept. Avoid lowballing in the hope of haggling upward; it often costs you the candidate before the talk even starts.

2. Listen before you respond

When the candidate counters, ask what is behind the number. Is it a current salary they want to beat, a cost-of-living issue, or a competing offer? Understanding the reason shows you what you can actually solve.

3. Decide what is flexible

Salary is only one lever. If the base cannot move, you may still be able to adjust other parts of the package:

  • a sign-on bonus or a performance bonus
  • an earlier pay review, for example after six months
  • extra paid time off or flexible hours
  • remote or hybrid days
  • a training budget or a clearer path to promotion
  • a later start date

4. Hold your limits kindly

It is fine to say no. Say it plainly and with respect: “That is above what we can offer for this role, but here is what I can do.” Do not invent a competing budget, and do not promise raises you cannot deliver.

5. Set a deadline and close

Once you reach agreement, repeat the terms out loud, then send the written offer promptly. Give a reasonable window, typically a few business days, for the candidate to decide.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Asking about current salary. It is banned in many places and anchors the talk on someone else's decision. Ask about expectations instead.
  • Treating negotiation as a loyalty test. Withdrawing an offer because someone politely asked for more rarely leads to a good working relationship.
  • Making verbal promises. “We will review it in six months” must be in the offer letter, or it will be disputed later.
  • Paying a new hire more than current staff without a plan. Resentment follows quickly, and then you have a retention problem.
  • Dragging it out. Slow replies signal disinterest. Good candidates usually have other options.

Document everything

Whatever you agree, put it in writing: base pay, bonus terms, review date, start date and any other benefits. A clear offer letter protects both sides and gives you a consistent record for the next hire. Note the reason behind any exception so you can explain it later.

Frequently asked questions

Should I ask a candidate for their current salary?

It is better not to. Many places ban the question, and it anchors the talk on a past decision. Ask about salary expectations for this role instead.

What if the candidate asks for more than my budget allows?

Say so honestly and explain your range. Then explore other levers such as a sign-on bonus, an earlier pay review, flexible hours or extra time off.

Is it okay to give a lower offer first and raise it later?

It is risky. A lowball can insult strong candidates and end the talk. A fair, well-explained first offer is more likely to be accepted.

How long should I give a candidate to accept an offer?

A few business days is typical. Give enough time to decide calmly, but not so long that the role and other applicants are left waiting.
