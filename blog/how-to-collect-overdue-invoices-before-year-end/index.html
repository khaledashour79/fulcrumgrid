How Do You Collect Overdue Invoices Before Year-End?

A week-by-week plan to collect overdue invoices before December 31: prioritise by aging, use budget flush, offer payment plans and decide what to write off.

November 20, 2026 · 6 min read

Every December the same thing happens. Customers slow down, approvers leave for the holidays, and the invoices you meant to chase in October are suddenly 60 or 90 days old. Yet cash collected before December 31 strengthens your year-end balance sheet, funds January payroll and spares you from carrying stale debts into a new year. The good news: year-end collection is predictable, and a plan that starts in mid-November brings in most of what you are owed before the books close.

Why is collecting in December so hard?

Three things work against you at the end of the year:

  • Holidays and leave. The person who approves your invoice may be away for two weeks, and nobody else has the authority to release payment.
  • Early or frozen payment runs. Many accounts payable teams bring their last payment run of the year forward, or stop payments entirely while they close their own books. Miss that run and you wait until January.
  • Budget cycles. Some customers are short of cash and hold payments to make their own year-end look better. Others have the opposite problem: unspent budget they must use before it expires.

The fix for all three: start earlier than feels necessary, and get specific dates from every customer.

How should you prioritise overdue invoices?

You will not have time to chase everything equally, so start with your aging report. Sort open invoices into buckets, typically current, 1–30, 31–60, 61–90 and over 90 days past due, then rank customers by the combination of amount and age.

  • Large and 31–90 days overdue. Your top priority: big enough to matter and still very collectible.
  • Large and over 90 days. Needs a senior conversation now, not another email. Find out whether there is a dispute, a cash problem or simply a lost invoice.
  • Small and recent. Handle with a batch of polite reminders and statements.
  • Small and very old. Make one final attempt, then decide whether it deserves more effort or should be written off.

The older a debt, the harder it is to collect: at 45 days an invoice needs a reminder; at 120 days, a negotiation.

A week-by-week plan from mid-November to December 31

Mid-November: clean up and confirm

Reconcile your receivables first. Apply unmatched payments, issue outstanding credit notes and correct wrong invoices, because customers will not pay an invoice they believe is incorrect. Then send every customer with an open balance a statement of account listing all open items. Ask two questions: is there anything here you cannot pay, and when is your last payment run of the year?

Late November: chase the priority list

Phone the customers at the top of your list. Confirm that each invoice is approved and scheduled for the final payment run, and record the promised date and amount. Escalate anything blocked by a dispute now, while there is still time to resolve it.

First week of December: use the budget flush

Customers with money left in their budgets are often keen to spend it before year-end. If you are discussing new work or a renewal with them, it is a natural moment to ask for overdue balances to be cleared at the same time. Keep it friendly: “Before we schedule the new order, could we settle the two open invoices so your account is clear for the new year?”

Second week of December: follow up on promises

Check every promised payment against your bank statement and contact anyone who missed a date the same day. For customers who genuinely cannot pay in full, offer a payment plan: a first instalment before December 31 and the rest in fixed monthly amounts, agreed in writing.

Last two weeks: final calls and decisions

Most payment runs are done by now, so confirm what will arrive in each customer’s first January run and turn to the decisions you need to make before closing the books.

What should you write off or provision before closing the books?

Some invoices will not be collected by December 31, and a few never will be. Review what remains with your accountant and sort it into three groups:

  • Collectible. Confirmed, scheduled or covered by a payment plan. Leave them open.
  • Doubtful. Old, disputed or owed by a customer in difficulty. Consider a provision for doubtful debts, which reflects the likely loss without giving up the claim.
  • Uncollectible. The customer has closed, cannot be traced, or pursuing the debt would cost more than it is worth. These may be candidates for write-off.

The rules on when a bad debt can be written off, and how it is treated for tax, differ from country to country, so keep a record of your collection attempts and confirm the treatment with your accountant before acting.

How do you set up January follow-up?

Before you leave, prepare a short list for the first working week of January: every promised payment with its date, every payment plan instalment and every open dispute with its owner. Send a brief note to customers who promised a January payment, so it is waiting when they return. Then look back at the year: which customers paid late repeatedly, and do their terms, credit limits or deposits need to change?

Frequently asked questions

When should you start chasing overdue invoices before year-end?

By mid-November. Many customers make their last payment run of the year in early or mid-December, and approvals take time, so invoices chased only in December often slip into January.

Is it acceptable to ask for payment during the holidays?

Yes, if you are polite and specific. Ask customers to confirm a payment date rather than sending repeated demands, and if your contact is away, ask who is covering for them.

What is the difference between a write-off and a provision?

A provision records a likely loss while you keep pursuing the debt. A write-off removes the debt from your receivables because you no longer expect to collect it. The rules differ by country, so check with your accountant.

Should I offer a discount to get paid before December 31?

Sometimes. A small settlement discount can make sense for a large, old balance, but offer it once with a clear deadline, and only where having the cash now is worth more to you than the margin you give up.
