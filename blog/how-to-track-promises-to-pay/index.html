How Do You Track and Follow Up on a Customer's Promise to Pay?

How to track a customer's promise to pay: what to log, how to follow up before and after the date, how to handle broken promises, and when to escalate.

October 12, 2026 · 6 min read

A promise to pay is a customer's commitment to settle an overdue invoice by a specific date. It is often the first real progress in a collection case, but only if you capture it properly. A promise that lives in someone's memory or a buried email thread is easy to lose, and a lost promise means a late chase, a weaker position and cash that arrives later than it should.

What counts as a promise to pay?

Not every "we'll sort it soon" is a promise. A useful promise has a specific date, an amount and a named person who made it. Vague answers should be turned into something you can track by asking one simple follow-up question: "Can you confirm which day the payment will be made, and for how much?" If the customer will only commit to part of the balance, record that partial promise as well; it is still a date you can hold them to.

What should you log?

Record every promise the moment it is made, in the same place you track the invoice. At a minimum, capture:

  • The date promised: the day the customer says funds will arrive, not the day they will "start the process".
  • The amount: full balance or partial, and which invoices it covers.
  • Who made it: name, role and contact details, so you know who to call next.
  • How it was made: phone, email or meeting, with a short note of what was said and a copy of any written confirmation.
  • The payment method: bank transfer, card or cheque, and any condition attached, such as "once the PO is approved".

Then send a short written confirmation: "Thank you for your call today. We understand payment of 4,200 will be made by 20 October." This turns a verbal promise into a record both sides can see, and it quietly raises the odds that the customer keeps it.

How do you follow up around the date?

Treat the promised date as a checkpoint, not a deadline you wait out in silence.

  • Before the date: a friendly reminder one or two days ahead, with the invoice and bank details attached, removes easy excuses.
  • On the date: check your bank account in the afternoon rather than assuming. If you can match the payment, close the promise and update the invoice.
  • The day after: if nothing has arrived, contact the customer the same morning. Speed matters because the promise is still fresh.

What if the promise is broken?

A missed promise is information. Ask calmly what happened, because the reason shapes your next move. A genuine delay, such as a bank error or an approver on leave, deserves a new date. A pattern of excuses does not. Log the broken promise, secure a fresh date in writing, and note how many times this customer has missed one. Two broken promises on the same invoice is a good signal to stop accepting open-ended assurances and move to firmer terms, such as a shorter deadline or a payment plan with fixed instalments.

When should you escalate?

Escalate by steps rather than jumping straight to the final one, and tell the customer what the next step is before you take it.

  • Second contact: move from the usual accounts contact to someone with authority to release payment, such as a finance manager or owner.
  • Formal reminder: a written notice that states the amount, the original due date, the broken promises and a final date.
  • Account controls: pause new orders or credit until the overdue balance is settled, if your terms allow it.
  • External action: a demand letter, collection agency or legal advice, depending on the amount and your contract.

Keeping a dated record of every promise and every missed date makes each of these steps easier to justify, and it helps if the debt ever has to be written off.

How do you keep this manageable?

A spreadsheet works for a handful of accounts, but it depends on someone remembering to check it. Frequently asked questions

What is a promise to pay?

It is a customer's commitment to pay an overdue invoice, in full or in part, by a specific date. To be useful it needs a date, an amount and the name of the person who made it.

Should I get a promise to pay in writing?

Yes, wherever you can. If the promise was made by phone, send a short email confirming the date and amount. A written record is easier to refer back to and makes the customer more likely to keep to it.

How soon should I follow up after a broken promise?

As soon as possible, ideally the next morning. A quick, polite contact while the promise is fresh works better than waiting a week, and it shows that you track dates.

How many broken promises should I accept before escalating?

There is no fixed number, but two broken promises on the same invoice is a sensible point to move to firmer terms, such as a payment plan, a formal notice or involving a senior contact.

Is a promise to pay legally binding?

Usually it is not a contract on its own, and the rules vary by country. It is still valuable evidence of the debt and of your efforts to collect it. For legal questions, ask a qualified adviser.
