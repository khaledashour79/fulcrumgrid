How Do You Run a Performance Calibration Meeting in a Small Business?

Learn how to run a performance calibration meeting: who attends, how to prepare, a 90-minute agenda, bias checks and how to share results with employees.

November 21, 2026 · 6 min read

Two managers can watch similar work all year and still hand out very different ratings. One gives almost everyone a 4 out of 5; the other saves a 4 for exceptional work. When those ratings feed into pay rises, bonuses or promotions, the gap quietly becomes unfair. A calibration meeting is where managers compare their proposed ratings against shared standards before anything is final. It sounds like a big-company ritual, but it works just as well with three managers and thirty employees.

What is a performance calibration meeting?

It is a structured session in which the people who rate employees review the proposed ratings together, test them against the same definitions and evidence, and adjust them where needed. The goal is not to force everyone onto a curve. It is to make sure that "meets expectations" in the warehouse means the same thing as "meets expectations" in sales.

Why do ratings drift between managers?

  • Different personal standards. Some managers are naturally generous, others naturally tough.
  • Vague scale definitions. If "exceeds expectations" is never written down, each manager invents a meaning.
  • Uneven visibility. Managers see more of people who sit near them or work on visible projects.
  • Conflict avoidance. A low rating means a hard conversation, so it gets rounded up.

Who should attend?

Invite every manager who submitted ratings for the group being calibrated, plus a facilitator who does not rate anyone in that group. In a small business this is often the owner, an operations lead or whoever handles people matters. Aim for roughly four to eight people. Employees do not attend: the meeting discusses their individual ratings, so it must stay confidential.

How do you prepare?

  • Write down the rating scale. Define each level in one or two sentences, with an example of observable behavior or results, and share it with managers before they rate.
  • Collect pre-submitted ratings. Ask managers to enter a proposed rating and a short justification for each person at least three working days before the meeting. Ratings made live in the room get anchored by whoever speaks first.
  • Require evidence. Each justification should cite two or three concrete examples from the whole review period: goals met or missed, customer feedback, quality data, peer input.
  • Prepare a summary view. A simple table of each manager's rating distribution makes leniency or severity visible at a glance.

How do you run the session?

A practical agenda for a 90-minute meeting:

  • Ground rules (5 minutes). Confidentiality, focus on evidence, and every rating is open to questions.
  • Review distributions (10 minutes). Compare the shape of each manager's ratings. A team where everyone scored top marks is a question, not an accusation.
  • Discuss outliers (30 minutes). Start with the highest and lowest ratings. The rating manager summarizes the evidence; the others ask questions.
  • Discuss borderline cases (30 minutes). People sitting between two levels are where most inconsistency hides.
  • Confirm and recap (15 minutes). Read back every changed rating and the reason for it.

Timebox each person to three to five minutes. If a case cannot be settled, park it and agree who will gather more information by a set date.

Which biases should you check for?

  • Recency bias. Judging the whole year on the last few weeks. Ask: "What happened in the first half of the period?"
  • Halo and horns effect. Letting one strong or weak trait color everything. Check whether the rating holds for each criterion separately.
  • Leniency and central tendency. Rating everyone high, or everyone in the middle, to avoid hard calls.
  • Similarity bias. Favoring people who think, talk or work like the manager.

The facilitator's main job is to ask these questions neutrally and for every manager, not only the ones they happen to disagree with.

What should come out of the meeting?

Leave with a final rating for each employee, a short note explaining any change and who agreed to it, and a list of follow-ups. Record reasons in plain words, for example "lowered from 4 to 3: two of three goals missed, no evidence of work beyond the role". These notes protect fairness if a rating is challenged later and reveal patterns over several cycles. How do you communicate results to employees?

The manager, not the calibration group, delivers the rating and owns it. Never say "the committee lowered your score": it undermines the manager and makes the process feel secretive. Explain the rating with the same evidence used in the meeting, describe what the next level looks like and agree on one or two goals. It is fine, and helpful, to tell employees that ratings are reviewed across managers for consistency. Being open about the process builds trust.

What are the common mistakes?

  • Calibrating without written definitions. The discussion turns into a contest of who argues loudest.
  • Forcing a strict curve. In a team of ten, a fixed quota of low ratings is statistically meaningless and damages morale.
  • Letting the most senior person decide. Calibration is about evidence, not rank.
  • Debating personality instead of results. "Not a team player" needs specific examples or it should not count.
  • Skipping the follow-through. If changes are not documented and communicated, the next cycle starts from the same drift.

Frequently asked questions

How often should a small business hold calibration meetings?

Once per review cycle, which for most small teams means once or twice a year. Hold it after managers submit ratings but before anything is shared with employees or linked to pay.

Is calibration worth it with only two or three managers?

Yes. Even two managers can drift apart in how they use a rating scale. A 30 to 45 minute session, with the owner or another neutral person facilitating, is usually enough.

Should calibration use a forced distribution?

Usually not in a small business. With few employees a fixed quota distorts reality. Use rating distributions as a prompt for discussion, not as a target to hit.

Should employees know their rating was calibrated?

Yes, explain that ratings are reviewed across managers for consistency. Keep the discussion of individuals confidential, and have the manager deliver and own the final rating.
