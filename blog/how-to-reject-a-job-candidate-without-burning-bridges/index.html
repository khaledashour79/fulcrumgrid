How Do You Reject a Job Candidate Without Burning Bridges?

Rejecting candidates well protects your reputation and future hiring. Learn when to say no, which channel to use, what to say and how to keep runners-up warm.

November 15, 2026 · 6 min read

Most candidates you interview will not get the job. How you tell them shapes what they say about your business, whether they apply again and whether they send friends your way. For a small team that hires a few people a year, every one of those conversations counts. This explainer covers how to say no clearly, kindly and safely.

Why does the way you reject candidates matter?

A rejected candidate is still a potential customer, referrer or future employee. A respectful no protects you in several ways:

  • Reputation. Candidates talk to friends and post reviews. In a small local market, word spreads quickly.
  • Referrals. People who were treated well often recommend you to others, even when they did not get the role.
  • Future hires. Someone who is not right today may be ideal in a year, or for a different position.
  • Runners-up. Your second choice for a role is often a strong hire. If the first choice declines or leaves early, you want that person to take your call.

When should you tell a candidate they did not get the job?

As soon as the decision is final, ideally within two to three working days. Waiting weeks leaves people in limbo and makes your business look disorganized. The one exception is your runner-up: it is reasonable to wait until your preferred candidate has signed before closing the door, but tell the runner-up honestly that a decision is taking a little longer.

Above all, do not ghost. Silence after an interview is one of the most common complaints candidates have, and it is the fastest way to burn a bridge. If someone took the time to interview, they deserve an answer.

Should you reject by email or by phone?

Match the channel to how much time the candidate has invested:

  • Application or screening stage: a short, personal email is fine. Use their name and the role title rather than a blank form letter.
  • After one or two interviews: a thoughtful email, sent by the hiring manager rather than a no-reply address.
  • Final round: a brief phone call, followed by a short email confirming the outcome. Someone who met the team and possibly did a task deserves to hear it from a person.

What should you say, and what should you avoid?

Good rejections are clear, brief and kind. Thank the candidate, state the decision in the first or second sentence and close warmly. Do not bury the answer in paragraphs of praise.

Be careful with reasons. Detailed explanations can create legal risk, invite argument or simply be misheard. Keep any reason short and tied to the job, such as “we chose a candidate with more experience managing suppliers”. Never mention, or hint at, age, gender, pregnancy or family plans, health, disability, religion, ethnicity, nationality or any other protected characteristic. Also avoid phrases like “not a culture fit” or “overqualified”, which can sound like a cover for bias. Do not make promises you cannot keep, such as “we will definitely have something for you soon”.

This is general guidance, not legal advice. Employment rules differ by country and region, so check local requirements if you are unsure.

Should you give feedback?

Feedback is a courtesy, not an obligation. If you offer it, keep it short, specific and based on the job criteria you assessed, ideally from your interview notes. Many small businesses offer feedback only to final-round candidates, and only when asked. A safe approach:

  • Give one or two points the candidate can act on, for example deeper examples of budgeting work.
  • Stick to skills and evidence from the process, never personality or personal circumstances.
  • Do not compare them with the person you hired.
  • If you cannot say something useful and fair, a polite thank-you is enough.

What does a good rejection message look like?

Adapt these short examples to your own voice.

Application stage: “Hi Sam, thank you for applying for the Office Coordinator role. We received many strong applications and have decided not to move forward with yours this time. We appreciate your interest in our team and wish you the best in your search.”

Final round: “Hi Alex, thank you for the time you spent with us, including the planning exercise. It was a close decision, and we have offered the role to another candidate whose experience matched our current needs slightly more closely. We were impressed by your work and would like to keep in touch about future openings, if you are happy for us to do so.”

How do you keep strong runners-up warm?

Your best near-misses are a ready-made shortlist for the next hire. To keep them engaged:

  • Ask permission to keep their details and contact them about future roles, and respect any local data protection rules.
  • Add them to a talent pool with a note on their strengths and which roles they might suit.
  • Follow up when something relevant opens, rather than sending generic newsletters.
  • If your new hire does not work out in the first months, call your runner-up first.

How do you keep rejections consistent?

Consistency is fairness in practice. Decide a standard timeline for each stage, keep a small set of approved templates and record when and how each candidate was told, along with the job-related reason. That way nobody slips through the cracks, every candidate is treated the same way and you have a clear record if a decision is ever questioned.

Frequently asked questions

How soon should I reject a candidate after the interview?

Within two to three working days of making the final decision. If you are holding a runner-up while your first choice decides, tell them honestly that it will take a little longer.

Do I have to tell candidates why they were rejected?

Usually not, though rules vary by location. If you give a reason, keep it brief and tied to the job criteria, and never refer to protected characteristics such as age, health or family status.

Is it acceptable to reject candidates by email?

Yes, for application and early interview stages. For final-round candidates, a short phone call followed by a confirming email is more respectful and leaves a better impression.

Can I keep a rejected candidate's details for future roles?

Yes, if you ask their permission and follow local data protection rules. Note their strengths and contact them when a genuinely relevant role opens.
