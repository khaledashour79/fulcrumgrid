How Do You Write a Remote Work Policy for a Small Business?

A remote work policy sets clear rules on eligibility, hours, equipment, security and legal duties. Here is how a small business can write one that works.

October 16, 2026 · 6 min read

Remote and hybrid work stop being a favor and start being a system the moment more than one person does it. Without written rules, every manager improvises, and people compare notes. A short, clear policy protects fairness, the business and the employees. This Q&A covers the questions small business owners ask most.

Why does a small business need a written policy?

Because informal deals do not scale and are hard to defend. A policy tells everyone what is expected, treats similar roles similarly and gives you something to point to when a request is declined. It does not need to be long. Two or three pages that people actually read beat twenty that nobody opens.

Who is eligible to work remotely?

Decide by the nature of the job, not by favoritism. Ask whether the tasks can be done well away from the office, whether the role needs on-site presence such as handling goods or meeting walk-in customers, and whether the person has shown they can work independently. State the criteria, say whether remote work is full-time, hybrid or occasional, and explain that eligibility can change if the role or performance changes.

What about core hours and availability?

Flexibility works best with a few fixed points. Define core hours, for example 10:00 to 15:00 in the team’s time zone, when everyone is reachable for meetings and quick questions. Say how fast people are expected to answer messages, which channel is used for what, and how to signal absence. Outside core hours, let people organize their own day.

What equipment and expenses should you cover?

Be explicit, because this is where disputes start. Common choices:

  • Equipment. A laptop, screen or headset supplied by the company and returned when the person leaves.
  • Allowances. A fixed monthly amount toward internet or electricity, or reimbursement against receipts.
  • Approval. A rule that purchases above a set amount need prior sign-off.
  • Ownership. Who repairs, insures and replaces company equipment.

Rules on reimbursement differ by country, and in some places employers must contribute, so check yours.

How do you protect security and data?

A home network is less controlled than an office. Require company-managed devices where possible, strong unique passwords with a password manager, multi-factor authentication, automatic updates and screen locks. Forbid sharing work devices with family, set rules for public Wi-Fi and say how to report a lost device or suspected breach immediately. If you handle personal data, privacy law still applies at the kitchen table.

How should performance be measured?

Measure outcomes, not hours online or green status dots. Agree on goals, deadlines and quality standards, review progress in regular one-to-ones and give feedback early. Managers need the same discipline for remote and office staff, otherwise remote people become invisible at promotion time.

What about time tracking and working-time rules?

In many countries employers must still record working time, respect maximum hours, daily and weekly rest and overtime rules, even when staff work from home. Decide how hours are logged, how overtime is approved and what the right to disconnect means in practice. Tracking should be proportionate: record time worked, not keystrokes.

Which tax and legal points should you check?

This is general information, not legal advice, so confirm details locally. Points to review:

  • Work location. Whether employees may work from another city or country, and for how long. Working abroad can create tax, social security and permit issues for both sides.
  • Contracts. Whether the employment contract needs updating to name a place of work.
  • Health and safety. Employer duties can extend to the home workspace.
  • Insurance. Whether business equipment and work accidents at home are covered.

How often should you review the policy?

Treat the first version as a trial. Review it after three months, then at least once a year, and whenever laws or the business change. Ask staff and managers what is unclear or unfair, and record every change with a date.

A short policy outline

  • Purpose and scope. Why the policy exists and who it covers.
  • Eligibility. Criteria, arrangement types and how to apply.
  • Hours and availability. Core hours, response times, channels.
  • Equipment and expenses. What is provided, allowances, approvals.
  • Security and data. Required controls and incident reporting.
  • Performance. Goals, check-ins, feedback.
  • Working time. Logging, overtime, rest, disconnecting.
  • Location and legal. Permitted places, tax and insurance notes.
  • Review. Owner, schedule and version history.

Once the policy exists, the paperwork around it needs a home. Write it plainly, apply it consistently and revisit it often.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a remote work policy be?

Two to three pages is usually enough for a small business. Cover eligibility, hours, equipment, security, performance, working time and review, and link to separate documents for details.

Do we have to pay for an employee's home internet?

It depends on local law and your contract. Some places require employers to contribute to work-related costs, others leave it to agreement. Check your rules, then state the allowance clearly in the policy.

Can we track remote employees' screens or keystrokes?

Intrusive monitoring can breach privacy and data protection law and damages trust. Record working time where required and measure results, and get local advice before using any monitoring software.

Can an employee work from another country?

Not without checking first. Working abroad may trigger tax, social security, permit and data-protection obligations. Set a rule that it needs prior written approval and take local advice.

Can we withdraw remote work later?

Often yes, if the policy and contract allow it and you give reasonable notice and reasons. Say so in the policy, and check local employment law before changing an existing arrangement.
