How Do You Create Salary Bands for a Small Business?

A practical guide to creating salary bands for a small business: benchmarking, grades, midpoint, range spread, placing people, compa-ratio and communicating pay.

October 18, 2026 · 6 min read

A salary band is a pay range for a group of similar jobs, with a minimum, a midpoint, and a maximum. Instead of negotiating every salary from scratch, you decide in advance what a role is worth to your business and where each person sits within that range. For a small business, bands do not need to be complicated. They need to be fair, affordable, and written down.

Why use salary bands?

Without bands, pay tends to drift: the person who negotiated hardest earns more than a colleague doing the same work, and hiring managers have no guide when an offer is needed quickly. Bands give you consistency, make pay decisions easier to defend, and show employees what progression looks like. They also help you spot pay gaps before they become a retention or legal problem.

Step 1: Benchmark the market

Start with what the market pays for comparable jobs in your location and industry. Match on the actual work, level, and responsibilities rather than on job titles, which vary widely between companies.

  • Salary surveys: published industry or government pay data, and surveys from professional bodies.
  • Job adverts: where advertised ranges are shown, they are a useful, if imperfect, signal.
  • Recruiters and peers: a recruiter or other local business owners can sanity-check your numbers.
  • Your own data: recent offers accepted and declined, and why people have left.

Use two or three sources and look at the typical (median) figure, not just the highest. Treat benchmarks as a guide, then adjust for your budget and the labour market you actually hire in.

Step 2: Group jobs into grades

A grade is a level that holds jobs of roughly similar size. Rank your roles by the knowledge needed, the complexity of the work, the independence expected, and whether the person manages others. Then group them. A small business often needs only five to eight grades, for example entry, associate, experienced, senior or specialist, and manager.

Grade the job, not the person. A highly capable employee in an entry role stays in that grade until the job itself changes or they move into a larger one.

Step 3: Set the midpoint and range spread

For each grade, set the midpoint first. It is the market rate for a fully competent person in the role, usually taken from your benchmarking. Then choose a range spread, the gap between minimum and maximum, expressed as a percentage of the minimum.

  • Narrow spreads (about 20-30%): common for entry and support roles, where the learning curve is short.
  • Medium spreads (about 30-40%): typical for experienced professionals.
  • Wide spreads (40-50% or more): used for senior, specialist, and management roles, where growth takes longer.

Worked example: if the midpoint is 50,000 and you choose a 50% spread, the minimum is 40,000 and the maximum is 60,000 (60,000 is 50% above 40,000, and the midpoint is the average of the two). Adjacent grades should usually overlap a little, so an experienced person in one grade can earn about the same as a new starter in the next, but not so much that grades lose their meaning.

Step 4: Place people and use compa-ratio

Placing someone in a band depends on skills, experience, performance, and what you can afford. New hires usually start in the lower half of the range, with the top reserved for people who have mastered the role and perform strongly. Avoid hiring at the maximum, because it leaves no room to reward growth.

Compa-ratio is a simple check on where each person sits: salary divided by the midpoint. A salary of 46,000 against a midpoint of 50,000 gives a compa-ratio of 0.92, or 92%. Roughly:

  • Below about 0.85: likely a new starter, or underpaid and a flight risk if performance is strong.
  • Around 0.90 to 1.10: fully in the expected zone for a competent employee.
  • Above about 1.15: near the top of the range, so further rises may need a promotion rather than a pay review.

Calculate it for everyone once a year. If someone is well below the midpoint without a good reason, or if similar roles show unexplained differences by gender or other protected characteristics, investigate and correct it.

Step 5: Communicate the bands

Decide how open to be. At minimum, tell each employee the range for their own grade and what it takes to move within it. Many employers now share ranges in job adverts, and in some jurisdictions pay transparency rules require it, so check the requirements where you operate.

  • Explain the logic: grades reflect the job, ranges reflect the market, and position reflects experience and performance.
  • Be honest about limits: a band is not a promise of a raise, and budgets still apply.
  • Train managers: they should be able to explain a pay decision in plain words.
  • Review yearly: refresh benchmarks and adjust ranges when the market moves.

A final note: pay rules, minimum wage, and transparency requirements differ by country, and this article is general information, not legal or compensation advice. Frequently asked questions

What is a salary band?

A salary band is a pay range for a group of similar jobs, defined by a minimum, a midpoint, and a maximum. It sets what the business is willing to pay for a role and where an individual can sit within that range.

How many salary bands does a small business need?

Usually five to eight grades is enough. Too many grades create false precision, while too few force very different jobs into the same range.

What is compa-ratio and how do I calculate it?

Compa-ratio is an employee's salary divided by the midpoint of their band. A result of 1.00 means paid exactly at the midpoint, below 1.00 means below it, and above 1.00 means above it. It helps you spot people who are underpaid or near the top of their range.

Should I tell employees the salary bands?

At least share each person's own range and how to progress. Some jurisdictions require pay transparency in job adverts or on request, so check local rules and be ready to explain your reasoning.

How often should I review salary bands?

Review benchmarks and ranges at least once a year, and sooner if you struggle to hire or lose people to better-paying employers.
