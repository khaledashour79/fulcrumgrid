What Is a Credit Memo and When Should You Issue One?

A credit memo reduces what a customer owes you without rewriting the original invoice. Learn when to issue one, what it must include, and how it differs from a refund.

October 7, 2026 · 5 min read

Sooner or later every business sends an invoice that turns out to be wrong, or sells something that comes back. You could delete the invoice and start again, but that rewrites history and leaves your books hard to follow. A credit memo fixes the problem cleanly. This guide explains what a credit memo is, when to issue one, and how to avoid the mistakes that cause the most confusion.

What is a credit memo?

A credit memo, also called a credit note, is a document a seller issues to reduce the amount a customer owes. It refers to a specific earlier invoice and states how much is being credited and why. The original invoice stays untouched, so both sides keep a clear record of what was billed and what was later adjusted.

Credit memo, refund and debit memo: what is the difference?

  • Credit memo. A document that lowers the customer’s balance. No money moves at that moment.
  • Refund. Money actually paid back to the customer. A credit memo can lead to a refund, but it does not have to.
  • Debit memo. The opposite: a document that increases what the customer owes, for example when you under-billed.

If the customer has not paid yet, the credit memo simply reduces the amount due. If they have already paid, it creates a credit balance that can be applied to a future invoice or refunded.

When should you issue a credit memo?

  • Returned goods. The customer sends back items and you accept the return.
  • Damaged or wrong items. The order arrived broken, incomplete or not as described.
  • Billing errors. A wrong price, quantity, tax rate or duplicate charge appeared on the invoice.
  • Agreed discounts. A volume rebate, loyalty discount or goodwill gesture is granted after invoicing.
  • Cancelled or partly delivered services. The work was stopped early or only partly done.
  • Overpayments. The customer paid too much and you want to hold the difference as credit.

When not to issue one

If an invoice has not been sent yet, just correct it. If the customer simply disputes a charge you believe is right, resolve the dispute first. A credit memo is a record of an agreed adjustment, not a way to end an argument. And never use it to write off a debt you cannot collect; that is a bad-debt write-off, which belongs in a different process.

What should a credit memo include?

  • A unique credit memo number and the issue date.
  • Your details and the customer’s details, as on the invoice.
  • The number and date of the original invoice.
  • A line-by-line description of what is credited, with quantities and prices.
  • The tax adjustment, calculated at the same rate as the original invoice.
  • The total amount credited and a short reason.
  • What happens next: deducted from the balance, applied to a future invoice, or refunded.

How do you issue one correctly?

Confirm the reason and get approval

Check the facts: the return, the damage photo, the pricing agreement. Larger credits should be approved by someone other than the person who requests them.

Link it to the original invoice

Always reference the invoice number. A credit memo floating without a link is almost impossible to reconcile later.

Credit the right amount, including tax

Credit only what is actually being adjusted. For a partial credit, calculate the tax proportionally.

Tell the customer and apply it

Send the document and say plainly what happens to the balance. Then record it so the open amount shown in your system matches reality.

A simple example

You invoice a customer for 40 units at 100 each, a total of 4,000 before tax. Five units arrive damaged and you accept the return. You issue a credit memo for 5 units, 500 plus the matching tax, referencing the original invoice. The customer now owes 3,500 plus tax instead of 4,000. If they had already paid in full, the 500 would stay as credit or be refunded.

Common credit memo mistakes

  • Editing or deleting the invoice instead. This breaks your audit trail.
  • Forgetting the tax part. The credit must reverse tax as well as the net amount.
  • No reference to the invoice. It makes reconciliation slow and error-prone.
  • Not telling the customer. They keep chasing or paying the old amount.
  • Chasing the full invoice anyway. A reminder for 4,000 when the real balance is 3,500 damages trust fast.

Credit memos and collections

Credit memos matter most when you chase unpaid invoices. A reminder that ignores a credit memo asks the customer for money they do not owe. If you manage receivables in FulcrumGrid Collection, you can keep credit memos next to the open invoices they relate to, so the balance you follow up on is the real one. A spreadsheet works too, as long as someone keeps it up to date.

Issue a credit memo whenever the amount owed legitimately changes, link it to the invoice, and tell the customer. A clean paper trail saves far more time than it costs.

Frequently asked questions

Is a credit memo the same as a refund?

No. A credit memo lowers what the customer owes or creates a credit balance. A refund is money actually paid back. A credit memo can lead to a refund, but many customers prefer to use the credit on their next order.

Is a credit memo the same as a credit note?

Yes. The two names describe the same document. Which one you see depends on the country and the accounting software, but the purpose is identical.

Can I just cancel the invoice instead?

Sometimes, if the invoice was never sent or has not yet been recorded. Once the customer has received it, issuing a credit memo is cleaner, because it keeps the original and shows the adjustment openly.

Does a credit memo affect tax?

Usually yes. It reduces the tax you charged, so it should show the tax adjustment at the original rate. Rules differ by country, so check with your accountant.
