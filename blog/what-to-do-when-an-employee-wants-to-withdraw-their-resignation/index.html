What If an Employee Wants to Withdraw Their Resignation?

Can an employee take back a resignation? What managers should weigh before deciding, how to say yes or no fairly, and what to put in writing afterwards.

November 12, 2026 · 6 min read

A resignation usually feels final. Then, a few days later, the employee is back at your door: the new job fell through, the family move is off, or they simply realise they acted too quickly. Can they take it back? Should you let them? There is no single right answer, but there is a sensible way to decide and a fair way to handle either outcome. This is general guidance, not legal advice: employment rules differ by country and by contract, so check local law or speak to an adviser when the stakes are high.

Are you obliged to accept a withdrawn resignation?

In many places, generally not. Once an employee has clearly resigned and you have accepted it, the resignation usually stands, and you are free to agree to a withdrawal or to decline it. There are important exceptions. A resignation given in the heat of the moment, during an argument or under severe stress, may not be treated as genuine in some jurisdictions, and employers are often expected to allow a short cooling-off period before relying on it. Contracts, collective agreements or internal policies may also set rules. When in doubt, pause and get advice before you reply.

What should you ask yourself before deciding?

Treat the request as a new decision, not as an undo button. Before you meet, think through:

  • Why did they resign in the first place? Pay, workload, a difficult relationship or no room to grow? If the cause is still there, it will probably push them out again.
  • Why do they want to stay now? A genuine change of heart is different from a fallback because another offer collapsed.
  • Has the role been backfilled? If you have made an offer to a replacement or reorganised the work, reversing that may be costly or unfair to others.
  • How will the team react? Colleagues may have taken on extra work or been promised the role.
  • Would you hire this person again today? If the honest answer is yes, a withdrawal is worth considering.

What if a counteroffer is involved?

Sometimes the retraction follows an offer of more money or a new title to keep the person. Be careful. A counteroffer treats the symptom but rarely the cause, and many people who accept one leave within a year anyway. It can also tell the rest of the team that resigning is the way to get a raise. If you agree to new terms, make sure they reflect the value of the role rather than the pressure of the moment, and that you would offer the same to others in a similar position.

How should you have the conversation?

Meet in private, ideally within a day or two, and do not give an answer on the spot unless you are certain. Thank them for being open. Ask them to explain, in their own words, what has changed and what would need to be different for them to stay for the long term. Then tell them when they will have a decision, and ask them to put the request in writing.

How do you say yes the right way?

  • Confirm it in writing. State that the resignation is withdrawn by mutual agreement, that employment continues on the existing terms (or on the new terms, if any were agreed) and that continuity of service is unaffected, where that applies.
  • Reset expectations. Agree on what each side commits to over the next few months, such as a project, a schedule or a development goal.
  • Address the root cause. If workload or a working relationship drove the resignation, act on it. Otherwise you are only postponing the next one.
  • Tidy up loose ends. Cancel offboarding tasks, take down the job advert and update anyone who knew.
  • Check in. Book follow-ups after 30 and 90 days to see whether things have actually improved.

How do you say no respectfully?

A refusal does not have to damage the relationship. Give the answer in person first, then in writing. Be honest but brief: the role has been filled, the team has been reorganised, or the business has already planned around the departure. Do not reopen old performance complaints unless they are genuinely the reason. Confirm that the original leaving date stands, explain what happens next with the handover and final pay, and thank them for their work.

Why do consistency and precedent matter?

Whatever you decide becomes an example. If you accept one person's withdrawal and refuse another's in a similar situation, the difference needs a clear business reason, not personal preference. Inconsistent treatment can look like favouritism and, in some cases, may expose you to discrimination claims. A short written rule helps: withdrawals are decided case by case, by a named manager, on the same factors every time.

What records should you keep?

Keep the original resignation, the withdrawal request, notes from your meeting, your decision and the reasons for it, and any changed terms. File them with dates in the employee's record. If questions come up later about notice, continuity of service or fairness, this trail is your best answer. What is the short version?

You are usually not obliged to accept a withdrawn resignation, but you may want to. Ask why they left and why they want to stay, look honestly at the role and the team, decide on the same criteria every time and put the outcome in writing. Either way, handle it so the rest of the team would call it fair.

Frequently asked questions

Can an employee take back a resignation once it has been accepted?

Usually only if the employer agrees. Rules vary by country and contract, and a resignation given in the heat of the moment may be treated differently, so check local law if you are unsure.

Should a withdrawn resignation be confirmed in writing?

Yes. A short letter or email confirming the withdrawal, the terms of continued employment and any agreed changes protects both sides and avoids disputes later.

Is it fair to refuse a withdrawal if the job has already been filled?

Generally yes, as long as you explain the reason honestly and treat similar requests the same way. Having already hired or reorganised is a legitimate business reason.

Does accepting a withdrawal set a precedent?

It can. Decide every case on the same criteria, record your reasons and avoid treating a raise as an automatic fix, so others do not see resigning as a bargaining tactic.
