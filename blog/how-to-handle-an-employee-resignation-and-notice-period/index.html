How Do You Handle an Employee Resignation and Notice Period?

Handle a resignation calmly: respond well, get it in writing, confirm the notice period, plan the handover, pay final wages and PTO correctly, and close access.

October 25, 2026 · 6 min read

When a valued employee says “I’m resigning,” most managers freeze for a moment. What you do in the next few days affects the handover, the team’s morale and whether the person leaves as an ally or a critic. This guide answers the questions managers ask most about handling a resignation and the notice period, in the order they come up.

How should a manager respond when an employee resigns?

Stay calm, thank the person and listen. You do not need to react, argue or decide anything in the first conversation. A good first response is short: “Thank you for telling me. I’d like to understand your plans and make the transition smooth. Can you confirm your last day in writing?” Avoid guilt, surprise or questions about the new employer that sound like pressure. Then tell your own manager and HR the same day, and ask the employee to keep it private until you have agreed on how to tell the team.

Does a resignation have to be in writing?

In most workplaces, yes, or at least it should be. A short resignation letter or email removes doubt about the date, starts the notice period clearly and protects both sides if details are disputed later. It only needs the employee’s name, the date of the message, a clear statement that they are resigning and the proposed last working day. Acknowledge it in writing, repeat the last day and say what happens next. Some countries and contracts have specific form requirements, so check yours.

How long is the notice period for a resignation?

It depends. Notice periods vary widely by country, state, contract, role and length of service. Some employees only owe a few days, others a month or three, and some places set statutory minimums while others leave it entirely to the contract. Look at the employment agreement, handbook and local rules first, and count from the date the written notice was given. This article is general information, not legal advice. If anything is unclear, ask HR or an employment lawyer.

Can you shorten or waive the notice period?

Often by mutual agreement, yes. You may release someone early if the handover is done, or if you prefer a clean break. Put any change in writing, with the agreed last day, and check whether you owe pay in lieu of notice. Placing someone on paid “garden leave” is another option, but only if the contract and local law allow it.

Should you make a counteroffer?

Decide quickly and with a clear head. Counteroffers can work when the reason for leaving is fixable, such as pay or workload, and the employee is someone you want to keep. They often fail when the real reason is growth, management or culture, because money rarely fixes those. Before you offer anything, ask yourself whether you would have made this offer without the resignation and whether it is fair to colleagues in similar roles. If the employee has already accepted another job, a counteroffer is usually not worth pursuing.

How do you plan the knowledge transfer?

Treat the notice period as a project with a deadline.

  • List the work. Recurring tasks, open projects, deadlines, key contacts, logins and where files live.
  • Name successors. Assign each item to a person, even if temporary.
  • Ask for short documents. A one-page handover note per area beats a long manual nobody reads.
  • Schedule overlap. Let the successor shadow the departing employee for real tasks before the last day.
  • Introduce clients and partners. Do it in the first week, not the last.
  • Hold a final check. Review the list on the last full day.

What final pay is owed, including unused PTO?

Final pay usually includes wages through the last day, any approved overtime or commission already earned, expense reimbursements and, in many places, payment for accrued unused vacation or PTO. Whether PTO must be paid out depends on local law and your policy. Some regions require it, others leave it to the contract. Deadlines for the final paycheck differ too, and may be the last day, the next payday or a set number of days afterward. Work out the figures early, share them with the employee in a clear statement and have payroll confirm them. Remember benefits, such as when health coverage ends and what the employee needs to do about it.

How do you handle access and offboarding?

Agree a checklist and a timeline before the last day.

  • Return of equipment: laptop, phone, badge, keys and company cards.
  • Accounts: email, files, chat, shared passwords and customer systems, removed or reassigned on the last day (or earlier for sensitive roles).
  • Email and calendar: an auto-reply or forwarding to a successor, according to your policy.
  • Payroll and benefits: stop date, final pay, pension or retirement details and tax documents.
  • Confidential information: remind the person of any confidentiality or non-compete terms in the contract.

Be careful with sensitive access. For roles with financial, customer or security data, remove it at the right moment, not out of distrust but as standard practice for everyone.

Should you hold an exit interview?

Yes, if you can make it honest and low-pressure. Ask what worked, what did not and what would have made them stay. Let HR or another neutral person run it where possible, because people say more to someone who is not their direct manager. Look for patterns across several exits and act on them. Do not argue with what you hear.

What should you do next?

Write down the date, confirm it in writing, and plan the handover on day one. Then tell the team clearly, thank the employee publicly and use what you learn to reduce the next resignation. A smooth departure leaves the door open for referrals, and sometimes a returning colleague later on.

Frequently asked questions

Do I have to accept a resignation letter?

In most cases the employee can resign without your approval, but you should acknowledge it in writing and confirm the last day. Check your contract and local rules for the notice required.

Can I refuse to let an employee leave during the notice period?

Generally no. You can agree an earlier or later last day together, but you cannot force someone to stay. Requirements vary by country, so ask HR or a lawyer if unsure.

Do I have to pay out unused vacation or PTO when someone resigns?

It depends on local law and your policy. Some places require payout of accrued unused leave, others leave it to the contract. Check the rules and confirm figures with payroll.

When should I remove a resigning employee's system access?

Usually on the last day, or earlier for roles with sensitive financial, customer or security data. Plan it in advance and apply the same rule to everyone.

Is a counteroffer a good idea?

Sometimes, if the reason for leaving is fixable and you want to keep the person. If the real issue is growth or culture, money rarely changes the decision.
