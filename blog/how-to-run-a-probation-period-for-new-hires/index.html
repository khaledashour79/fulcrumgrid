How Do You Run a Probation Period for New Hires? A Guide

A practical guide to running a probation period for new hires: its purpose, typical length, goals, check-ins, extending or ending it, and what to document.

October 10, 2026 · 5 min read

A probation period is a defined stretch at the start of employment when you and the new hire test the fit. It lets you assess performance and conduct against clear expectations, and it lets the employee judge the role, the team, and the manager. It is not a licence to skip fair process: in many countries probation changes some employment rights, but not all of them.

What is a probation period for?

Done well, probation is a structured onboarding period with a decision point at the end, not a waiting game. It serves four purposes:

  • Assess performance: can the person do the job to the standard you need?
  • Check fit: do their working style and conduct suit the team and the company?
  • Provide support: training, feedback, and coaching early enough to make a difference.
  • Reach a clear decision: confirm the hire, extend the period, or end the employment.

How long should probation last?

There is no universal answer. Some countries set a statutory maximum, often somewhere between one and six months and sometimes longer for senior roles. Others require the probation clause to be in writing, limit extensions, or treat probation very differently for notice and dismissal. In some places there is no formal concept of probation at all. Check the law that applies where the employee works, plus any collective agreement, before you write the contract. This article is general information, not legal advice.

Within what the law allows, match the length to the role. A simple role may be clear in a few weeks; a complex or senior one needs enough time to see real results.

Set goals before day one

People cannot meet expectations they have never heard. Before the start date, agree what success looks like:

  • Choose three to five outcomes: the results that matter most, not a long task list.
  • Make them observable: “handles customer tickets within the agreed response time” beats “is a good communicator”.
  • Phase them: a 30-60-90 day structure works well, moving from learning to contributing to owning.
  • Include conduct and values: reliability, collaboration, and how the person works with others.
  • Plan the support: name the buddy, the training, and the tools they will have.

Run regular check-ins

The first week

Walk through the goals, introduce the team, and make sure the person has access to everything they need. A slow start caused by missing equipment is not a performance problem.

Weekly or fortnightly one-to-ones

Keep short, regular conversations: what went well, what is blocked, where feedback is needed. Give both praise and concerns as they arise, so nothing in the final review is a surprise.

Mid-point and final reviews

Hold a formal review at the halfway point and again before the period ends. Compare progress to the agreed goals, ask the employee for their own view, and agree next steps. Schedule the final review early enough to leave time for a decision and any required notice.

Extending or ending probation

At the end you have three honest options.

  • Confirm the hire: tell the employee in writing and update their record.
  • Extend the period: only if the law and the contract allow it. Do it before the original period ends, explain why, set new goals and a new end date, and record the employee’s acknowledgement.
  • End the employment: follow the notice, process, and protections that apply in your country, which may be stricter than you expect. Be honest and respectful, and do not wait until the last day if the answer is already clear.

If a performance concern appears mid-way, raise it straight away with specific examples and a chance to improve. Springing a negative decision at the end is unfair and, in many places, legally risky.

Document everything

Good records protect both sides and make decisions easier to explain.

  • The terms: the signed contract and probation clause, with start date, length, and notice terms.
  • The goals: the written objectives given to the employee.
  • The check-ins: brief notes of each meeting, with dates, feedback given, and actions agreed.
  • The reviews: mid-point and final assessments, ideally acknowledged by the employee.
  • The decision: the outcome letter, any extension, and the reasons.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Vague expectations: goals that are never written down.
  • Silence: no feedback until the final week.
  • Missed deadlines: letting probation lapse by accident, which can mean the employee is confirmed by default.
  • Assuming fewer rights: probationary employees often keep protections against discrimination and unfair treatment.

Frequently asked questions

What is the purpose of a probation period?

It gives the employer and the new hire a defined time to test the fit. The employer assesses performance and conduct against agreed goals, and the employee assesses the role and the team. It ends with a clear decision to confirm, extend, or end the employment.

How long should a probation period be?

It depends on the country and the role. Many laws set a maximum, often between one and six months, and some set none or restrict extensions. Check local employment law and any collective agreement before choosing a length.

Can I extend a probation period?

Sometimes. It depends on local law and the contract, and some jurisdictions limit or forbid extensions. If it is allowed, extend before the original period ends, explain the reasons in writing, and set new goals and a new end date.

Can I end employment without reason during probation?

Do not assume so. Notice and dismissal rules during probation vary widely, and employees usually keep protection against discrimination. Take local legal advice before ending employment, and keep clear records of the reasons.

What should I document during probation?

Keep the signed contract and probation terms, the written goals, notes from each check-in, the mid-point and final reviews, and the final decision with its reasons.
