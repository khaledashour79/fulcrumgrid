What Is a Personal Guarantee and Should You Ask Customers for One?

A personal guarantee makes a business owner personally liable if their company fails to pay you. Learn when to ask for one, what it covers, and its limits.

November 13, 2026 · 6 min read

You have a new business customer who wants 30-day terms on a large first order. Their company is two years old, has thin credit history, and is structured as a limited company. If it fails, the debt dies with it. A personal guarantee is one of the oldest tools suppliers use to close that gap. This guide explains what it is, when it is reasonable to ask for one, and how to do it without scaring good customers away.

What is a personal guarantee?

A personal guarantee is a written promise by an individual, usually the owner or a director of the customer company, to pay the company's debt to you if the company does not. It turns a debt that is normally limited to the company's assets into one the guarantor can be asked to cover personally.

In practice, the guarantee usually sits inside your credit application or as a short separate document signed alongside it. It names the guarantor, the company being guaranteed, and the scope of the debts covered.

What does a personal guarantee usually cover?

Scope varies, so read and write it carefully. Common elements include:

  • Which debts: all present and future amounts owed, or only a specific contract or order.
  • A cap: an unlimited guarantee, or one limited to a set amount, often linked to the credit limit you grant.
  • Costs: whether late fees, interest and reasonable collection costs are included.
  • Duration: whether it continues until withdrawn in writing, or ends on a fixed date.
  • Demand process: whether you must first pursue the company, or can go straight to the guarantor after a missed payment.

Rules on enforceability differ by country, so have a lawyer review your template once. After that, you can reuse it consistently.

When should you ask for one?

A personal guarantee is not something to demand from every customer. It works best as a targeted control for higher-risk accounts. Reasonable triggers include:

  • A young company with little or no trade credit history.
  • A requested credit limit that is large relative to the customer's size or your own cash position.
  • Weak or mixed credit reports, or references that pay late.
  • A previous account that was closed with unpaid balances.
  • A request to raise an existing limit well above the customer's usual order size.

For established customers with a solid payment record, asking for a guarantee can feel like an insult. A clear credit policy that states when guarantees are required helps you apply the rule fairly and explain it without sounding personal.

How do you ask without losing the deal?

Most owners of healthy small companies understand the request if you frame it as standard policy rather than suspicion. A few habits help:

  • Tie it to the limit: "For credit lines above this amount, our policy is a personal guarantee or a deposit."
  • Offer alternatives: a deposit, a shorter term, a lower starting limit, or payment upfront for the first orders.
  • Make it reviewable: agree to revisit the guarantee after six or twelve months of on-time payments.
  • Keep it proportionate: a capped guarantee matched to the credit limit is far easier to accept than an unlimited one.

What are the limits of a personal guarantee?

A guarantee is only as good as the guarantor's ability to pay and your willingness to enforce it. If the owner has few personal assets, the guarantee may recover little. Enforcing it can also mean legal costs and the end of the relationship. Treat it as a backstop, not a substitute for good collection practice.

It also does not replace monitoring. Most losses still come from accounts that drift slowly into arrears while nobody is watching. Clear terms, prompt reminders, and early follow-up on the first missed due date matter more than any document in the file.

Personal guarantee or deposit?

A deposit gives you cash now; a guarantee gives you a claim later. For one-off projects or customers you may never see again, a deposit is often simpler and more certain. For ongoing trade relationships where you want to offer terms, a capped guarantee lets you extend credit with less exposure while keeping the customer's cash free for their business.

Other options sit between the two. You can start with a lower credit limit and raise it as payments arrive on time, or, for larger export or project deals, ask the customer's bank for a letter of credit or a bank guarantee. These cost the customer more to arrange, but they move the risk to a bank rather than an individual. This article is general guidance, not legal advice.

Keeping guaranteed accounts under control

Record which accounts carry a guarantee, its cap, and its review date next to the credit limit. When a guaranteed account goes overdue, you want that information in front of you before the second reminder, not when you are already writing a demand letter. Frequently asked questions

Is a personal guarantee legally binding?

Generally yes, when it is in writing, signed by the guarantor and clearly describes the debts covered. Requirements vary by country, so have a lawyer review your template.

Who should sign the personal guarantee?

Usually an owner or director of the customer company who has the means to cover the debt. A guarantee from someone with no personal assets offers little protection.

Should a personal guarantee be capped?

A cap linked to the credit limit is easier for customers to accept and keeps the request proportionate. Unlimited guarantees often meet resistance from good customers.

Can a guarantor withdraw the guarantee?

Many templates allow written withdrawal for future debts, while amounts already owed stay covered. Check how your own document handles this and reduce the credit limit if a guarantee ends.
