How Do You Give Constructive Feedback to an Employee?

Learn how to give constructive feedback that changes behaviour: when to give it, the SBI framework with an example, handling defensiveness and following up.

November 19, 2026 · 5 min read

Most managers know they should give more feedback, and most put it off. The conversation feels awkward, the moment passes, and a small issue quietly becomes a pattern. With a simple structure and a little preparation, it becomes a normal part of working together. This guide explains how to do it well in a small business, where you often manage people you also work beside every day.

What is constructive feedback, and how is it different from criticism?

Constructive feedback is specific information about something an employee did, shared so they can repeat it or improve it. Criticism judges the person; constructive feedback describes the behaviour. “You’re careless” is criticism. “The invoice you sent on Tuesday had the wrong VAT rate, and the client queried it” is feedback. The first makes people defensive. The second gives them something they can act on.

Constructive feedback can be positive or corrective, and both matter. Telling someone precisely what worked is as useful as telling them what did not, because it shows them what to keep doing.

When should you give it?

  • Soon. Within a day or two, while the details are fresh for both of you. Feedback saved for the annual review is history, not help.
  • In private. Corrective feedback belongs in a one-to-one setting, never in front of colleagues or customers. Praise can be public if the person is comfortable with it.
  • When you are calm. If you are still annoyed, wait a few hours. The aim is improvement, not letting off steam.
  • Regularly. Small, frequent comments feel normal. Rare, big ones feel like an event.

What is the SBI framework?

Situation–Behaviour–Impact (SBI) is a simple way to keep feedback factual. You describe:

  • Situation: when and where it happened.
  • Behaviour: what the person actually did or said, as a camera would record it, not your interpretation of it.
  • Impact: the effect it had on you, the team, a customer or the work.

A worked example

Imagine a team member, Sam, who interrupted a client twice during a project call. Instead of “You need to be more professional”, you might say:

“In this morning’s call with the Patel account (situation), you cut in twice while their operations manager was explaining the delivery problem (behaviour). She stopped going into detail, and I’m not sure we now understand what went wrong (impact).”

Then stop and ask: “How did the call look from your side?” That question turns a statement into a conversation. The same structure works for praise: “In Friday’s stock count, you set up the checklist before anyone arrived, and we finished an hour early.”

How do you open the conversation?

Be direct and brief. Ask whether now is a good time, then name the topic: “Have you got ten minutes? I’d like to talk about the client call this morning.” Avoid long warm-ups and vague openers such as “We need to talk”, which create anxiety without giving any information.

Why does the “feedback sandwich” often backfire?

The sandwich wraps criticism between two compliments. It feels kinder, but people quickly learn the pattern. Praise starts to sound like a warning, and the real message gets lost in the middle. Keep praise and corrective feedback separate, and give both often. A healthy rhythm is to notice good work more often than problems, so corrective comments land in a relationship that already feels fair.

How do you listen and agree next steps?

Once you have shared your observation, listen. There may be context you do not know about: unclear instructions, too much work, something happening outside work. Ask open questions, then decide together what happens next. Good next steps are concrete: “On the next client call, let’s agree you’ll hold your questions until they’ve finished and note them in the chat.” Finish by confirming what you both agreed and when you will check in.

What if the employee becomes defensive?

Defensiveness is a normal reaction, not a sign that the conversation has failed. Stay calm and do not argue over every detail. Acknowledge the feeling (“I can see this is frustrating”), return to the specific behaviour and its impact, and ask for their view. If emotions run high, it is fine to pause and pick it up the next day. What you should not do is drop the point altogether, or the behaviour will continue.

How do you give feedback remotely?

For anything corrective, use video rather than chat or email, because tone is easy to misread in writing. Book a short call, keep your camera on and give the person time to respond. Afterwards, send a brief written summary of what you agreed. Quick positive feedback, on the other hand, works well in a message.

How do you follow up and document it?

Check in when you said you would, and acknowledge improvement when you see it; that matters as much as the original conversation. Keep short, factual notes: the date, what you observed and what you agreed. Notes help you spot patterns and stay fair over time. If you already use FulcrumGrid HR Suite, you can keep them with your one-to-one records in each employee’s history rather than in scattered files.

Everyday feedback is informal. If the same issue continues after clear, repeated feedback and support, or if it involves misconduct, it is time to move to a formal process, such as a performance improvement plan or, for conduct issues, a fair disciplinary procedure. Your notes will show that the employee was told clearly and given a fair chance to improve.

Start this week: pick one recent example, positive or corrective, and share it using SBI.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good example of constructive feedback?

“In yesterday’s team meeting, you summarised the open customer issues in two minutes, and everyone left knowing their priorities.” It names the situation, the behaviour and the impact, so the person knows exactly what to repeat.

How often should managers give feedback?

Little and often. Aim to share something specific at least every week or two, ideally soon after the event, so feedback feels like a normal part of work rather than a rare, serious event.

Should constructive feedback be given in writing?

Give corrective feedback in person or on video first, then follow up with a short written summary of what you agreed. Written-only feedback is easy to misread and can feel colder than intended.

When does feedback become a formal performance process?

When the same issue continues after clear, repeated feedback and reasonable support, or when misconduct is involved. At that point, move to a documented process such as a performance improvement plan.
