How Should You Respond When a Customer Asks for Longer Payment Terms?

A customer wants net 60 or net 90 instead of net 30. Learn what longer terms really cost, how to evaluate the request and which counteroffers protect your cash.

November 17, 2026 · 6 min read

Sooner or later a good customer will say it: “Can we move to net 60?” or even “Our policy is net 90 for all suppliers.” It is tempting to agree on the spot to keep the relationship warm, or to refuse out of reflex. Neither is a decision. This Q&A walks through what longer terms actually cost, how to judge the request and how to answer it in a way that protects both your cash and the account.

Why do customers ask for longer payment terms?

Most requests are not a warning sign. Common reasons include:

  • Their own cash cycle. They get paid slowly by their customers and want to match what goes out with what comes in.
  • A company-wide policy. Larger buyers often standardise supplier terms at 60 or 90 days, and procurement simply applies the rule.
  • Growth or a bigger order. They are buying more from you and need breathing room to fund it.
  • Negotiating leverage. Longer terms are a discount that does not appear on the price list, and some buyers ask every supplier.
  • Financial stress. Occasionally the request is the first sign a customer is struggling, which is why it deserves a closer look.

What does extending terms really cost you?

Extending terms means you lend your customer money for free. Every extra day is a day your cash sits in their bank account instead of yours, and you either borrow to cover the gap or give up using that cash elsewhere.

A worked example: a customer buys $50,000 a month from you on net 30. Moving them to net 60 means roughly one extra month of sales is outstanding at any time, so your receivables grow by about $50,000. If your credit line costs 8% a year, carrying that balance costs around $4,000 a year. On $600,000 of annual sales that is under 1% of revenue, but at a 20% gross margin it is about 3% of the profit you make on that customer. Move to net 90 and the cost doubles to roughly $8,000, before counting the higher risk of a bad debt the longer an invoice stays unpaid.

How do you evaluate the request?

Before you answer, look at four things:

  • Payment history. Do they pay on time today? A customer who already pays net 30 invoices on day 50 is really asking you to make late payment official.
  • Credit risk. Check trade references, credit reports and any recent changes, such as new ownership, layoffs or slower payments elsewhere.
  • Margin. A high-margin account can absorb the financing cost; a thin-margin one may turn unprofitable.
  • Size of the account. Consider how much of your revenue depends on them and how much cash you can afford to have tied up with a single customer.

What can you offer instead of a straight yes?

Treat the request as the start of a negotiation. Useful counteroffers include:

  • A partial extension. Offer net 45 instead of net 60, or net 60 only on larger orders.
  • An early-payment discount. Grant net 60 but add 2/10 net 60, so paying within ten days earns a 2% discount. Many customers take it.
  • A deposit. Ask for 20–30% upfront, with the balance on the longer terms.
  • Milestone billing. For projects, invoice at defined stages so no single large amount waits 90 days.
  • A price adjustment. Build the financing cost into the price: standard price on net 30, a small premium on net 60 or net 90.
  • A trial period. Agree to longer terms for three or six months, then review them against actual payment behaviour.

How do you say yes with conditions, or no politely?

Keep the tone positive and the reasoning simple. To say yes with conditions:

“We value working with you and are happy to move to net 60 from next month. To make that work on our side, the new terms apply to orders above $10,000, and we will review them together in six months.”

To say no while keeping the door open:

“Thank you for asking. Our pricing is based on net 30, so we cannot extend terms at the current price. We can offer net 45 with a 1% surcharge, or keep net 30 with a 2% discount for payment within ten days. Which would suit you better?”

A clear answer with alternatives rarely costs you a customer. Silence or a vague “we will see” usually ends with them paying late anyway.

How do you document and monitor the new terms?

Agreed terms only protect you if they are written down and followed:

  • Confirm in writing. Send an email or contract addendum with the new terms, the start date, any conditions and the review date.
  • Update every system. Change the customer record so new invoices show the correct due date automatically.
  • Watch actual payment days. Compare when they pay with the agreed terms. Net 60 should mean day 60, not day 80.
  • Review on schedule. At the review date, keep, adjust or withdraw the extension based on the record.

What is the bottom line?

A request for longer terms is a request for credit. Work out what it costs, check whether the customer has earned it, and answer with a clear yes, a conditional yes or a polite no with alternatives. Then put the agreement in writing and watch that it is honoured.

Frequently asked questions

Should I always agree when a big customer asks for net 60 or net 90?

No. Calculate the financing cost, check their payment history and credit risk, and consider counteroffers such as a partial extension, a deposit or an early-payment discount before agreeing.

How do I calculate the cost of extending payment terms?

Multiply the customer's average daily sales by the extra days to find the additional receivables, then multiply that by your annual cost of borrowing. For example, $50,000 a month moved from net 30 to net 60 at 8% costs about $4,000 a year.

Can I charge more for longer payment terms?

Yes. Many suppliers quote a standard price on net 30 and a small premium on longer terms, or offer a discount for early payment. Explain it simply as the cost of financing the extra time.

What if a customer on extended terms starts paying late?

Raise it promptly, refer to the written agreement and, if late payment continues, move them back to the original terms or require a deposit on new orders.
