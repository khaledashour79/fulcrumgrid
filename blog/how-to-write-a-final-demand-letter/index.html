How Do You Write a Final Demand Letter Before Collections?

A final demand letter is your last written notice before an account goes to collections. Learn what to include, what tone to use, and how to keep a record.

October 13, 2026 · 6 min read

Reminders have gone out, a phone call or two went unanswered, and the invoice is still unpaid. Before you hand the account to a collection agency or start formal steps, most businesses send one last written notice. This guide explains how to write a final demand letter that is clear, professional and easy to prove later. It is general information, not legal advice, so check the rules that apply where you and your customer operate.

What is a final demand letter?

A final demand letter is a written notice that tells a customer exactly what they owe, sets a firm deadline to pay, and states what you will do next if they do not. It is the last step of a normal reminder sequence, not the first. Its job is to remove any doubt: the customer knows the amount, the date and the consequence.

When should you send one?

Send it after your regular reminders and at least one personal attempt to reach the customer have failed. Confirm first that the invoice was delivered, that the amount is correct, and that no dispute, credit note or partial payment is open. Sending a final demand for an invoice the customer has already queried can damage the relationship and weaken your position.

What should a final demand letter include?

  • Your details and the customer’s. Legal names, addresses and the account or customer number.
  • Invoice references. Invoice numbers, issue dates, due dates and the original amounts.
  • The exact balance. Total owed today, with any late fees or interest shown as a separate line and only if your agreement or local rules allow them.
  • A clear deadline. A specific calendar date, commonly 7 to 14 days out, not “soon” or “promptly”.
  • How to pay. Bank details, payment link or the name of the person to call.
  • Previous contact. A short list of dates when you sent reminders or spoke to them.
  • The next step. What happens after the deadline, stated plainly.
  • A way to respond. An invitation to contact you if there is a dispute or a problem paying.

How do you get the tone right?

Firm and factual works better than angry. State what is owed, what you have done so far and what comes next. Avoid insults, exaggeration and threats you cannot or will not carry out. Never threaten something you are not entitled to do, because that can create problems of its own. Keep an open door: many customers pay once they see a clear date and a human contact.

How do you write it, step by step?

1. Open with the facts

Begin with a sentence such as: “Our records show that invoice 1042, dated 5 June, for 3,450.00 remains unpaid.” Attach a copy of the invoice and a statement of account.

2. Summarize your earlier attempts

List the reminders and calls with dates. This shows reasonable effort and gives the reader no room to say they were never told.

3. Set the deadline and the consequence

For example: “Please pay the full balance by 27 October. If we have not received payment or heard from you by then, we intend to refer the account to a collection agency.” Only name steps you genuinely plan to take.

4. Offer a route to resolve it

Give a phone number or email and, if your policy allows, mention that you are willing to discuss a payment plan. People in genuine difficulty respond to this far better than to silence.

5. Keep it to one page

A short letter is read. Put the amount and the deadline in the first paragraph so they cannot be missed.

How should you send it and keep proof?

Use at least two channels, for example email plus a tracked postal delivery, and keep copies of everything. Record the date sent, the address used and any delivery confirmation. Some regions require specific wording, delivery methods or waiting periods before collection can begin, so check your local rules or ask a qualified professional before relying on a template.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Vague deadlines. Without a date, there is nothing to enforce.
  • Wrong amounts. A small error gives the customer a reason to ignore the whole letter.
  • Skipping earlier steps. Jumping straight to a demand feels aggressive and leaves a thin paper trail.
  • Empty threats. If you do not follow through, the next letter loses its force.
  • No follow-up. When the deadline passes, act on what you wrote or explain why you have extended it.

Frequently asked questions

Is a final demand letter legally required before collections?

It depends on where you operate and on your agreement with the customer. Some places expect a written notice first, others do not. Check your local rules or ask a qualified professional. Even when it is not required, it is good practice.

How long should the payment deadline be?

Many businesses allow 7 to 14 days from the date the letter is delivered. Choose a date that is realistic for the customer yet firm enough to be taken seriously, and write it as a calendar date.

Can I add late fees or interest to the letter?

Only if your contract or terms allow them and local rules permit. Show them on a separate line so the original invoice amount stays clear.

What if the customer disputes the invoice after the letter?

Pause and look into it. Review the invoice, delivery records and any correspondence, and answer in writing. Pushing ahead on a disputed amount can harm the relationship and your position.
