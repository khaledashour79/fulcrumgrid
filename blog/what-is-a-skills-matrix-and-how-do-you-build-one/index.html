What Is a Skills Matrix and How Do You Build One for Your Team?

A skills matrix maps who can do what on your team. Learn how to pick skills, rate them 0 to 4, find gaps, plan cross-training and keep the matrix current.

November 3, 2026 · 6 min read

When one person goes on leave and a whole process stalls, the problem is rarely effort. It is visibility: nobody knew who else could do the work. A skills matrix makes that visible. This guide explains what a skills matrix is, how to build one in an afternoon, and how to use it for cross-training, hiring and succession planning without turning it into a bureaucratic exercise.

What is a skills matrix?

A skills matrix (also called a competency matrix) is a simple grid. People are listed down one side, skills across the top, and each cell holds a rating of how well that person can perform that skill. At a glance you can see who is strong where, which skills only one person has, and which skills nobody has.

It is a planning tool, not a performance review. Its job is to answer practical questions: who can cover this role, who could learn this next, and where are we exposed?

Step 1: Choose the skills that matter

The most common mistake is listing everything. A matrix with 80 columns is never filled in and never read. Aim for 10 to 25 skills for one team, and pick them carefully:

  • Start from the work. Look at your processes, systems and customer commitments and ask what must be done well for them to run.
  • Mix technical and practical skills. For example a specific tool, a regulatory task, a customer-facing skill or a leadership duty such as running a shift.
  • Be specific. “Payroll month-end close” is useful. “Finance knowledge” is not.
  • Group them. Three or four headings, such as core, technical, compliance and leadership, keep the grid readable.

Step 2: Define a 0 to 4 rating scale

A scale only works if everyone reads it the same way, so describe each level in terms of observable behaviour rather than confidence:

  • 0 – No knowledge. Has not been trained and has not done this.
  • 1 – Learning. Understands the basics and can do it with close supervision.
  • 2 – Competent. Can do routine work alone, and asks for help with exceptions.
  • 3 – Proficient. Handles exceptions independently and works to a consistent standard.
  • 4 – Expert. Could teach it to others, improve the process and decide edge cases.

Five levels are enough. Fewer hide real differences; more invite arguments over the difference between a 6 and a 7.

Step 3: Rate people honestly

Self-rating alone is unreliable. Confident people tend to rate themselves high and careful people low, so the grid ends up measuring personality. A better process is to have each person rate themselves, have their manager rate them separately, and then discuss every gap of more than one point. Ask for an example (“when did you last do this without help?”), and where possible use evidence such as completed training, certifications or work samples.

Step 4: Run the gap analysis

Once the grid is filled in, set a target level for each skill, for example “at least two people at level 3 or above” for critical skills. Then look for three patterns:

  • Single points of failure. Skills where only one person is at level 3 or higher. These are your biggest risk.
  • Coverage gaps. Skills where nobody reaches the target, which call for training or hiring.
  • Surplus. Skills with many experts, where people could be moved toward gaps or developed into mentors.

Rank the gaps by business impact, not by how easy they are to fix.

Using the matrix for cross-training

Cross-training is the direct payoff. For each single point of failure, name a backup who is currently at level 1 or 2 and agree a path to level 3: shadowing, a supervised task each week, then a solo task with review. Give the expert time to teach, because training that is squeezed in between other work rarely sticks. Review progress monthly and update the ratings when someone moves up.

Using it for hiring and succession

For hiring, the matrix turns “we need another person” into a precise brief: which skills, at which level, and how many. It also tells you whether to hire or to train, since a gap one level away is usually faster to close internally. For succession, compare key roles with the people who are two to three skills away from them. That shows who is ready now, who is ready in a year and where you have no successor at all.

Common pitfalls

  • Self-rating bias. Fix it with manager input and examples, as described above.
  • Using it as a weapon. If ratings feed bonuses or layoffs, people will inflate them. State clearly that the matrix is for development and planning.
  • Too many skills. Cut anything nobody would miss.
  • Vague levels. If two managers would score the same person differently, rewrite the definitions.
  • One-off exercise. A matrix built once and never touched is out of date within months.

Keeping it current

Treat the matrix like any other living document. Review it every quarter, and update it when someone finishes training, changes role, joins or leaves. Give one person ownership, add new skills when you adopt a new system or service, and retire skills that are no longer needed. A spreadsheet is a perfectly good place to start; what matters is that the matrix is used in real decisions about rosters, training budgets and hiring.

Start small this week: pick one team, choose 12 skills, agree the scale and fill in the first draft together. The first conversation about the gaps is usually worth more than the grid itself.

Frequently asked questions

How many skills should a skills matrix include?

For a single team, 10 to 25 skills is a practical range. Fewer than that and the grid says little; more than that and it becomes too heavy to keep updated. Focus on skills that are critical to delivering the work.

Why use a 0 to 4 scale instead of 1 to 10?

A short scale with clear definitions is easier to rate consistently. Five levels are enough to show who can work alone, who can handle exceptions and who can teach, while a 10-point scale invites debates about tiny differences.

How do you avoid self-rating bias?

Combine self-assessment with a manager rating and discuss any difference of more than one point. Ask for concrete examples, and back ratings with evidence such as completed training, certifications or observed work.

How often should a skills matrix be updated?

Review it at least quarterly, and update it whenever someone completes training, changes role, joins or leaves. Assign a single owner so the review does not get forgotten.

Is a skills matrix the same as a performance review?

No. A skills matrix describes capability for planning cover, training and hiring. A performance review judges results over a period. Keeping the two separate encourages honest ratings.
