What If a Customer Pays Into the Wrong Bank Account?

A customer says they paid, but the money went to the wrong account. Learn whether the invoice is still owed, what to do first and how to stop payment fraud.

November 26, 2026 · 6 min read

“We paid last week.” You check your bank account and nothing has arrived. After some digging, the customer sends a remittance showing the money went to an account number you do not recognise. It is one of the most stressful conversations in accounts receivable, because both sides believe they have done their part and real money has gone somewhere it should not.

The cause usually falls into one of two groups, and the first job is to work out which one you are dealing with.

Why do payments go to the wrong account?

  • A simple error. The customer used an old account you closed, mistyped a digit, picked the wrong supplier from their payee list or paid another of your invoices twice instead.
  • Bank-detail-change fraud. A criminal, often after compromising your email or the customer’s, sends a convincing message saying your bank details have changed. The customer updates their records and pays the fraudster. This is known as business email compromise, or invoice redirection fraud, and it is common, well organised and fast.

If the customer mentions an email, letter or call telling them your details had changed, and you sent no such thing, treat it as fraud from that moment.

Is the invoice still owed?

In most jurisdictions, generally yes. A debt is normally discharged only when the creditor, or someone authorised to receive payment on its behalf, actually receives the money. Paying a third party, whether by mistake or because a fraudster impersonated you, usually does not settle the invoice. There are exceptions: if the payment went to an account you had genuinely told the customer to use, or if your own systems were compromised and contributed to the loss, the position can be less clear and liability may be shared. This is not legal advice; where a large sum is involved, speak to a lawyer early.

Knowing the debt probably still stands should not change your tone. The customer has usually lost money too, and they will remember how you treated them.

What should you do immediately?

Speed matters more than anything else. Funds sent to a fraudulent account are often moved on within hours.

  • Verify the facts. Confirm the invoice number, amount and date, and check your accounts again, including any old or secondary ones.
  • Get proof of payment. Ask for the remittance advice or bank confirmation showing the date, amount, receiving account and payment reference.
  • Ask the customer to call their bank now. Only the paying party can ask its bank to trace and recall the payment. They should phone, not email, and say clearly whether it was a mistake or suspected fraud.
  • Understand the recall process. The sending bank contacts the receiving bank and asks it to return or freeze the funds. For a simple error to a legitimate account, the money is often returned within days to a few weeks, usually with the account holder’s consent. For fraud, a recall only works if the funds are still there.
  • Report fraud. If fraud is suspected, both businesses should report it to the police or national fraud reporting authority and keep the reference number. Banks and insurers often ask for it.
  • Check your own systems. If the fake message appeared to come from your domain, have your mailboxes checked, change passwords, turn on multi-factor authentication and warn other customers.

How should you handle the conversation and the open invoice?

Be calm and factual. Acknowledge that the customer tried to pay and that this is frustrating for both of you. Then explain, politely, that the payment has not reached you and the invoice remains open while the recall is pursued.

  • Put collections on hold. Pause automated reminders and late fees for that invoice. A dunning email landing in the middle of a fraud investigation damages the relationship.
  • Record everything. Note the date reported, the proof of payment, the bank reference and every contact. In FulcrumGrid Collection you can mark the invoice as disputed, place a hold on reminders and keep the notes on the customer’s account, so anyone picking it up sees the full story.
  • Agree a timeline. Set a date to review progress, for example two weeks. If the funds come back, the customer forwards them. If not, agree how the invoice will be settled, which may include a payment plan.

How do you prevent it from happening?

  • Show your bank details on every invoice. Use one consistent invoice template so customers know exactly what your real details look like.
  • Never accept a change of bank details by email alone. Apply the same rule to your own suppliers and expect customers to apply it to you.
  • Use call-back verification. Any change must be confirmed by phone on a number already on file, never one taken from the message asking for the change.
  • Tell customers you will never change bank details by email. Put the statement on invoices and in onboarding emails, so a fraudulent request stands out immediately.
  • Offer a payment portal. When customers pay through a secure link or portal, they never have to type in an account number, which removes both typos and redirection.
  • Alert customers if you really do change banks. Announce it well in advance, through more than one channel, and confirm by phone.

A misdirected payment is rarely anyone’s fault alone. Move fast, keep the customer on side, document every step and make your bank details hard to fake, and the next attempt is far more likely to fail.

Frequently asked questions

Does the customer still owe the invoice if they paid the wrong account?

In most jurisdictions, generally yes, because a debt is usually settled only when the creditor receives payment. Exceptions can apply, so take legal advice for large amounts.

Can a payment to the wrong bank account be recovered?

Often, if the paying customer contacts their bank quickly and asks for a trace and recall. Simple errors are usually recoverable; fraud recoveries depend on the funds not having been moved.

Should I keep sending reminders while the payment is traced?

No. Put the invoice on hold, pause reminders and late fees, record the situation and agree a review date with the customer instead.

How can I stop customers being tricked by fake bank details?

Show your bank details on every invoice, tell customers you will never change them by email, require call-back verification on a known number and offer a secure payment portal.
